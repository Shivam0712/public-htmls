From Business Case to Design Specification: How a Data Center Owner Turns Intent Into a Buildable, Certifiable Facility (2025–2026)

The Desktop Research report for Topic 2, run 2026-09-23 from briefs/ and published as delivered: no editing, no lint. The chapter and appendix were distilled from it; where they and this report disagree, the chapter is the course's position.

TL;DR

  • The owner turns a business case into a build by writing an Owner's Project Requirements (OPR) — mission, capacity, density, availability, efficiency, budget, schedule and target certifications — which the engineer answers with a Basis of Design (BOD); ASHRAE Guideline 0-2019 and Standard 202 make the OPR the legal yardstick that commissioning later tests the finished building against, so a vague or late OPR is the single largest source of change orders and disputes.
  • The density decision (kW per rack) cascades through everything: it sets the power block, the cooling method, floor loading, room size, cost per MW and cost per rack. In 2025 the industry average is still single-digit-to-mid-teens kW/rack (Uptime's modal average ~8–9 kW; AFCOM ~16 kW), but AI pods now run 40–130+ kW, so planners design a distribution of densities, not an average — and the cooling method changes at roughly 20, 30–40 and 80+ kW thresholds that sources genuinely disagree about.
  • Availability is a design criterion, not a marketing label: the Uptime Institute Tier system (I–IV), TIA-942-C (Rated 1–4), BICSI 002-2024 and EN 50600 / ISO/IEC 22237 (Availability Classes 1–4) all encode redundancy topology; most colo and hyperscale targets "Tier III / concurrently maintainable" (≈99.982%, ~1.6 h/yr), Tier IV adds fault tolerance (≈99.995%, ~26 min/yr) at a 25–60% capex premium, and codes (NEC/NFPA 70 Article 645, NFPA 75/76, IBC/IFC, ASHRAE 90.4) plus audit frameworks (SOC 2, ISO 27001, PCI DSS, FedRAMP/NIST 800-53, LEED, ENERGY STAR) bind the rest.

1. Owner's Project Requirements and Basis of Design

1.1 What the OPR is and what it contains

The Owner's Project Requirements (OPR) is the owner's plain-language statement of what the facility must do and how success will be judged. ASHRAE Guideline 0-2019 — the document that governs the whole commissioning process — defines the OPR as the record of the owner's objectives and criteria "from which all design, construction, acceptance, and operation decisions are made," and defines the companion Basis of Design (BOD) as "a document that records the concepts, calculations, decisions, and product selections used to meet the OPR and to satisfy applicable regulatory requirements, standards, and guidelines." The OPR is normally written in pre-design, before any design begins, and is deliberately kept out of the contract documents themselves (ASHRAE's own headquarters OPR notes it "is not considered to be a 'Contract Document' to avoid the possibility of conflicting" language) — instead it is issued with the RFP and used as the benchmark for acceptance.

A realistic OPR table of contents for a colocation data center (synthesized from ASHRAE Guideline 0, the ASHRAE HQ OPR, LBNL's data-center OPR template work, and the CSU/Manitoba public templates) looks like this:

  1. Project mission & business objectives — what the facility is for (multi-tenant retail + wholesale colo), the market it serves, success criteria.
  2. Capacity & growth — day-one IT load, ultimate IT load, phasing trigger (leasing velocity), expansion horizon (15–20 yr).
  3. Availability / resilience — target tier or availability class, concurrent maintainability vs fault tolerance, maximum tolerable downtime, redundancy stance (N / N+1 / 2N named only — see Topic 1).
  4. Density profile — average and peak kW/rack, high-density zones, liquid-cooling readiness.
  5. Efficiency — design PUE target, WUE stance, energy-code posture, sustainability commitments.
  6. Environmental / design conditions — site climate design points, ASHRAE thermal envelope class to be met (requirement side only).
  7. Budget & schedule — capex envelope ($/MW target), delivery date, phasing dates.
  8. Certifications & compliance — Uptime Tier certification (design + constructed), SOC 2 / ISO 27001 / PCI / HIPAA / FedRAMP readiness, LEED / ENERGY STAR targets.
  9. Operations & maintenance requirements — staffing model, maintainability, monitoring/DCIM expectations.
  10. Constraints & owner standards — owner's reference designs, preferred vendors, local AHJ constraints.

1.2 What the BOD is and what it contains

The BOD is the engineer's technical answer to the OPR. A representative BOD table of contents:

  1. Design narrative & code list — codes and standards applied (NEC, IBC/IFC, NFPA 70/75/76, ASHRAE 90.4, local amendments), AHJ.
  2. Design conditions — outdoor design temperatures (dry-bulb/wet-bulb), indoor setpoints, seismic/wind criteria.
  3. Electrical topology — one-line diagram, utility service, UPS topology, generator sizing, redundancy configuration, ELC assumptions.
  4. Mechanical/cooling topology — cooling method(s) by zone, plant sizing, redundancy, MLC assumptions.
  5. Capacity & density basis — IT load, critical load, design density profile, load diversity/utilization assumptions.
  6. Space program — white space, gray space, support areas; floor loading; room layout basis.
  7. Sizing calculations & assumptions — the arithmetic linking OPR targets to equipment ratings.
  8. Sustainability/efficiency basis — PUE model, 90.4 MLC/ELC compliance path.
  9. Product selections — major equipment classes and rationale.

1.3 Who writes each, and when — how the documents evolve

The owner (or owner's representative / owner's engineer) writes the OPR; the design team (or design-build/EPC engineer of record) writes the BOD. Under ASHRAE Guideline 0, the OPR originates in pre-design, and a Design-Phase objective is explicitly "verifying the BOD document with the OPR document." The documents are living: the OPR is refined as owner objectives sharpen, and the BOD is drafted at schematic design and refined through design development into construction documents. Mapping the two common phase vocabularies:

  • AIA design phases: Programming/Concept → Schematic Design (SD) → Design Development (DD) → Construction Documents (CD).
  • ASHRAE Guideline 0 phases: Pre-Design → Design → Construction → Occupancy & Operations.

They align: the OPR is a pre-design/programming, owner-owned document; the BOD begins at SD and is refined through DD, with commissioning requirements folded into the CD set. Best practice (per USGBC LEED commissioning guidance and industry commissioning practice) is to revisit and formally update both documents at each phase gate, with change control so that decisions and unresolved items are documented — because the people who close out commissioning at the end are often not the people who set the requirements at the start.

1.4 How commissioning tests against them (requirements side only)

ASHRAE Guideline 0-2019 and ASHRAE/IES Standard 202 (the Commissioning Process standard Guideline 0 harmonizes with) make the OPR the acceptance yardstick: the entire purpose of the commissioning process is described as "verifying that the facility and its systems meet the Owner's Project Requirements (OPR)." Verification checklists and functional performance tests are written to confirm OPR compliance. (The commissioning process, levels and agent are Topic 11 — not covered here.) The practical implication for the requirements author: anything you want tested at handover must be a measurable statement in the OPR. "Highly available" is untestable; "concurrently maintainable per Uptime Tier III, ≤ 1.6 h/yr expected unplanned downtime, PUE ≤ 1.4 at design load" is testable.

1.5 Delivery-and-risk angle: who owns the gap between "meant" and "built"

This is where the OPR/BOD becomes a risk-allocation instrument. The core principle (AIA Contract Documents, How Construction Delivery Methods Allocate Risk, updated 2026): the specification type and the delivery model together decide who pays when reality diverges from intent.

  • Design-bid-build (DBB): the owner holds the design contract and therefore carries design risk. Per AIA, "if the design contains errors or omissions, the consequences typically flow through to the contractor in the form of change orders" — the owner pays. A vague or late OPR here produces an incomplete design, which produces change orders during construction.
  • Design-build (DB): one entity carries design and construction risk, so a vague OPR is less dangerous to the owner's budget — but only if scope was well defined at signing. AIA warns that "if the scope is not well defined when the design-build contract is signed, the owner may lose cost certainty," because the builder fills gaps with the cheapest compliant interpretation.
  • EPC turnkey / lump-sum: the owner provides a performance/output specification and the contractor guarantees the result for a fixed price; risk sits largely with the contractor, backed by performance security typically 5–15% of contract price and performance liquidated damages. In data center EPC, those guarantees are written around Uptime Tier (99.982% / 99.995%), PUE (commonly ≤ 1.4), MW capacity and redundancy, and enforced through a staged commissioning gate with final payment tied to integrated systems testing. This is the strongest protection for an owner who can write a precise performance spec — and the weakest for one who cannot.

Where change orders come from and their scale. The AIA's primary dataset — its "Market Standard Range" is "created from a large database of construction market data - 892,457 change orders," covering "18,229 completed building projects" with "an average duration of 8.11 to 32.88 months, depending upon the construction project value" (The Truth About Change Orders, 2023) — shows average change-order cost impact of roughly 4–5% of value, but the "Market Standard Range" (middle 80%) reaches up to ~15% cost increase, and large projects (>$50M) average 11.29 change orders each — most occurring in the final half of the project, when the owner has the fewest options. Independent academic work (Alexandria Engineering Journal, 2013) found change-order-driven cost overruns of 11–15% of original contract value on large building projects, with design changes and errors/omissions the dominant cause. Vague requirements are, in effect, deferred change orders.

How good owners prevent them. Three mechanisms recur: 1. Performance specifications with measurable acceptance criteria (Uptime Tier is itself an outcome spec — Uptime "does not prescribe any specific technology, schematic or other design criteria beyond those resulting outcomes"). 2. Reference designs / owner standards — repeatable, pre-engineered building blocks (the hyperscaler approach; also sold by engineering firms as standardized modules) issued under a master agreement with per-phase work authorizations, so cost stays predictable. 3. Locking irreversible decisions into the OPR before design begins — above all the availability tier and the density profile, because both are extremely expensive to change mid-design.

2. Density-First Capacity Planning: Choosing kW per Rack

2.1 How the number is chosen in 2025–26

Rack density is chosen from tenant mix and prospective customers, the IT roadmap, the expected AI/accelerated-compute share, and what colos are actually leasing. In 2025–26 there is a wide gap between the installed base and the leading edge:

  • Uptime Institute Global Data Center Survey 2025 (15th annual, surveyed April–May 2025, >800 operators): "Average server rack power densities continue to rise slowly, driven by greater adoption of racks in the 10 kW to 30 kW range. Few facilities exceed 30 kW, and extreme densities remain rare." Uptime's 2024 data put the modal average density at ~8 kW (skewed up by a handful of >50 kW sites); the 2025 report states "the average of the modal densities in our total sample reaches almost 9 kW, up slightly from" the prior year — and adds that "More than 80% of the operators responding to our survey say their facility has no racks" above the high-density thresholds.
  • AFCOM State of the Data Center 2025 (Executive Summary): "Rack density continues to rise, jumping from 7 kW/rack in 2021 to 16 kW/rack in the latest survey. 79% of respondents expect rack density to grow further due to AI and high-performance workloads."
  • Vendor / market view: AFCOM's 10th State of the Data Center report (2026), via Data Center Knowledge, found "average rack density reached 27 kW per rack this year, up from 16 kW last year – the largest year-over-year increase recorded in the report's decade-long history," which AFCOM/Informa executive chair Bill Kleyman called a "step-function change"; Schneider Electric's Steven Carlini separately noted fully-loaded NVIDIA GPU racks "require 132 kW" with the next generation requiring "240 kW per rack," and NVIDIA's Vera Rubin platform is specified up to 246 kW.
  • Broker view (CBRE H1/H2 2025): North American vacancy hit a record-low 1.6%, 74.3% of under-construction capacity was pre-leased, and AI-optimized (liquid-cooled, high-density) space commands rent premiums; per CBRE's H2 2025 report "the average monthly asking rate for a 250-to-500-kilowatt (kW) requirement rose by 6.5% year-over-year to $195.94 per kW/month, the fourth consecutive annual increase."

Reconciling the disagreement: Uptime measures the whole installed base (hence single-digit-to-teens); AFCOM and vendors weight newer and AI deployments (hence teens-to-tens). I trust Uptime's ~9 kW modal figure for "what the average existing hall runs today" and the AFCOM/vendor 16–27 kW figures for "what new colo is being designed to," because they are measuring different populations, not contradicting each other. For a new 2026 colo build, planning to a mixed profile anchored well above the installed-base average is the defensible choice.

2.2 The distribution, not the average

An "average 15 kW/rack" hall can contain 100 kW AI pods and 5 kW storage rows simultaneously; the average hides the design problem. Planners therefore describe a density profile in bands / zones / pods: e.g., a low-density band (≤10 kW, air-cooled), a mainstream band (10–20 kW), a high-density band (20–50 kW, containment + rear-door), and an AI band (50–150 kW, direct-to-chip liquid). Schneider Electric's capacity-planning method (White Papers 150 and the space/power-density papers) builds the facility as rooms → pods → cabinets, each pod specified for an average and a peak per-cabinet power, with the rule that no cabinet may exceed the pod's peak rating because the power distribution isn't rated for it. A colo reserves high-density zones by pre-provisioning power busway, floor loading and liquid-cooling manifolds in specific pods, while leaving the rest of the hall at lower density — so the high-density capability doesn't strand the general floor.

2.3 The cascade: how kW/rack sizes everything

Density is the master variable. Rules of thumb practitioners use:

  • Racks per MW: at 10 kW/rack, ~100 racks/MW; at 20 kW, ~50; at 100 kW, ~10. (IT-load basis.)
  • Area per rack / per MW: conventional air-cooled layouts run ~15–25 ft² per rack including aisles (20 ft² is a sensible default), so ~50 racks per 1,000 ft² of white space; dense/liquid-cooled halls pack tighter, ~12–15 ft² per rack. At 250 W/ft² and 20 ft²/rack, each rack supports ~5 kW; a 10,000 ft² hall at 250 W/ft² supports ~2.5 MW IT.
  • Cost per kW as density rises, then falls: raising density concentrates more IT load into less building, so $/kW of white space first falls (you amortize shell/land over more compute) — then at some point rises again because liquid cooling, higher floor loading, and denser power distribution add cost. Liquid-cooling construction premium is put at ~7–10% (Turner & Townsend, via Global Data Center Hub).

2.5 Oversizing vs phasing vs modular growth; stranded capacity; utilization

Stranded capacity is capacity you paid for but cannot use because a different resource ran out first — e.g., power available but cooling maxed, or floor space free but the PDU/busway is full. Schneider Electric's capacity-management work (White Paper 150, Neil Rasmussen) identifies this as endemic: operators "typically do not have the information they need," so facilities are "grossly over-designed or under-utilized" with large safety margins. Load-balancing failures across PDUs strand power at the row level.

Design vs nameplate vs actual load matters enormously. Facilities are sized for worst-case cooling, design-case PUE, and contingency + growth headroom, so average operational draw is well below nameplate. Recent modeling (LBNL-parameter power-flow work, 2026 arXiv) finds applying server-level utilization to facility capacity overstates facility load by ~9–18 percentage points versus a facility-level estimate that accounts for design contingency and headroom; illustrative facility-load coefficients used in that work cluster around 0.66. The practical planning lesson: design load ≠ nameplate ≠ actual, and prudent owners plan the power block against a diversified design load, not the sum of nameplate ratings.

Oversizing vs phasing vs modular: building the ultimate plant on day one wastes capital and strands capacity until load arrives; phasing (shell for all, MEP for some) and modular MEP blocks/prefab skids defer capex to match leasing, at a modest premium. A common reserve rule is to keep ~15–20% of white/gray space for future reconfiguration.

2.6 How hyperscalers and colos plan differently

Hyperscalers plan to a known, standardized workload; colos plan for speculative, multi-tenant flexibility. Hyperscalers (Meta, Google, Microsoft, AWS) use standardized owner designs and open reference designs — Meta open-sourced its Prineville design and founded the Open Compute Project (OCP) in 2011; OCP's Open Systems for AI initiative (with NVIDIA's GB200-NVL72 and Meta's Catalina rack contributions) now standardizes AI rack power and liquid-cooling interfaces. Notably, Meta historically targeted only ~5.5 kW/rack and scaled by adding racks, not densifying — the opposite of the AI-era trend. Colos, by contrast, cannot know their tenants' hardware, so they design a flexible density profile and market reserved high-density zones. Vendors are responding with "standardized IT Pods as reference designs or even prefabricated modules" (Schneider's Steven Carlini).

3. Load Growth, Phasing and Flexibility Over the Facility's Life

3.1 Phased build-out

A large campus is almost never built to ultimate capacity on day one. The standard pattern: build the shell (and often site power/civil) for the ultimate campus, but install MEP for only the first phase(s). Phases are sized against leasing velocity (how fast tenants sign) and interconnection delivery (when utility power actually arrives — increasingly the binding constraint; CBRE and JLL both flag power availability and delivery timelines as the top 2025 site/leasing constraint, with North American vacancy at a record 1.6%). Day-one vs ultimate MW is an explicit OPR decision: it fixes how much transformer, generator, UPS and chiller plant to buy now versus later.

3.2 Designing for density uplift over 15–20 years

Some things are cheap to future-proof, some are not: - Cheap (do it on day one): structural floor-loading headroom, taller floor-to-floor heights, pipe stubs and valved taps for future liquid cooling, spare conduit/busway pathways, space reservations for future plant, and electrical room space. - Expensive (defer / modularize): transformers, switchgear, UPS, chillers and generators — the plant itself, which is capital-intensive and technology-dependent.

The design principle: spend a little on passive future-proofing (space, structure, stubs) to avoid stranded plant later.

3.3 "AI-ready" as a specification — and how much is marketing

In 2025–26 "AI-ready" in an OPR or broker sheet generally means a concrete bundle: liquid-cooling readiness (chilled-water loops, CDU space, secondary-loop provisions), higher per-rack power (50–120+ kW busway), higher floor loading (fully populated AI racks can weigh ~1,500 lb; WWT cites deep racks >1,200 mm), and higher supply-water/air temperatures for efficiency. WWT's readiness glossary states current requirements around 50 kW/rack rising to 80–120 kW. Some of the label is genuine engineering; some is marketing — the honest test is whether the spec names measurable numbers (kW/rack, kg/m² or lb/ft², supply temperatures, GPM per rack) or just says "AI-ready." Flexibility mechanisms (modular MEP blocks, prefab skids, convertible white space) carry a premium but preserve optionality; liquid-cooling construction premium runs ~7–10%.

Caveat / fast-moving: density forecasts are escalating so fast that "AI-ready" is a moving target — Deloitte (via DC&T) projects next-gen AI racks reaching ~370 kW in 2026; NVIDIA Vera Rubin is cited up to 246 kW. Treat all forward density numbers as projections, not facts.

4. Availability Frameworks as Design Criteria

4.1 Uptime Institute Tier Standard: Topology (Tier I–IV)

The Uptime Tier Standard: Topology classifies site infrastructure by redundancy topology, not by a statistic — it is "an objective basis for comparing the functionality, capacity, and expected availability… of a particular site infrastructure design topology." The four levels are progressive (each includes the ones below): - Tier I — Basic Capacity: single path, no redundancy. - Tier II — Redundant Capacity: redundant components (N+1), still single path. - Tier III — Concurrently Maintainable: redundant components and redundant distribution paths, so any component can be maintained without shutting down IT. No "hot work" risk to staff. - Tier IV — Fault Tolerant: multiple independent, physically isolated, simultaneously active paths; any single failure is survived automatically.

Certification proceeds in stages: Tier Certification of Design Documents (TCDD) (Uptime reviews 100% of design docs), Tier Certification of Constructed Facility (TCCF) (on-site verification of the as-built), and Operational Sustainability (TCOS) (staffing/maintenance/management — a separate track). Uptime is the sole licensed source of Tier Certification, which is the commercial basis of the business.

Criticisms are real and worth knowing: cost and time of certification; rigidity (you meet all requirements of a tier or drop to the one below — "you can't add a generator to get to Tier III"); and rampant "Tier III-like" self-declaration — Uptime itself notes some companies achieved a design certification (TCDD) then walked away, "willfully misrepresenting the Tier Certification, using a design foil to market a site that was not physically tested." Many "Tier III" plaques are design-only or expired. Buyers should confirm the specific hall/suite and whether the constructed facility (not just the design) was certified.

4.2 The other frameworks

  • TIA-942-C (2024) — "Rated 1–4." A telecom/infrastructure standard (updated to Rev C, May 2024 by TIA TR-42.1) covering cabling, architectural, electrical, mechanical; it has a certification/audit program via third parties. Dominant in North America; broader in scope than Uptime (covers cabling) but relies on some "informative only" elements for power/cooling availability assessment.
  • BICSI 002-2024 — a ~550-page design and implementation best-practices standard defining availability Classes F0–F4; used as the "best-practice ceiling" reference alongside TIA-942's "minimum floor." International use.
  • EN 50600 / ISO/IEC 22237 — the European/international family, using Availability Classes 1–4 (Class 1 basic → Class 2 redundant components → Class 3 concurrently maintainable → Class 4 fault tolerant) plus separate Protection Classes 1–4 for physical security and a granularity level for energy KPIs. It is the most comprehensive because it sets availability requirements separately for power, cooling and cabling. Certified by bodies like TÜV, EPI, SGS; design certification (DCDV) valid ~1 year, facility certification (DCCC) valid 3 years with annual surveillance audits. Used in Europe and internationally.

4.3 How owners actually choose

The decision reduces to concurrent maintainability vs fault tolerance and what downtime costs. Indicative numbers (multiple 2025–26 sources; treat as indicative — they vary by source):

  • Tier III ≈ 99.982% availability ≈ 1.6 h/yr unplanned downtime; Tier IV ≈ 99.995% ≈ 26.3 min/yr. (Tier I ≈ 99.671% / ~28.8 h; Tier II ≈ 99.741% / ~22 h.) These follow directly from the annual-downtime formula ((1 − availability) × 8,760 h): 99.995% → 0.438 h ≈ 26.3 min; 99.982% → 1.58 h ≈ 95 min. Uptime has de-emphasized these percentages in its own documentation, stressing that real-world uptime depends heavily on operations, not just topology — treat them as design targets, not SLA guarantees.
  • Capex delta: Tier IV costs roughly 25–60% more than Tier III for the same IT capacity (Global Data Center Hub: 25–40%; other sources 40–60%), because every path doubles into physically separated rooms. One consultancy pegs Tier III power architecture at ~2.5× the cost of Tier II. Illustrative $/kW ranges cited: Tier II ~$4,000–5,600/kW, Tier III ~$7,000–10,000/kW, Tier IV ~$11,000–16,000/kW.
  • Break-even heuristic: Tier IV's premium is justified when cost of downtime exceeds ~$500K/hr and expected unplanned downtime exceeds ~1.3 h/yr.

Why most hyperscale is "Tier III-like, uncertified": hyperscalers build to Tier III topology and handle failures in software across availability zones, so paying for Tier IV hardware or Uptime certification adds little value — they self-declare. Colos market their level to win tenants (CBRE quotes wholesale rates specifically for "N+1/Tier III requirements"), and Tier IV is reserved for financial/government/healthcare workloads (<5% of certified facilities). Retrofitting up a tier is usually uneconomic — moving built Tier II→III typically needs a second distribution path ("often impossible without major demolition"); Tier IV retrofit can exceed 70–90% of a new-build cost.

5. Codes and Standards That Bind the Design

5.1 Taxonomy: code vs standard vs guideline vs certification

  • Code — a set of minimum legal rules; enforceable when adopted by a jurisdiction. Example: NFPA 70 (the National Electrical Code) becomes law where a state/city adopts it.
  • Standard — detailed technical requirements developed as good practice (e.g., ANSI/BICSI 002-2024, TIA-942-C, ASHRAE 90.4). A standard becomes binding when a code adopts it by reference — i.e., the code text says "comply with Standard X," giving X the force of law.
  • Guideline — advisory process/best-practice (e.g., ASHRAE Guideline 0). Not mandatory unless a contract or owner adopts it.
  • Certification — third-party verification against a standard (Uptime Tier, ISO 27001, SOC 2). Voluntary, market- or contract-driven.

The Authority Having Jurisdiction (AHJ) — usually the local building/fire official — decides which editions apply and enforces them, including local amendments. This is why the BOD must name the exact code editions in force at the site.

5.2 The binding US codes and how they reach a data center

  • NFPA 70 (National Electrical Code / NEC) — the core electrical code. Article 645 (Information Technology Equipment) is optional: it offers alternative wiring methods only if the owner meets all conditions in 645.4 (dedicated HVAC, room restricted to qualified personnel, fire-rated separation, and a compliant disconnecting means / Emergency Power Off, EPO). Owners "opt into" 645 to gain wiring flexibility; if they don't meet 645.4, ordinary NEC Chapters 1–4 apply. Other relevant NEC articles: 110 (working clearances), 250 (grounding), 408 (switchboards), 480 (batteries), 646 (modular data centers), 685. The 2026 NEC tightens Article 645 cabling/listing rules aligned with NFPA 75.
  • NFPA 75 — Fire Protection of Information Technology Equipment; NFPA 76 — Fire Protection of Telecommunications Facilities. Not directly referenced by the IBC but used as industry best practice and cross-referenced with NEC 645. Also relevant: NFPA 855 (lithium-ion / energy storage — drives UPS/battery-room fire ratings and suppression), NFPA 72 (alarm), NFPA 2001 (clean-agent suppression), NFPA 70E (electrical worker safety, arc-flash).
  • IBC (International Building Code) and IFC (International Fire Code) — the principal architectural/fire basis in most US jurisdictions, plus IMC/IPC (mechanical/plumbing).
  • Energy codes — IECC and ASHRAE 90.1 / 90.4: ASHRAE 90.4 is the data-center-specific energy standard (companion to 90.1, which still governs envelope, lighting, service water). 90.4 compliance rests on two calculated numbers checked against climate-zone maxima: the Mechanical Load Component (MLC) (cooling/fan/pump/heat-rejection power ÷ IT power) and the Electrical Loss Component (ELC) (power-chain losses). 90.4-2022 lowered both, dropped the design-MLC snapshot in favor of an annualized MLC (8760-hour model at 25/50/75/100% IT load), aligned ELC to the same part-load method, and brought transformer efficiency in at realistic loading. MLC maxima vary by climate zone (cooling depends on weather); ELC maxima do not (losses don't care about weather). 90.4 defines PUE but does not use it for compliance. IECC adopts 90.4 by reference (2024 IECC → Sections 6 & 8 of 90.4-2022); the enforceable edition varies by jurisdiction. 90.4 applies to data centers with >20 W/ft² and >10 kW IT load.
  • Seismic / structural — via IBC and ASCE 7 (site-dependent; the requirement side is what the OPR/density plan demand of the structure — see the worked example's floor-loading numbers).

5.3 Outside the US (one paragraph)

Internationally, the IEC publishes electrical standards, and the EN 50600 / ISO/IEC 22237 family is the dominant data-center design standard in Europe and increasingly globally — covering building construction, power distribution, environmental control, cabling, physical security and management, with availability/protection classes and ISO/IEC 30134 energy KPIs. National building and electrical codes (and, in the EU, the Energy Efficiency Directive's data-center reporting) sit on top. EN 16798 plays the ventilation/indoor-climate role analogous to parts of ASHRAE's suite.

6. Certifications and Audits as Facility Concerns

6.1 What each audit demands physically from the facility

The key distinction for an owner: most of these frameworks are about documented process, but each also imposes concrete physical/design requirements. Separating them:

  • SOC 2 (AICPA) — a report on trust-services controls. Physical side: continuously operating perimeter cameras (auditors check they "operated continuously during the observation period" with logs to prove it), physical access controls, environmental monitoring (temperature/humidity sensors, water-leak detection under floors, clean-agent fire suppression, UPS). Mostly process, but the environmental controls are real hardware.
  • ISO/IEC 27001:2022 — information-security management. Physical side: Annex A.7 "Physical Controls" (renamed/reorganized from the 2013 A.11) requires secure perimeters, physical entry controls, secure cabling, equipment maintenance, and protection of supporting utilities. Audits test control effectiveness, not just documentation.
  • PCI DSS (PCI SSC) — Requirement 9 governs physical access to the cardholder-data environment: facility entry controls, video cameras and/or access-control mechanisms monitoring entry/exit to sensitive areas, with footage/logs retained ≥ 3 months (90 days), visitor badges visibly distinct from personnel and surrendered/deactivated on exit, visitor logs, and restriction of network jacks/wireless. (Note: the 90-day retention applies to PCI DSS Req 9.1.1-type controls; a common myth overstates it — verify against the current PCI DSS 4.0 text.)
  • HIPAA Security Rule — facility access controls with contingency operations, visitor validation, maintenance records (45 CFR §164.310 physical safeguards: four required + three addressable specs).
  • FedRAMP / NIST SP 800-53 — for US federal workloads. The Physical and Environmental Protection (PE) control family is the concrete one: PE-3 (physical access authorizations, access logs reviewed at defined frequency), plus surveillance, intrusion detection, environmental monitoring, and (at FISMA High / FedRAMP High) the most stringent physical-and-environmental controls. Colos market "NIST 800-53 High baseline" specifically to serve federal/regulated tenants.

The design takeaway: concrete, steel and hardware requirements (concentric access zones, mantraps, camera coverage of all entry/exit, ≥90-day retention storage, fire-rated separations, leak detection, redundant power to security systems) are driven mostly by SOC 2 environmental controls, PCI Req 9, ISO A.7 and NIST PE — while the bulk of each framework is documented process the operator, not the building, must satisfy.

6.2 Energy/sustainability certifications at the requirements stage

  • LEED (USGBC) — green-building rating. LEED for data centers requires, as a prerequisite, whole-facility energy metering exportable to ENERGY STAR Portfolio Manager (EAp3), and rewards efficient cooling, water and PUE. Committing to LEED in the OPR forces day-one design choices: an OPR (LEED itself requires an OPR for its Fundamental/Enhanced Commissioning prerequisites), submetering/DCIM infrastructure, higher-efficiency MEP, and often on-site renewables or heat-reuse provisions. LEED v4.1 O+M offers a performance path via the Arc platform; LEED v5 is current for new construction.
  • ENERGY STAR for data centers (EPA) — a performance label: a facility must operate ≥ 12 months and score ≥ 75 on the 1–100 scale (measured PUE-based benchmarking) to certify. Because it needs 12 months of operating data, it cannot be "designed in" — but committing to it in the OPR forces the metering and efficiency design that makes a ≥75 score achievable.

Committing to either in the OPR mainly changes metering, MEP efficiency targets, and documentation obligations in the BOD — and adds a design constraint the engineer must hit, not just an operational aspiration.

6.3 How certification targets enter the OPR and the contract — and who owns achieving them

Certification targets belong in OPR §8 (Certifications & compliance) as measurable commitments (e.g., "Uptime Tier III Certification of Constructed Facility," "SOC 2 Type II ready," "LEED Gold," "NIST 800-53 High physical/environmental controls"). They then flow into the contract as performance obligations. Who owns achieving them varies by delivery model: - DBB: the owner owns the risk that the assembled design achieves the target; gaps become change orders. - DB / EPC: the design-builder/EPC contractor typically guarantees the certifiable outcome (e.g., "facility will achieve Uptime TCCF Tier III"), backed by performance security and liquidated damages — the strongest way to transfer certification risk, provided the OPR states the target unambiguously. - Split ownership is common for audit frameworks: the building/design achieving physical controls (cameras, access zones, fire rating) can be contracted to the builder, while operational certifications (SOC 2 Type II, ISO 27001, ENERGY STAR's 12-month score) are inherently the operator's to earn after handover.

7. Worked Example — The Reference Facility

Facility: a 1,000-rack colocation campus, ~40 MW IT load, on the outskirts of a hot-climate US metro (Phoenix/Dallas archetype). All numbers below are labeled indicative and are for teaching arithmetic, not procurement.

7.1 OPR headline requirements (indicative)

  • Mission: multi-tenant wholesale + retail colo serving cloud, enterprise and AI/GPU tenants; AI-ready high-density zones.
  • Capacity: ~40 MW ultimate IT load; day-one Phase 1 ≈ 10 MW IT (shell + site power for 40 MW; MEP for 10 MW), phased in ~10 MW increments against leasing velocity and utility interconnection delivery.
  • Availability: Uptime Tier III / concurrently maintainable (≈99.982%, ~1.6 h/yr) as the campus standard, with an optional 2N ("Tier IV-like") suite for financial/government tenants. Rationale: Tier III is the colo market standard (CBRE quotes wholesale rates at "N+1/Tier III"); Tier IV's 25–60% capex premium isn't justified for general multi-tenant load, and hyperscale-style tenants handle resilience in software.
  • Density profile: mixed — see §7.2.
  • Efficiency: design PUE ≤ 1.4; ASHRAE 90.4-2022 MLC/ELC compliance for the applicable hot-climate zone; WUE reported.
  • Certifications: Uptime Tier III (design + constructed); SOC 2 Type II + ISO 27001 ready; PCI DSS Req 9 physical controls; NIST 800-53 physical/environmental controls for a federal-capable suite; LEED target.
  • Schedule/budget: phased; capex tracked as $/MW (indicative Tier III ≈ $7–10M/MW of IT for the fit-out portion, higher all-in).

Cascade sanity checks (indicative): - Racks per MW: the AI band runs ~9 racks/MW (at 110 kW); the low-density band ~125 racks/MW (at 8 kW) — a >10× spread within one building. - Area: air-cooled zones at ~20 ft²/rack → the 650 air-cooled racks need ~13,000 ft²; liquid zones at ~12–15 ft²/rack → the 350 dense/AI racks need ~4,500–5,250 ft². Gray space (power/cooling plant) adds substantially on top. - Floor loading: AI racks up to ~1,500 lb each require structural headroom designed in on day one — cheap to future-proof, expensive to retrofit.

7.3 Day-one vs ultimate, and the phasing logic

  • Shell & site: built for 40 MW ultimate (structure, floor loading, pathways, pipe stubs) on day one — the cheap future-proofing.
  • Phase 1 MEP: ~10 MW (transformers, UPS, generators, chillers) — the expensive plant, deferred and modularized so capital tracks leasing and interconnection.
  • Reserve: ~15–20% of white/gray space held for reconfiguration; high-density/AI pods pre-provisioned with busway and liquid manifolds so they don't strand the general floor.

Stranded-capacity watch: if the AI band grows faster than planned, cooling or busway can max out before floor space does — the classic stranding failure. The OPR's phasing triggers and the BOD's diversified design-load assumption (design load < sum of nameplate) are the guards against it.

Open Questions and What's Changing Fast in 2026

  • Density forecasts are escalating faster than facilities can be built. GB200 NVL72 at ~120–132 kW is shipping; Vera Rubin is cited up to 246 kW; Deloitte projects ~370 kW racks in 2026. Any "AI-ready" spec risks obsolescence within one hardware generation. These are projections — treat as such.
  • The cooling thresholds are genuinely unsettled. Sources disagree on where air gives out (20 vs 40–50 kW), how far RDHx reaches (40 vs 80 vs 100 kW), and where immersion becomes necessary. Design to ranges, not points.
  • Power availability, not space or capital, is now the binding constraint (CBRE/JLL: record-low 1.6% vacancy, 74%+ pre-leasing, interconnection delays) — phasing is increasingly dictated by the utility, not the leasing pipeline.
  • Codes are moving: 2026 NEC tightens Article 645; jurisdictions adopt different ASHRAE 90.4 editions; NFPA 855 (batteries) is reshaping UPS-room design. Always verify the AHJ's adopted editions.
  • OCP standardization of AI infrastructure (Open Systems for AI, NVIDIA + Meta contributions) may soon give colos reference designs that reduce the speculative-flexibility penalty — a genuine structural shift to watch.

Glossary

  • OPR (Owner's Project Requirements) — the owner's measurable statement of what the facility must do; the acceptance yardstick.
  • BOD (Basis of Design) — the engineer's document recording how the design meets the OPR, with calculations, topologies and codes.
  • ASHRAE Guideline 0 / Standard 202 — the commissioning-process guideline/standard that make the OPR the basis for acceptance.
  • AHJ (Authority Having Jurisdiction) — the local official who decides which code editions apply and enforces them.
  • Density profile — the distribution of kW/rack across zones/pods, not a single average.
  • Stranded capacity — capacity paid for but unusable because a different resource (power, cooling, space) ran out first.
  • Design vs nameplate vs actual load — the sized design load, the sum of equipment ratings, and the real operating draw; each is smaller than the last.
  • Concurrent maintainability — any component can be serviced without shutting down IT (Uptime Tier III).
  • Fault tolerance — any single failure is survived automatically (Uptime Tier IV).
  • Tier / Rated / Availability Class — redundancy-topology levels from Uptime, TIA-942, and EN 50600/ISO 22237 respectively.
  • MLC / ELC — Mechanical Load Component and Electrical Loss Component; ASHRAE 90.4's two compliance metrics.
  • NEC Article 645 — optional NEC provisions for IT rooms (including the EPO) that owners opt into for wiring flexibility.
  • TCDD / TCCF / TCOS — Uptime's design-document, constructed-facility, and operational-sustainability certifications.
  • RDHx / DLC / immersion — rear-door heat exchanger, direct-to-chip liquid cooling, and immersion cooling — cooling methods enabled at rising densities.
  • PUE / WUE — power and water usage effectiveness (efficiency metrics; named only here).
  • Code / standard / guideline / certification — legal minimum rules / good-practice technical requirements / advisory best practice / third-party verification.

What Surprised the Researcher

  1. The average rack density is genuinely low. Despite AI headlines, Uptime's 2025 survey still puts the modal average near 9 kW and finds >80% of operators have no racks above the high-density thresholds — the installed base is far behind the leading edge, and "40 kW average" only appears in new designs.
  2. The OPR is deliberately kept out of the contract. ASHRAE's own HQ OPR excludes itself from the contract documents "to avoid conflicting language" — a subtle but important legal design choice that surprised me given how central the OPR is.
  3. Uptime openly documents its own gaming problem. The Institute itself describes companies achieving a design certification and then "willfully misrepresenting" it to market untested facilities — the "Tier III-like" plaque problem is acknowledged by the certifier, not just critics.
  4. "Cheap to future-proof vs expensive" is a cleaner rule than expected. Space, structure and pipe stubs cost little on day one; transformers and plant are the expensive, deferrable items — a genuinely useful heuristic that cuts through the phasing debate.
  5. Change orders cluster in the final half of the project (AIA's 892k-change-order dataset) — meaning a vague OPR doesn't bite early when it's cheap to fix, but late when the owner has the fewest options and the most leverage against them.
  6. Power, not money or land, is the real constraint in 2025–26 — record-low 1.6% vacancy and utility interconnection delays now dictate phasing, inverting the traditional "build to leasing velocity" logic.
