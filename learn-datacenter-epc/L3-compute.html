What computing equipment asks of a data center

Deep detail and sources: the appendix for this topic. Short version: sixty words. The research report this chapter was distilled from. Earlier version: archived 2026-09-26.

Imagine renting a powered rack position at the course's 1,000-rack colocation campus outside Phoenix. In colocation, the owner supplies space, electricity, cooling and connection routes; customers usually bring the computing equipment. Information technology (IT) load is the electrical power drawn by that equipment. One kilowatt (kW) is 1,000 watts, and one megawatt (MW) is 1,000 kW.

Across four rack zones the design IT load is 38.9 MW, rounded to 40 MW. Power usage effectiveness (PUE) is total facility power divided by IT power; at the design value of 1.4, that rounded 40 MW calls for about 56 MW at the site boundary. The 200 artificial intelligence (AI) positions receive 24 MW of the IT allowance. These five numbers frame the story before we look inside a rack.

Why the rack mix matters

A rack allowance is the electrical service planned for a position, not a reading from equipment already installed. The course divides 1,000 positions among four zones. The bar compares their planned IT power rather than their floor area.

Takeaway: Only one rack in five is an AI position, yet those positions take nearly two thirds of the planned IT power. The other 800 positions together take 14.9 MW and can support a varied mix of customers. An AI pod is a group of rack-scale machines and their supporting connections; whether all its racks run one job depends on the tenant. Design each position for its declared draw, not a campus-wide average (APX-compute §12).

Capacity is not occupancy

The four zones sum to 38.9 MW of allowed IT power, not measured electricity use. Multiplying the unrounded figure by the design PUE gives about 54.5 MW; the course's approximately 56 MW site figure uses its rounded 40 MW plan. The first roughly 10 MW phase can include about 50 AI positions at 6 MW, leaving capacity for other zones (APX-compute §12).

What is inside an AI rack

Most server cabinets contain many independent computers. NVIDIA's GB200 NVL72 is different: it is a factory-built cabinet with 72 graphics processing units (GPUs) for parallel calculations and 36 central processing units (CPUs) that help run them. NVIDIA's NVLink connects the GPUs inside the rack so they can work together.

Compute trays hold the chips; NVLink switch trays connect them, and power shelves feed a 54-volt (V) busbar inside the rack. A coolant manifold sends liquid to cold plates pressed against the chips; dripless quick-disconnects join its pipes to the building loop. Optical connections leave the cabinet for other racks. Building cooling, studied in Topic 7, carries heat away from the coolant connection shown here.

Takeaway: Instead of feeding dozens of little machines separately, the hall must feed and cool one integrated system. The picture's 72 fibers out label is shorthand for approximately 72 outward optical connections, not a guaranteed count of individual glass strands. The coolant connector is a practical handoff, but who supplies the cooling equipment beyond it depends on the lease (APX-compute §1; APX-compute §3).

What a conventional cabinet does and does not standardize
  • The Electronic Industries Alliance (EIA) rack standard EIA-310 fixes a 19-inch mounting pattern. It does not certify a cabinet's external depth or safe loaded mass.
  • Larger AI frames can use other widths. Liquid-cooled cabinets still put some heat into room air, so airflow and blanking panels remain important.
  • The later NVIDIA GB300 NVL72 remains a 72-GPU rack class, but its power and loaded mass must be checked independently of GB200 (APX-compute §1; APX-compute §3).

Why a training job changes power demand

Imagine many tenants running unrelated applications: one busy rack need not peak when its neighbor does. A training job instead coordinates many GPUs: they calculate, exchange results, wait and repeat. When one job spans much of an AI pod, their electrical peaks can align. Scientific high-performance computing (HPC) can likewise coordinate many machines, although its exact power pattern depends on the task. The illustration contrasts independent and aligned demand.

Takeaway: The coordinated case can stay near rated draw for hours and change appreciably roughly once a second. Capacitors in some rack power shelves and slower software ramps can soften brief peaks. They cannot power an outage, so the uninterruptible power supply (UPS) still needs its own backup duty. In a multi-tenant pod, only racks sharing a job necessarily move together; the owner cannot assume useful diversity without evidence or a contract (APX-compute §4; APX-compute §9).

What smoothing does, and does not, buy

Measurements of some training workloads show strong swings around 0.2–3 hertz (Hz), or cycles per second. GB300 power shelves have capacitors with about 65 joules (J) per GPU, roughly 4,700 J per rack. NVIDIA reports about a 30% lower grid-facing peak for a particular training workload, not a universal reduction or credit against UPS sizing. A tenant can also slow a job's startup or cap GPU power, potentially affecting performance (APX-compute §4).

How the rest of a job fits in the room

A training job needs data, a network and a way to save progress. Flash storage uses chips instead of moving disks; a checkpoint is a saved job state used after a fault. Network racks hold switches for optical connections between cabinets, while NVLink stays within a cabinet.

A busway is an overhead power rail with A and B feeds, two separate supply routes. A coolant distribution unit (CDU) moves heat toward the building's cooling plant. Phase 1 is the first roughly 10 MW IT build, with 50 AI racks taking 6 MW. This drawing places those racks in the full pod.

The counts do not design the network or storage
  • A high-speed port per GPU is a useful starting count. Switches, cable lengths and the number of glass strands per optical connection require a separate network design (APX-compute §5).
  • A few flash racks may serve many GPU racks, but their count and checkpoint capacity depend on the job. Confirm whether supporting racks occupy positions within the course's 1,000-rack mix; if not, budget their power and floor space separately (APX-compute §8).

What the other rack positions hold

Most of the other 800 positions hold separate computers in 19-inch cabinets, not one linked machine. A 15 kW rack may hold roughly 20–30 ordinary CPU servers, while a 40 kW position may hold two or three eight-GPU servers, depending on their draw. Blanking panels cover empty slots so cooling air passes through equipment rather than around it. The AI cabinet shown weighs about 1,500 kilograms (kg); drive-filled storage racks may draw few kilowatts but still weigh a lot. The sketch puts three rack types on the same patch of floor.

Takeaway: The picture compares a server cabinet, a complete AI machine and a storage cabinet; it does not assert that storage is heavier in absolute terms than the AI rack. Compare the draw of each machine with its rack's allowance before deciding how many fit. Three servers drawing 14 kW each, for instance, would exceed a 40 kW position.

Takeaway: Storage can have high mass relative to its low power draw, so watts are a poor shortcut for floor loading. Its racks still need network routes and a suitable position; storage is not a fifth zone added to the 1,000-rack count. Size it by actual equipment and drive mass, not a universal kilograms-per-kilowatt rule (APX-compute §7; APX-compute §8).

Storage and software choices
  • A server may keep data on its own drives. Tenants can also use separate file storage, block storage or object storage. Large training jobs may use a parallel file system to feed many GPUs and save checkpoints (APX-compute §8).
  • Enterprise tenants often run independent virtual machines, meaning software-defined servers that share physical equipment. A training tenant may schedule one coordinated job across many machines. The colocation owner usually sees meters, not the tenant's job schedule (APX-compute §9).

Check a rack against the installed service

The course's AI position is planned for 120 kW of rack power, roughly 160 L/min at the coolant connection and a rack weighing about 1,500 kg. Together those limits form a service envelope, not a promise that any newer cabinet will work. The NVIDIA GB300, a later 72-GPU rack, can draw 132–142 kW in normal operation and weigh about 1,580 kg when populated. The course sizes room-air cooling at 75% liquid capture for a 120 kW rack, leaving about 30 kW for room air. Compare those requirements with what the site planned.

Takeaway: This GB300 example exceeds both the electrical and loaded-mass planning allowances. A cited 155 kW peak does not fit merely because the two supply routes are separate; check the whole power path and cooling system, or agree on a tested power cap. Even a GB200 can draw above its nominal 120 kW in observed full-load operation, so a model name is not proof of fit. Floor checks must include the loaded rack's delivery route and wheels, not just its resting position (APX-compute §2; APX-compute §6).

Coolant flow and temperature belong in the same calculation

At 160 L/min and a rack-water temperature rise of 8 degrees Celsius (°C), water removes roughly 90 kW of heat, about three quarters of a 120 kW rack load. A different liquid-capture fraction or allowable temperature rise changes the required flow. Facility water and rack coolant may pass through a heat exchanger without mixing, so facility supply temperature does not automatically equal the specified rack inlet temperature (APX-compute §2).

Why future racks may not fit

At the course's 2026 snapshot, rack designs are changing faster than concrete can be rebuilt. GB200 was planned around 120 kW but has been measured above it; its GB300 successor is 132–142 kW nominal. NVIDIA's announced Rubin variants reach roughly 190 kW, while a later design targets roughly 600 kW. Those future figures are not services this campus has installed. The scale separates the building's allowance from products and targets.

Takeaway: Keep the 120 kW pod for equipment operated within its verified power, cooling and mass limits; do not sell it as automatically GB300-ready. At far higher densities, low-voltage busbars need unwieldy current and copper. Designers therefore plan new rack systems using 800-volt direct current (DC), which also requires different conversion and protection. Preserve routes for upgrades, but contract only for capacity installed and tested (APX-compute §10).

Why voltage enters the rack roadmap

At a simplified 600 kW load, 54 V would imply more than 11,000 amperes, while 800 V implies about 750 amperes, before losses. Lower current eases conductor demands but requires compatible equipment. A vendor's suggestion to provision a busway around 192 kW is not evidence that this course's 120 kW rack position already has that service (APX-compute §10).

Why buildings and computers keep different clocks

Even a well-matched rack will eventually be replaced. AI equipment may turn over after roughly 3–5 years, while the building shell, its structure and enclosure, is planned for 15–20 years and major electrical and cooling equipment for about 10–15. Put the handoff in the lease

Set limits for sustained and peak power, loaded mass, residual air heat and optical connections. At the quick-disconnect, name who warrants the rack and who guarantees coolant temperature, flow, filtration and chemistry. State refresh rights, power caps and who funds upgrades. A and B feeds do not double a 120 kW allowance. Tier III class means the facility can be maintained without a planned IT shutdown; it does not protect every single-corded server from a failed feed (APX-compute §6; APX-compute §12).
