Networks and cabling for a new data center

Deep detail and sources: the appendix for this topic. Short version: sixty words. The research report this chapter was distilled from. Earlier version: archived 2026-09-26.

Picture the course's 1,000-rack colocation campus near a hot American city. Colocation means customers called tenants rent powered space from an operator, which builds the shared facility. The equipment draws about 40 megawatts (MW) of information technology (IT) power. At a design power usage effectiveness (PUE) of 1.4, the ratio of total facility power to IT power, the campus draws about 56 MW. Its 200 artificial intelligence (AI) racks each draw 120 kilowatts (kW), and the campus opens in roughly 10 MW phases. The network question has two parts: how a tenant's servers communicate, and how an outside network provider, or carrier, reaches that tenant. Those paths require different switches, rooms and owners.

Separate the three journeys

At 800 gigabits per second (800G), an unpowered copper connection reaches only about two metres (m). For longer connections, fiber carries data as light through thin glass strands. Those limits, and the campus's size, explain why network planning needs both rack locations and outside routes.

Takeaway: An internal server exchange stays on the tenant's switches; an outside connection passes through shared rooms. The repair route must remain accessible even if normal switching fails. The 24 MW AI zone spans the campus, not just its first 10 MW hall (APX-network §12).

Where the 40 MW comes from

Three hundred racks at 8 kW, 300 at 15 kW, 200 at 40 kW and 200 at 120 kW sum to 38.9 MW. The course rounds this to 40 MW, then multiplies by design PUE 1.4 to get about 56 MW for planning. The AI racks are part of that total, not an extra 24 MW.

Give servers more than one short route

When servers in different tenant racks exchange data, they do not have to visit the carrier room. A leaf switch connects nearby servers; a spine switch connects leaves. In a spine-leaf Clos fabric, every leaf connects to every spine, providing several usable paths instead of one central switch.

Takeaway: The two switch-to-switch links are leaf to spine and spine to leaf; servers on the same leaf need neither. Other spines remain usable if one fails, provided the fabric has enough spare capacity. How many ports should each leaf devote to those spine links?

Oversubscription is server-facing bandwidth divided by spine-facing bandwidth. A non-blocking 1:1 design matches both amounts, while 3:1 assumes servers do not all transmit at full speed at once.

Takeaway: Synchronized AI jobs favor 1:1 and the extra uplinks and fiber it requires; lighter traffic can accept 3:1. Neither split by itself guarantees an application's performance (APX-network §1).

The same trip between tenant racks can use either spine because every leaf connects to both.

A packet from Server A to Server B crosses two switch-to-switch links whether it travels through Spine 1 or Spine 2. Both routes are usable, and the other spine remains available if one fails, provided the fabric has enough spare capacity.

How a fabric chooses paths

One common design uses external Border Gateway Protocol (eBGP) to exchange routes and equal-cost multipath (ECMP) to distribute conversations across available spines. To join larger groups of racks, a third switch layer called a super-spine can be added; that also needs more fiber and ports. A service sharing routed switches among customers may add an Ethernet virtual private network (EVPN) over Virtual Extensible Local Area Network (VXLAN), while a dedicated AI back end can omit that overlay. These are design choices, not equipment the facility operator necessarily supplies (APX-network §1 and APX-network §2).

Place AI switches where copper can reach

An AI pod is a repeatable group of nearby compute racks and their supporting network gear. Its graphics processing units (GPUs) exchange training results between racks, not just answer user requests. Joining GPUs inside a rack is scale-up; connecting racks is scale-out. A separate back end carries those GPU exchanges, while a front end carries user requests.

Takeaway: A rail puts the same numbered GPU network port on nearby machines onto the same leaf, so local exchanges can avoid a spine. Passive direct-attach copper (DAC) reaches about 2 m at 800G; retimed active electrical cable (AEC) reaches about 5 to 7 m. An overhead raceway, a protected cable route, carries longer fiber links toward the spines.

Takeaway: The picture illustrates two GPU racks with one nearby network rack. It does not mean one leaf can reach all 200 AI racks over copper; farther links need fiber or another leaf group. The tenant chooses its fabric, while the operator reserves space, power, cooling and cable paths for it (APX-network §3 and APX-network §12).

Inside the rack versus between racks

NVIDIA NVLink joins GPUs inside a high-density accelerator rack; that is scale-up, outside the building's structured fiber plant. A tenant may use two separate back-end switch networks for redundancy and throughput. Exact switch and optical-module counts cannot be inferred from this two-rack sketch (APX-network §3).

Choose the AI back end before buying equipment

A tenant chooses how its GPUs exchange training results, while the operator supplies space and shared routes. Remote direct memory access (RDMA) lets one server move data directly into another's memory. RDMA over Converged Ethernet version 2 (RoCEv2) carries that traffic on Ethernet; InfiniBand is a specialized alternative, and Ultra Ethernet is an emerging open design.

Takeaway: Ethernet overtook InfiniBand in AI back-end switch sales in 2025; sales do not reveal the entire installed base. The running example's AI tenant chooses Ethernet with RoCEv2, but other wholesale tenants can choose their own fabrics (APX-network §4).

An optical transceiver is a powered module that converts electrical signals to light, or light back to electrical signals. The high-speed fiber links here need a transceiver at each end. One 2025 estimate puts network equipment at about 15% of the cost of a large GPU compute cluster.

Takeaway: The $60 is not 60% of the building budget. The course's $400 million to $500 million construction cost excludes tenant IT hardware; these AI back-end transceivers are a tenant equipment and spares issue (APX-network §6).

Keep the estimates in their proper buckets

The 15% and 60% estimates come from a particular NVIDIA GB300 accelerator-cluster design in SemiAnalysis's 2025 study, not every AI tenant. Optics account for about 45% of networking power in that estimate, but some switch power specifications already include their plug-in optics. RoCEv2 commissioning also checks priority flow control (PFC) and explicit congestion notification (ECN); ordinary Ethernet connectivity is not enough to prove GPU performance. The course's first roughly 10 MW phase costs $180 million to $230 million because it includes shared site and shell work. Whole-campus yearly operating costs of about $65 million, including about $34 million for electricity, are yet another denominator (APX-network §6 and APX-network §12).

Let distance choose the cable

Once switch positions are known, each cable has a required distance and speed. Passive copper is useful inside a rack, retimed copper reaches an adjacent rack, and fiber reaches across rows and rooms. The bands show convenient choices for 800G, not a minimum distance that fiber must travel.

Takeaway: The 2 m and 7 m marks explain why AI leaves sit close to GPUs. Fiber also works at short distances; its marked reaches describe example optical modules, not the limits of the glass itself. For permanent high-speed runs, the campus uses optical single-mode grade 2 (OS2) fiber, which can remain as active optics are replaced. Category 6A (Cat6A) twisted-pair copper is for management, cameras and sensors; it supports 10 gigabits per second to 100 m (APX-network §5 and APX-network §9).

Count fibers and connectors, not just cable lengths

A base-8 trunk groups fibers in multiples of eight. Multi-fiber push-on (MPO) connectors exist in different fiber counts: a conventional eight-lane parallel optic uses 16 fibers, so one 12-fiber connector cannot terminate that link. Other optics use a pair of fibers and a two-fiber connector. Specify the module, fiber count, connector, transmit-to-receive mapping and permitted signal loss for each run. Permanent fiber may remain for 15 to 25 years while replaceable optics follow a much shorter equipment cycle (APX-network §9).

Give carriers two ways into the building

A carrier brings fiber along a duct bank, a group of buried conduits. That cable enters an entrance room (ER), where outside service is handed over; this campus has two separately routed approaches, at least 20 m apart. In a carrier-neutral meet-me room (MMR), multiple providers can join their panels to customers' panels through short fiber jumpers called cross-connects. The main distribution area (MDA) is a hall's backbone hub beyond those shared rooms.

Carriers can use dense wavelength-division multiplexing (DWDM) equipment to place several channels of light on one fiber pair. This drawing follows the campus meet-me room:

Takeaway: Two separated approaches reduce excavation risk, but one MMR remains a single shared room. The campus is Tier III-class, aiming to maintain building power and cooling without shutting down equipment; that goal does not guarantee two independent carrier services. The campus's single meet-me room must work before a tenant can use its circuit. Carrier laterals, the off-site links into a new building, can take months, so they are ordered early (APX-network §7).

What sits behind a fiber jumper

A cross-connect is sold as a managed connection, not merely a piece of glass. Research quotations run roughly $100 to $300 per month, varying by market and contract. Data center interconnect (DCI) links separate sites using dark fiber, which the customer lights, or a lit circuit supplied by a carrier. At an internet exchange point (IXP), networks can exchange traffic directly through peering; transit is paid access to wider networks. End-to-end carrier route diversity must be checked beyond the site: 20 m between entrances cannot prevent a shared upstream cut (APX-network §7).

Take shared fiber from the hub to the rack

The first hall needs shared fiber before any tenant installs a switch. From its main distribution area, an overhead star reaches each pod's horizontal distribution area (HDA), a patching point. The equipment distribution area (EDA) is the rack and its outlets; an ordinary rack may have a top-of-rack (ToR) switch that keeps server cables local. Other places to put a switch

An end-of-row (EoR) or middle-of-row (MoR) switch can serve several ordinary racks, reducing switch count but requiring longer server cables. A top-of-rack (ToR) switch uses more switch units while keeping the short links and a rack-scale failure boundary. The AI arrangement is a different compromise driven by high-speed copper reach (APX-network §2).

Coordinate cables with power and cooling

Above the 120 kW racks, several systems compete for the same ceiling. A busway is a rigid overhead power conductor; a fiber raceway holds protected optical cables; a liquid manifold distributes cooling fluid. The air-containment roof keeps warm exhaust away from cool intake air. Seismic braces secure suspended equipment to the structure, while each system still needs maintenance clearance.

Takeaway: The drawing offers one coordinated arrangement, not a universal height rule. Leave approximately half the planned tray area free for service and growth, then verify code and cable limits. Avoid liquid joints above cable pathways, preserve access to every system and keep power and communications routes separated. Electrical, cooling and network designers settle these routes before the hangers go up (APX-network §10).

Build an independent repair network

Suppose the normal facility network fails while an engineer is trying to restore a controller. An out-of-band (OOB) network provides a separate route to management ports without using the switches it may need to repair. A baseboard management controller (BMC) lets a server report faults and accept commands even if its main operating system stops. A console port is a direct configuration connection to a switch; both types of port need reachable, secured management access.

Takeaway: The flow shows the operator's route to building power, cooling and security controllers. A tenant provides a parallel management route for its BMCs and switches inside its cage. In Meta's 2021 outage, primary and out-of-band access both failed, forcing engineers onsite. Test a failure of the normal switches, outside path and power feed without losing the repair route; bring it online before equipment depends on remote monitoring (APX-network §8).

Deliver, test and hand over the fiber

The first roughly 10 MW phase is delivered by design-build: one contract joins the facility's design and construction, with long-lead building equipment supplied by the owner. Communications cabling is Division 27 construction work, planned alongside electrical and cooling routes. A Registered Communications Distribution Designer (RCDD) can coordinate that layout. The lease still decides where the operator's cable ends and the tenant's begins.

Takeaway: The first live carrier service cannot wait for a later phase, while tenants install their own fabric as leased space becomes ready. Certify installed cables before lighting circuits; get the management path running before relying on it for equipment commissioning (APX-network §10 and APX-network §11).

What certification proves

An insertion-loss test measures how much light a fiber link loses. Additional fault-location testing can produce a trace with an optical time-domain reflectometer (OTDR). Manufacturer warranties depend on approved parts, installers and documented results, not simply on a claim of Tier 2 testing. The RCDD credential is not, by itself, a universal legal requirement for a stamped design (APX-network §10 and APX-network §11).

Name a certifier and buyer for each link

For every run, name who buys the cable and optics, who pulls and labels it, who certifies it, and who accepts the result. Specify loss limits, any required trace, and drawings of the actual routes at handover. Owner-furnished building equipment does not automatically include the tenant's AI switches or 800G optics. Order carrier laterals and tenant long-lead network parts early enough that a finished hall does not open without usable connectivity (APX-network §11).
