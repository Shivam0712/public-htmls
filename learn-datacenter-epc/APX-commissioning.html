The levels as industry convention

Data-center commissioning is organised into numbered Levels whose verification scope widens from a single component to the whole plant. Levels 1–5 are an industry convention, not ASHRAE's commissioning phases. As CxPlanner puts it, "Rather than describing the process the way ASHRAE Guideline 0 does for general construction, data center projects organise testing into numbered levels... Instead of talking about an OPR and a BOD, the data center industry talks about Levels" (CxPlanner). Five is the common count; some programs run from Level 0 (design review) to Level 6 (handover) or 7 (a first-year check-in). What matters is that the tested scope widens and the witness share rises.

How ASHRAE maps on. ASHRAE Guideline 0-2019 and ASHRAE/IES Standard 202 supply the process within which the industry Levels operate: the OPR, BOD, Cx plan, functional test requirements, systems manual and training. Standard 202-2013 defines the minimum acceptable process; Guideline 0 describes best-practice phases. Design review corresponds roughly to Level 0, construction-phase functional testing to Levels 3–4, and occupancy verification to Levels 5–6, but these are comparisons, not ASHRAE Level definitions (CxPlanner; ASHRAE). Topic 2's OPR and BOD are the yardstick here.

Electrical acceptance before integrated operation. ANSI/NETA ATS-2025 means the Acceptance Testing Specifications for electrical power equipment and systems; here ATS does not mean an automatic transfer switch. ANSI-approved 20 February 2025, it specifies pre-energization field inspections and tests for transformers, switchgear, breakers, relays and cables and, new in 2025, Section 7.28 for battery energy storage and UPS. Testing by a NETA Accredited Company with certified technicians is "the last line of defense before commissioning" (NETA, 2025). For the reference hall it gates closing the first breaker on the 34.5 kV bus after Topic 5's 138 kV yard is ready at T+38. Passing electrical acceptance permits energization and individual start-up; it does not prove automatic transfer switches, generator paralleling or cooling ride-through. Those sequences require Levels 4–5.

The Cx plan, load banks and the Phase 1 schedule

The Cx plan. The commissioning plan and specification name what is commissioned, the scripts, the acceptance criteria and who witnesses; "the plan and the project specification, not a generic standard, set the actual scenarios, the load, and the acceptance criteria" (Anvilfield). Here that means the six 2.5 MW UPS blocks in two 3M2 groups (Topic 6), the six generators (Topic 5) and the seven CDUs (Topic 7). The owner and CxA agree the load steps, limits, timing and stop rules before testing.

Load banks. Levels 4–5 need artificial load because there is no live IT. Resistive banks provide kW for thermal and generator tests; reactive units add kVAR for power factor and alternator response; DC units test battery strings; medium-voltage units test transformers and the utility entry. Phase 1 needs roughly 10 MW total test load, built in steps from a mix of resistive or reactive trailers of up to about 3 MW on the pad or at busway and PDU tap-offs, server-emulating 4–20 kW units on rack PDUs, and liquid-cooled units connected to the CDU loop. About 1 MW is the rating of each liquid-cooled test unit, not the total planned liquid load. The number of those units and the load allocated to each part of the hall must follow the agreed Phase 1 rack and thermal test plan; an outdoor trailer cannot prove a 120 kW pod's cooling (research synthesis; PW Consulting).

Rental (verify: rental-house sources, 2026): a 3 MW-class trailer about $1,100–2,000 a day or $7,800–14,500 a month (CountBricks, Austin); a liquid-cooled unit around $18,000 a week against a $400,000-plus purchase price (PW Consulting); an 800 kW unit with fuel and technician about $18,000–25,000 for 48 hours in Northern Virginia; fuel tanks $81–120 a day. These are indicative equipment or package prices, not the commissioning agent's fee.

Heat load and the liquid loop. Distribute rack-level banks to map inlet temperatures, containment, fan-wall and CRAH response, and hot spots against the OPR band at the agreed load steps. For AI pods, check CDU factory witness records, engineered flushing to the agreed low-single-digit-micron cleanliness, fluid condition, flow and balance at the rack and CDU, pressure and leak integrity before and during heating. "Flush and fill" alone is inadequate. Vertiv completed its approximately $1.0 billion acquisition of PurgeRite on 4 December 2025 to strengthen "next-generation thermal chain services for liquid cooling systems" (Vertiv via PR Newswire) (verify: dated). Because server OEM, CDU maker and fluid-supplier boundaries remain unsettled, Topic 7 has the mechanical contractor, CDU vendor and rack integrator jointly commission the TCS loop.

Worked example: the Phase 1 schedule (indicative). Load check: 300 × 8 + 300 × 15 + 200 × 40 + 200 × 120 kW ≈ 38.9 MW; the AI pods are 20% of racks and about 61% of load. At PUE 1.4 the full campus draws about 56 MW at the fence. Phase 1 is the first roughly 10 MW of IT load in Hall 1.

  1. Levels 1–2, T+30–38, parallel to the build: factory witness for gensets, UPS, switchgear and CDUs, including Topic 3's transformer FAT at T+35; inspect gear on receipt.
  2. Level 3, about 4 weeks, beginning around T+38: complete pre-energization NETA acceptance on electrical gear, then energize and start devices in Topic 5's order.
  3. Level 4, about 5 weeks: run each system's normal and fault sequences on load banks, including paralleling, automatic transfer switch timing, chiller and CDU tests, BMS point-to-point checks and rack thermal mapping.
  4. Level 5, about 3 weeks, finishing around T+42: run full-hall integrated failure tests at design load, with recorded electrical, room and liquid-loop results.
  5. Target substantial completion and Hall 1 live around T+42; optional TCCF witness visit, 3–5 days, around T+43.

Allow 12–16 weeks on site for Levels 3–5, including preparation, interfaces and correction or re-test time. Build the roughly 10 MW total load at 0 → 25 → 50 → 75 → 100% of hall design load. On site for IST: CxA lead plus 2–3 engineers; NETA crew 3–4; controls integrator 2; OEM field engineers 4–6; design-builder QA/QC and subs 6–10; owner's operations team 4–6; Uptime 3–5 if TCCF follows; about 25–35 people.

Worked example: the agreed utility-loss sequence, with pass criteria in brackets. (1) Hold the agreed full Phase 1 design load, about 10 MW, with power, rack thermal readings and liquid-loop flow stable [within specified bands]. (2) Open the agreed utility feed at the main switchboard [loss detected and time-stamped as scripted]. (3) UPS carries the critical load on battery [critical IT bus never drops]. (4) Generators start, reach specified voltage and frequency, and parallel [available within the design window, typically no more than 10 seconds]. (5) Transfer the specified loads to generator [correct order and timing, no critical-bus disturbance]. (6) UPS-fed fans and chilled-water thermal mass support cooling through chiller restart [room stays within the OPR band; coolant supply and flow meet the agreed liquid-loop limits; no leaks]. (7) Hold at full load on generator for at least 30 minutes [stable supply and temperatures, no disqualifying alarms]. (8) Restore utility, retransfer and recharge [correct order, clean critical-bus trace and return to normal]. The CxA records electrical and thermal traces and each scripted result. A failed criterion is a finding to correct and re-test, not a pass because the generators started. For optional TCCF, Uptime additionally witnesses concurrent-maintainability demonstrations, removing redundant gensets, chillers or loops while N carries load (Uptime Institute). Topic 12 narrates a real outage.

Durations and cost

Weeks per level. Level 3 takes about 3–5 weeks, Level 4 about 4–6 and Level 5 about 2–4 for an indicative 10 MW hall (research synthesis). Their simple sums span 9–15 weeks, but the 12–16-week field window is a schedule allowance, not a fourth test level: it includes setup and interface checks, sequencing between tests, defects, corrections and re-tests. Levels 1–2 overlap procurement and delivery rather than adding to that field window. A 10-week alternative compresses Level 4 and leaves less time for findings; the course retains the longer window between the completed building and the T+42 opening (Topic 3).

Construction budget versus commissioning. Phase 1 costs about $180–230 million, including shared site, shell and plant work needed by later halls. That is a first-phase capital budget containing shared work, not an additional $180–230 million commissioning charge or an estimate that all of it is shared work. The whole 40 MW campus costs $400–500 million excluding tenant IT. Commissioning is "often estimated to be 0.5% to 2% of the construction costs" (CxPlanner): applied to the whole-campus construction figure, broadly $2–10 million, weighted to the MEP scope Topic 1 put at 75–80% of cost. Do not multiply that whole-campus allowance by Phase 1 again.

Test expenditure. Keep commissioning-agent and test-service fees separate from temporary load-bank rental (§2), generator fuel, vendor standby and purchased test energy. Roughly 10 MW run intermittently for weeks can add a six-figure energy line; fuel depends on generator run time, and rentals depend on fleet and duration (research synthesis). Staging the IST in quarters limits unnecessary running without reducing the specified full-load hold.

Compression and deviations. NETA's 2026 analysis states that "accelerated schedules, dense electrical infrastructure, and heightened performance expectations now place extraordinary pressure on acceptance testing and commissioning teams" (NETA, 2026). Compression can prompt substitutions such as primary-current verification in place of specified full secondary injection on relays. Record and obtain approval for any deviation rather than silently treating a different test as a pass.

What fails and who pays

Findings. Failures cluster in "the crevices... the interfaces between systems" (Gravel2Gavel): sequence-of-operations errors; generator paralleling; automatic transfer switch timing against the specified criterion; breaker settings and coordination across large populations of low-voltage breakers; BMS points mis-mapped; control loops hunting; leaks in liquid loops. Uptime's stated experience is that "more than 85% of all data center designs are incorrectly executed in construction" (Uptime Institute) (verify: Uptime marketing claim, but the stated reason a witnessed as-built check exists). The research gives no findings breakdown by system.

Who writes, executes and witnesses. The design-builder and subs typically write scripts to the specification; the owner's CxA reviews and approves them and witnesses key activity, often about 100% of Levels 4–5. Vendors and subs execute the steps. The owner's operations crew participates to learn the plant. A witness documents a failed result but does not thereby assume the cost of fixing it (§5).

Re-tests and standby. Preserve the failed script, traces, fault investigation, change record and corrected result. Allocate re-test labor, idle load-bank rental, vendor standby and delay by documented cause and the relevant construction, purchase, warranty and commissioning agreements, not automatically by ownership of the equipment or by who attended the test. A builder's installation or script error may fall to the builder or its sub under its contract; a supplied-equipment defect may require an OEM warranty claim, without automatically making every day of builder standby an owner delay. Utility switching restrictions and CxA extra days also require the agreed schedule and compensation terms. For the reference design-build hall with owner-furnished gear, determine whether a failure is in installation, the supplied genset or switchgear, its interface, the script or a utility restriction before allocating expense. If the contracts do not cover the resulting standby and delay, the owner can be left paying rental and carrying the tenant-facing schedule risk (research synthesis of contract practice; §7).

The commissioning agent

Independence. The commissioning agent (CxA) is engaged by and reports to the owner under a separate agreement. The builder must not be able to suppress findings on its own work. ACG (the AABC Commissioning Group) enforces this structurally: its CxA credential is for professionals who are "independent and work on behalf of building owners," and member firms "cannot have ties to contractors, manufacturers, or other" interested parties (ACG). The owner's engineer reviews design intent and submittals, but is design-focused; the engineer of record owns and stamps the design and is not its sole independent test judge. The design-builder's QA/QC is a necessary self-check before offering work for witness. The operations crew learns the equipment, rehearses live procedures and later demonstrates it, but is not a replacement for the CxA.

Test control. The design-builder and subs draft the project-specific scripts against the commissioning specification; the owner-contracted CxA reviews and approves the test scripts and pass criteria with the owner. OEM field staff and subs operate equipment and execute test steps under the approved script, with operations participating; for a later TCCF visit, operations executes the demonstrations Uptime witnesses (§6). The CxA witnesses, records results and logs a failed criterion as a finding with its trace and required re-test. Builder QA/QC logs and corrects its own work, while the owner decides contractual acceptance against the documented results. Witnessing and recording are not the same as performing repairs or accepting liability for their cost.

Credentials. Recognised credentials include ACG's CxA, BCxA's CCP, NEBB's certified commissioning professional and ASHRAE's BCxP. Employers accept 8–10 years of field experience in lieu of a degree and value a PE and NFPA 70E. ACG's CxA exam passed 84 of 145 candidates, 58%, in the year to 31 December 2025 (ACG). The reference Tier III class hall uses the independent CxA rather than relying on design-builder in-house commissioning for critical scope.

Uptime TCCF versus commissioning, and the TIA-942 alternative

The Tier Certification of Constructed Facility (TCCF) is an optional, separate third-party inspection of the as-built plant and witnessed re-demonstration against Uptime's Tier Standard: Topology. It follows completion of the Level 5 integrated systems test (IST); it does not replace the project's commissioning or accept the Cx report as proof. "Uptime Institute consultants observe the Operations team as it performs the different demonstrations, such as interrupting the incoming utility power supply to see that the UPS carries the critical load until the engine generators come on line... and that the cooling system maintains the critical load during the transition," plus "removing redundant capacity components from service and showing that... N engine generators can support the data center critical load or that the redundant chilled water distribution loop can be removed from service while still sufficiently cooling the data center" (Uptime Institute). Uptime also identifies discrepancies between drawings and installed equipment. Its Tier verdict is binary. The visit runs "3–5 days, generally a little more than a month after construction of critical systems ends" (Uptime Institute). IST scenarios "often serve as both the commissioning test and the Tier witness" (Anvilfield), so load banks and scenarios can be reused, but the independent witness and verdict remain distinct.

A topology verdict is not a service guarantee. Tier III addresses concurrent maintainability of the built facility. TCCF neither guarantees an annual availability percentage nor writes the tenant service-level agreement (SLA). Staffing, procedures, faults and the SLA's measurement and credit terms still determine operational and contractual outcomes.

Cost (verify: third-party estimates; Uptime does not publish fees). A full three-stage Tier III program is estimated at roughly $150,000–400,000 (dcgeeks), Tier IV 30–50% higher. TIA-942 rating audits (Rated-1 to Rated-4) are an alternative performed by TIA-licensed certification bodies rather than the standard's author. They cover site, architecture, security, fire, telecom, electrical and mechanical with a rating per domain, are valid three years, and cost about $20,000–50,000 initially (verify). Topic 2 selected optional TCCF for the course's certification path, so TIA-942 is the road not taken.

Acceptance mechanics and the handover package

Substantial versus final completion. Substantial completion is a contract-defined gate at which Hall 1 is safely usable for its intended tenant service, not a declaration that every minor defect is gone. For the reference hall, require signed evidence of named pre-energization checks, Level 4 functions and the Level 5 design-load IST, including utility loss, backup-power hold, cooling ride-through and restoration; energization; delivery of 10 MW IT capacity; and the handover records and operator training needed to run the site. List, price and assign deadlines for any permitted minor punch items. An unresolved safety, critical-load or required-test failure is not such an item. Final completion follows closure of the punch list and remaining close-out obligations, with final payment under the contract. Data-center contracts should name tests and milestones rather than rely on "generic 'substantial completion' language" (Gravel2Gavel).

Performance and payment terms. The course OPR targets PUE ≤ 1.4 at design load. If a PUE result is to gate acceptance or payment, the contract must specify the load and measurement boundary, metering method, environmental and operating conditions, steady-state interval, treatment of temporary test equipment and acceptable result. A design-load test is not a year's measured PUE or an uptime guarantee. Capacity and a properly defined PUE test can condition payment or performance remedies if the contract says so (Gravel2Gavel).

Retainage and damages. Retainage is often 5–10% of completed-work value. As an example, a contract might withhold 5%, release half at substantial completion and the remainder after a 12-month defects-liability period (Gravel2Gavel); that is not a universal release rule for this hall. Delay liquidated damages for a missed completion date and performance damages for capacity or PUE depend on agreed triggers, rates and caps, not merely the use of the word "completion." Topic 1's indicative delay range is 0.1–0.5% a day, capped at 5–10%. Tenant SLA losses may exceed construction-contract recovery: the owner "carries the liability gap between both" (Munich Re).

The owner-furnished-equipment warranty gap. OEM and workmanship warranties can have different start dates, terms and covered parties. Owner-furnished gear delivered at T+30–34 may start its OEM clock at delivery before Hall 1 opens at T+42; the builder's workmanship coverage does not necessarily cure an OEM defect. As a course proposal, negotiate an OEM start at energization or price an extended term at Topic 3's T+3–6 reservations (unsourced proposal). Confirm actual start, expiry, claim procedure and any builder exclusions in the contracts rather than assuming substantial completion starts every warranty.

The handover package. Deliver corrected record ("as-built") drawings reflecting field changes; O&M manuals; the signed Cx report, findings and re-test results; the systems manual (ASHRAE Guideline 1.x); final BMS and EPMS point lists and sequences; vendor start-up reports; breaker settings and coordination-study files; training hours and sign-offs; and, where required, the BIM or digital-twin model. The operations team checks these records against the equipment it witnessed during Levels 4–5. Keys, credentials and an initial set of critical spares change hands with the records. Missing corrected as-builts, point lists, sequences and settings are especially damaging at the first fault (research synthesis). Final account and retainage release follow the specific close-out terms, not possession of keys alone.

Procedures and incidents

Start with the A-side bypass. Before MOP-EL-014 Rev 3 moves UPS Block A to maintenance bypass, the shift engineer confirms that B-side UPS and batteries are healthy and capable, the bypass source is live, and conflicting A- or B-side work is frozen for the approved window. One technician executes each numbered action and a second checks its observed result and initials it; the NOC watches the critical bus. At any failed check, stop, use the staged EOP to return to the last known-good state where safe, and escalate. B-side work stays frozen until A is normal and redundancy has been verified restored.

SOP, MOP, EOP. BICSI 009 requires all three. A standard operating procedure covers repeatable routine work: "The goal of an SOP is consistency." A method of procedure is the step-by-step script for one state-changing action: "The purpose of an MOP is to control actions to ensure the desired outcome" (Uptime Institute). An emergency operating procedure addresses abnormal conditions, safe state first, then redundancy restoration and fault isolation. A MOP carries objective, scope, roles, document control, prerequisites, tools, expected result for each step and rollback. Change-bearing MOPs pass a change advisory board (CAB) and are reviewed periodically and after a failed check, incident or system change. A 10 MW Tier III hall carries dozens, covering UPS bypass, generator tests, chiller changeover, automatic transfer switches, leaks, EPO and fire alarms (Uptime Institute; BICSI 009).

Worked example: MOP-EL-014 Rev 3, "Move UPS Block A to maintenance bypass".

  • ID and dates: MOP-EL-014 Rev 3; effective 2026-01-15; next review 2026-07-15.
  • Objective: transfer UPS Block A to maintenance bypass for filter and capacitor preventive maintenance while the critical load remains powered.
  • Scope: one of six 2.5 MW UPS blocks, its static and maintenance bypass, Hall 1 A-side only.
  • Roles: lead technician executes; second technician verifies every step; shift engineer authorizes; NOC monitors; OEM field engineer is on call.
  • Redundancy state: A-side on utility-derived bypass during the window, so protection is reduced. B-side confirmed healthy; no B-side work. CAB-approved low-risk window.
  • Prerequisites: B-side UPS normal at full capacity; batteries healthy; bypass source live; EOP for loss of bypass source staged; NOC communications checked; NFPA 70E PPE; freeze conflicting work.
  • Tools: approved single-line, torque tools, meter, breaker racking tool.
  • Steps, each with expected result and second technician's initials: (1) notify NOC and tenants; (2) confirm B-side healthy [full capacity]; (3) verify UPS A load and bypass source [voltage present]; (4) command UPS A to static bypass per OEM sequence [no load disturbance]; (5) close maintenance bypass breaker [closed, confirmed]; (6) open UPS input and output isolators [UPS isolated, load on maintenance bypass]; (7) check critical bus [no drop, NOC concurs]; (8) perform maintenance; (9) reverse to normal [UPS online, bypass open, redundancy restored].
  • Rollback: stop on any failed expected result, revert to the last known-good state under the staged EOP where safe, and escalate.
  • Completion: notify NOC, update log and confirm redundancy restored before releasing the work freeze.

Incidents and denominators. Severity classification and root-cause analysis feed a revised procedure, setting or stocked spare through the CAB. Uptime's Annual Outage Analysis 2025 (6 May 2025) reports power as the leading cause of impactful outages in 2024 (54% of operators), cooling about 13% and network about 12%; across all outages, IT and networking accounted for 53%, associated with change management and misconfiguration. Nearly 40% refers to organizations that had a major human-error outage over three years. 85% refers to those human-error incidents, where staff failed to follow procedures or the procedures themselves were flawed; it does not mean 85% of organizations or of all outages. DPS Telecom's secondary report says "58% of human error-related outages were caused by staff failing to follow established procedures. That's a significant jump up from 48% the year prior" (verify: secondary reporting). "50% of data centers experienced at least one impactful outage over the past three years, down from 53% in 2024," and only about 10% of outages are serious or severe (Uptime Institute, 2025). Per-incident cost is not in the research.

The monthly tenant report. Report power availability against the SLA; temperature and humidity compliance; PUE and WUE against the OPR (≤ 1.4, ≤ 0.2 L/kWh); incidents and root-cause analyses; completed and upcoming maintenance with MOP references and reduced-redundancy windows; generator test logs; capacity and utilisation; and credits owed.

Maintenance and operations certification

Philosophy. OEM intervals set the floor; mature operators layer reliability-centred (RCM) and condition-based maintenance (CBM). Preventive work can itself cause an outage, for example through an incorrect switching action, isolation or concurrent work on the remaining path. Each task is therefore a risk trade governed by a MOP, a confirmed isolation boundary and concurrent maintainability. Tier III topology permits a component to be removed without dropping IT load only when the procedure and remaining capacity are respected.

Electrical. NFPA 70B became a standard with mandatory language in its 2023 edition. Infrared scanning and breaker servicing follow ANSI/NETA MTS-2023; work requires an isolation window under a MOP or appropriate live-work protection under NFPA 70E.

Generators. NFPA 110 Chapter 8 provides weekly emergency-power-system inspections, monthly transfer-switch operation and generator exercising. Under the applicable adopted edition and system classification, the monthly exercise is generally at least 30 minutes with at least 30% of nameplate kW or the manufacturer's minimum exhaust-gas temperature condition, to avoid wet stacking. If building load does not meet the required condition, determine the adopted edition's supplemental load-bank requirements; the report cites 50% for 30 minutes then 75% for one hour for an annual supplemental test under the 2025 edition (verify: 2025-edition figures), and a four-hour test every 36 months for Level 1 systems. These are not unconditional instructions for every generator or jurisdiction. Reconcile the actual NFPA 110 edition and adoption, OEM instructions, load or exhaust-temperature evidence, test classification and the hot-metro air permit's run-hour limits before writing the schedule for the six diesels (Topic 3).

Batteries, UPS, cooling. VRLA requires regular inspection under the applicable NFPA 110 provisions and loses roughly half its life per approximately 8 °C above 25 °C; the reference hall's LFP (Topic 6) uses battery-management monitoring and thermal-runaway precautions under NFPA 855 and OEM guidance. Service UPS filters and capacitors on bypass under §8's MOP. Cooling work includes chiller changeover, loop chemistry, filter differential and CDU cleanliness; refuse uncontrolled hot work beside live pods (§12).

Operations certification. Uptime's TCOS (Tier Certification of Operational Sustainability) is optional for facilities already holding TCCF, rated Bronze, Silver or Gold on a two-year cycle. Its M&O (Management and Operations) Stamp of Approval is optional and independent of Tier status, assessing staffing, maintenance and management with two-year validity. "More than 400 data centers globally have been awarded the M&O Stamp of Approval," and the enhanced program of 7 November 2024 added seven scope areas and a staff-assessment tool (Uptime Institute) (verify: dated). BICSI 009, 2019 and updated 2024, is the open-consensus operations standard complementing BICSI 002, not a requirement to purchase either Uptime operational certificate. The course proposes TCCF around T+43 if pursued, an M&O Stamp in year one and TCOS only as a later option.

Staffing, vendors and the SLA

The shortage. Uptime forecast staff requirements rising "from about 2.0 million full-time employee equivalents in 2019 to nearly 2.3 million in 2025," roughly 300,000 net new (Asia-Pacific 140,000, North America 84,200, EMEA 81,500) (Uptime Institute, Global Data Center Staffing Forecast 2021–2025, 26 January 2020) (verify: dated). In 2025 operations management was the worst skills gap, cited by 39% of operators, and nearly two thirds reported difficulty retaining staff, finding candidates or both (Uptime Institute, 2025). CBRE flags labour as a top-three risk on every major build; about 42% of operators lost key staff to 15–25% raises, notably in Northern Virginia and Phoenix (CBRE, 2025).

Headcount per MW. Automated hyperscale campuses above 100 MW may operate with as few as 20–30 permanent staff per 100 MW; high-density colocation needs more. Four or five shift crews cover 168 hours a week with leave and holidays, setting a staffing floor even at 10 MW. Outsourcing facilities management to CBRE, JLL or similar trades flexibility against direct control of procedure adherence. Remote hands and a remote NOC are real, but lights-out critical operations are not mainstream Tier III colocation practice in 2026. Uptime's 2025 survey finds greater trust in AI for analytics and predictive maintenance than for configuration changes, control or managing staff.

Vendors and spares. Service contracts specify response times, often 15–30 minutes for critical issues, and OEM coverage for gensets (Cummins, Caterpillar), UPS (Schneider, Vertiv, Eaton) and chillers. Response is not necessarily repair. Switchgear, transformers and large UPS modules have lead times of months to years (Topic 3), so stock critical spares on site or contractually guarantee their availability.

The SLA is not the Tier. A colocation SLA may promise 99.999% power availability, about 5.3 minutes of annual unavailability before its contractual measurement rules, plus temperature and humidity limits and service credits, for example 1/30th of monthly recurring charge per breach escalating with duration. The operator funds applicable credits after handover. Five-nines is a contractual service target; Tier III is a concurrent-maintainability topology, not a five-nines promise. The legacy indicative Tier availability figures Uptime removed in 2009 (Tier IV 26 minutes, Tier III 96, Tier I 1,729; Topics 2 and 6) are not tenant SLA terms. The research does not establish this lease's measurement exclusions, so a numerical comparison cannot determine credits.

Decommissioning

The ITAD flow. A decommission ticket and agreed custody plan precede de-installation: serialised inventory → sanitisation or destruction → value recovery or recycling → serialised certificates and downstream disclosures. Record each device and drive serial, transfer, method, result and downstream processor. Server refresh is often 3–5 years, with AI/GPU gear compressing to 18–36 months. Residual value matters because "server residual values fall monthly while a project deliberates" (Powergen). The decommissioning services market was cited at $12.95B in 2026, growing about 7.37% a year (verify: market-research source).

NIST SP 800-88 Rev. 2, September 2025. Published 26 September 2025, with Rev. 1 (2014) withdrawn the same day (NIST) (verify: dated). Rev. 2 retains Clear, Purge and Destroy, but drops media-specific technique tables in favour of device-appropriate selection using IEEE 2883-2022 and NSA/CSS specifications. It calls for a documented sanitisation program, expanded cryptographic-erase controls, verification that the process ran and validation that the required sanitisation result was achieved. A single-pass overwrite can Clear a magnetic HDD; Purge is preferred where feasible. Ordinary overwrite is inadequate for SSD/NVMe flash because wear-levelling and over-provisioning can conceal cells. Flash-compatible Purge uses a supported firmware sanitise command or cryptographic erase only if the whole relevant drive was encrypted, its keys can be securely destroyed, and the result can be verified and validated. Degaussing is for magnetic media, not flash, and is not a Destroy technique under Rev. 2. If the specified result cannot be demonstrated, use suitable physical destruction. Tenant contracts typically call for Rev. 2-compliant methods and serialised evidence.

Custody and downstream accountability. For the course's 50-rack move-out, the tenant identifies and authorises disposition of its devices and drives; the operator controls access, live-facility isolation and documented release at the cage door. The lease and ITAD agreement must name the point at which custody passes to the approved vendor, who may handle unsanitised media and who signs at each hand-off. Use scanned serialised manifests, sealed tamper-evident transport and witnessed on-site destruction or documented transport under the approved custody plan. Reconcile every serial to the final method, certificate and named downstream processor or subcontractor. R2v3 (SERI) addresses environmental practice, custody and downstream management; e-Stewards adds export restrictions; NAID AAA (i-SIGMA) audits security and destruction. "NIST 800-88 establishes what was done, NAID AAA establishes that someone outside your vendor verified it." A primary vendor's certification does not automatically cover subcontractors; the reference lease requires R2v3 and NAID AAA and disclosure of downstream handlers. The cage-door custody boundary must be resolved in writing, not assumed.

Facility end of life. MEP spans (verify: indicative, unsourced): UPS electronics 8–15 years, with VRLA batteries about 4–5 years and capacitors 5–7; chillers 15–20 with a rebuild at 10–15; CRAC and CRAH about 12; generators 25–30; switchgear 15–25; chilled-water piping about 30. Topic 1's mid-life MEP refresh at about 12% of capex lies in the years 8–15 band. Repurposing for density can require CDUs, piping, power and controlled tie-ins beside live space (§12). Space make-good is a separate lease obligation, not proof that drives were sanitised. Environmental work covers refrigerants (EPA Section 608), diesel tanks (SPCC, closure), lithium-ion batteries, SF6 in older switchgear, coolants and e-waste. The lease and ITAD contract allocate their respective work, cost and deliverables.

Worked example: a 50-rack tenant move-out. (1) Tenant issues notice and authorises an inventory of racks and drive serials; operator approves access and the live-neighbour protection plan; tenant, operator and R2v3/NAID AAA vendor agree the cage-door custody hand-off. (2) Before unsupervised trades enter, sanitise under Rev. 2: magnetic HDD by verified Clear or suitable Purge; SSD/NVMe by supported, validated firmware sanitise or cryptographic erase with encryption prerequisites met. Witness destruction if sanitisation cannot be demonstrated or unsanitised media cannot remain under controlled custody. (3) Power down under a MOP, drain and cap CDU manifolds where present, use suitable flame-free joining for tie-ins and protect adjacent live racks. (4) Seal and log each serial and each custody transfer; name downstream processors. (5) Remarket eligible gear promptly and recycle the rest with documented outcomes. (6) Separately make good the cage under the lease and return power and cooling capacity to the pool. (7) Reconcile serials to validation results, certificates and downstream records, update DCIM and release liability only under the agreed contract terms.

The feedback loop and building beside a live hall

Lessons learned. Hyperscalers use reference designs; colocation operators use product platforms. Carry Phase 1's witnessed failed transfers, breaker or control-setting errors, dirty liquid-loop filters, leaks and mis-mapped alarms into a findings register with cause, correction, re-test and operating consequence. Update Hall 1's as-builts, BMS/EPMS points, settings, spares and SOPs, MOPs and EOPs. Feed recurring defects and first-year PUE, WUE and failure history into Phase 2's OPR, BOD, procurement requirements and test scripts, then check that Phases 2–4 actually use the changes. Digital twins updated from records and BMS telemetry can support that loop. The course hands first-year findings to Phase 2's OPR at year one.

Phases 2–4 beside live Hall 1. Construction, tie-ins and IST now sit next to paying tenants. Before work, the operator documents a power and cooling isolation boundary identifying shared breakers, utility paths, generator capacity, chilled-water headers, valves, protected Hall 1 loads, permissible new-phase test load and rollback points. Obtain an approved MOP, confirm remaining capacity, freeze conflicting work and use temporary barriers with vibration, dust and EMI control. Hot work in a live hall is "an extraordinary operational risk and often an unacceptable process" (Data Center Frontier); where technically suitable, press-fit and other flame-free joining reduce that exposure. Prove the new phase rides the failures that can be safely simulated without disturbing Hall 1. The Phase 1 plantwide utility-loss rehearsal cannot simply be repeated against a now-shared live utility or cooling plant: select isolated feeds and bounded scenarios, obtain operator and tenant-protection approvals, and record any scenario that cannot safely be witnessed. The redundancy Hall 1 tenants rely on must not be borrowed to make a new-phase test appear to pass.
