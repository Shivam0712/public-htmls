Appendix: Commissioning, Operations and Decommissioning

The detail behind the Topic 11 chapter: how the five commissioning levels map onto the standards they borrow from, what the Cx plan and load-bank fleet look like for a 10 MW hall, what the work costs and what fails, who the commissioning agent is and why the owner pays it directly, how Uptime's Tier certification sits on top, how acceptance turns tests into payments, the procedures, maintenance and staffing that run the plant, how it is retired, and how Phase 1 feeds Phase 2. Non-obvious claims carry their source in parentheses; "(verify)" marks figures the research flagged as soft, secondary, dated or unsourced. Generators, UPS and CDUs as systems are Topics 5–7; security, fire and monitoring are delivered by Topic 10 and proven here; the utility-failure narrative and the dollar's full path are Topic 12's.

The levels as industry convention

Data-center commissioning is organised into numbered Levels whose verification scope widens from a single component to the whole plant. This is an industry convention, not an ASHRAE scheme: "Rather than describing the process the way ASHRAE Guideline 0 does for general construction, data center projects organise testing into numbered levels... Instead of talking about an OPR and a BOD, the data center industry talks about Levels" (CxPlanner). Five is the common count; some programs start at Level 0 (design review) and run to Level 6 (handover) or 7 (a first-year check-in), so the precision of "Level 5" is partly a shared fiction. What is constant is that the scope widens and the witness share rises.

How ASHRAE maps on. ASHRAE Guideline 0-2019 and ASHRAE/IES Standard 202 supply the wrapping process the levels execute against: the OPR, the BOD, the Cx plan, functional test requirements, the systems manual and training. Standard 202-2013 defines the minimum acceptable process; Guideline 0 the best-practice order of phases. Guideline 0's phases map roughly as design review to Level 0, construction-phase functional testing to Levels 3–4, and occupancy verification to Levels 5–6 (CxPlanner; ASHRAE). Topic 2 introduced the OPR and BOD as documents; here they are the yardstick.

Electrical acceptance. ANSI/NETA ATS-2025, ANSI-approved 20 February 2025, specifies the pre-energization field tests for transformers, switchgear, breakers, protective relays and cables and, new in 2025, Section 7.28 for battery energy storage systems and UPS. It is performed by a NETA Accredited Company with certified technicians and is "the last line of defense before commissioning" (NETA, 2025). For the reference hall it is the gate between Topic 5's energized 138 kV yard at T+38 and the first breaker closed on the 34.5 kV bus.

The Cx plan, load banks and the Phase 1 schedule

The Cx plan and specification. The commissioning plan and the commissioning specification, a division of the construction spec, name what is commissioned, the scripts, the acceptance criteria and who witnesses. Scripts are written to the topology: "the plan and the project specification, not a generic standard, set the actual scenarios, the load, and the acceptance criteria" (Anvilfield). Here that means the six 2.5 MW UPS blocks in two 3M2 groups (Topic 6), the six generators (Topic 5) and the seven CDUs (Topic 7).

Load banks. Levels 4–5 need artificial load because there is no live IT. Resistive banks give pure kW for thermal and generator testing; reactive units add kVAR to test power factor and alternator response; DC units test battery strings and rectifiers; medium-voltage units test transformers and the utility entry. A 10 MW hall needs on the order of 10 MW of capacity, reached in steps. Resistive trailers of up to about 3 MW sit on the pad or at busway and PDU tap-offs; server-emulating 4–20 kW units plug into the actual rack PDUs; liquid-cooled units of about 1 MW go into the CDU loop, because a trailer in the yard cannot prove a 120 kW pod's cooling (research synthesis; PW Consulting). Rental (verify: rental-house sources, 2026): a 3 MW-class trailer about $1,100–2,000 a day or $7,800–14,500 a month (CountBricks, Austin, 2026); a liquid-cooled or high-capacity unit around $18,000 a week against a $400,000-plus purchase price (PW Consulting); an 800 kW unit with fuel and technician about $18,000–25,000 for 48 hours in Northern Virginia; fuel tanks $81–120 a day.

Heat load and the liquid loop. Load banks are distributed across the hall to build heat, and thermal mapping checks containment, fan-wall performance and hot spots against the OPR band. For the AI pods, liquid-loop flushing and CDU testing is a distinct, high-stakes workstream: "flush and fill" is considered inadequate; CDU factory witness, engineered flushing to low-single-digit-micron cleanliness, flow and balance proof and leak integrity are becoming a discipline of their own. Vertiv completed its approximately $1.0 billion acquisition of Houston-based PurgeRite on 4 December 2025, saying it "strengthens its position as a global leader in next-generation thermal chain services for liquid cooling systems" (Vertiv via PR Newswire) (verify: dated). Responsibility boundaries between server OEM, CDU maker and fluid supplier remain unsettled, which is why Topic 7 has the TCS loop commissioned jointly by the mechanical contractor, the CDU vendor and the rack integrator.

Worked example: the Phase 1 schedule. Indicative throughout. Load check from the locked profile: 300 × 8 + 300 × 15 + 200 × 40 + 200 × 120 kW ≈ 38.9 MW, the 200 AI pods being 20% of racks and about 61% of load; about 56 MW at the fence at PUE 1.4. Phase 1 is the first 10 MW in Hall 1.

  1. Levels 1–2, T+30–38, parallel to the build: factory witness for gensets, UPS, switchgear and CDUs (the large power transformer's FAT at T+35 is Topic 3's); receiving as gear lands.
  2. Level 3, about 4 weeks, T+38–39: NETA ATS on all electrical gear, then energize and start each device in Topic 5's order.
  3. Level 4, about 5 weeks, T+39–41: each system's sequence under normal and fault on load banks; generator paralleling, ATS timing, chiller and CDU functional tests, BMS point-to-point.
  4. Level 5, about 3 weeks, T+41–42: full-hall integrated failure testing at design load.
  5. Substantial completion and Hall 1 live about T+42; TCCF, 3–5 days, about T+43.

Field window 12–16 weeks. Load banks: about 10 MW, a mix of 3 MW trailers for the air-cooled zones, rack units on PDUs and about 1 MW liquid units in the CDU loop, staged 0 → 25 → 50 → 75 → 100%. On site for the IST: CxA lead plus 2–3 engineers; NETA crew 3–4; controls integrator 2; OEM field engineers for genset, UPS, chiller and CDU 4–6; design-builder QA/QC and subs 6–10; the owner's operations team 4–6; Uptime 3–5 if TCCF follows; about 25–35 people.

Worked example: pull the plug, pass criteria in brackets.

  1. Load banks at 100% of Hall 1 design load; all systems normal. [Stable at design load.]
  2. Open the utility feed at the main switchboard. [Detected instantly.]
  3. UPS carries the critical load on battery. [Critical bus never drops.]
  4. Generators start, reach voltage and frequency, parallel. [Rated within the design window, typically ≤ 10 s to available power.]
  5. Transfer to generator. [Clean transfer; no bus disturbance.]
  6. Cooling rides through: fans on UPS or generator, the chilled-water thermal mass holding the loop through chiller restart (Topic 7). [Room inside the OPR band throughout.]
  7. Hold on generator at full load, 30 minutes or more. [Stable; no alarms.]
  8. Restore utility; retransfer; UPS recharges. [Clean retransfer; back to normal.]

Pass means the critical load never dropped, the room stayed inside the OPR band and every sequence-of-operations step fired in order. For TCCF, Uptime adds concurrent-maintainability demonstrations: each redundant genset, chiller or loop is removed while N carries the load (Uptime Institute). Topic 12 narrates what happens when this sequence runs for real.

Durations and cost

Weeks per level, 10 MW hall. Levels 1–2 overlap procurement; Level 3 about 3–5 weeks; Level 4 about 4–6; Level 5 about 2–4; a realistic Level 3–5 field window is about 12–16 weeks (research synthesis). A 10-week alternative compresses Level 4 and raises the defect rate; the course keeps the longer window because the building has been finished since T+34 and the four months to T+42 were planned as margin (Topic 3).

Share of construction cost. Commissioning is "often estimated to be 0.5% to 2% of the construction costs" (CxPlanner); on $400–500M ex-IT, broadly $2–10M campus-wide, weighted to the MEP scope Topic 1 put at 75–80% of construction cost.

The load-bank energy line. About 10 MW of load run intermittently for weeks is a real six-figure energy line on top of rental (§2), which is why the IST is staged in quarters rather than held at full load throughout.

Compression and deviations. NETA's 2026 analysis states that "accelerated schedules, dense electrical infrastructure, and heightened performance expectations now place extraordinary pressure on acceptance testing and commissioning teams" (NETA, 2026). Compression pushes teams toward shortcuts, for instance primary-current verification instead of full secondary injection on relays, which must be recorded as deviations rather than silently accepted.

What fails and who pays

Findings. Failures cluster in "the crevices... the interfaces between systems" (Gravel2Gavel): sequence-of-operations errors; generator paralleling; ATS timing against the 10-second criterion; breaker settings and coordination across large populations of low-voltage breakers; BMS points mis-mapped; control loops hunting; leaks in liquid loops. Uptime's stated experience is that "more than 85% of all data center designs are incorrectly executed in construction" (Uptime Institute) (verify: Uptime marketing claim, but the stated reason a witnessed as-built check exists). The research gives no findings breakdown by system, so the chapter draws none.

Who writes, executes, witnesses. The design-builder and subs write the scripts to the specification; the owner's CxA reviews, approves and witnesses, often 100% of Level 4–5 activity; vendors and subs execute; the owner's operations crew is embedded through Levels 4–5 so it learns the plant by testing it.

Re-tests and standby. The contract should place re-test cost and idle load-bank and vendor standby on the party responsible for the failure, or the owner absorbs schedule and rental (research synthesis of contract practice). For the reference hall under design-build with owner-furnished gear: a script error is the design-builder's; a termination or installation fault puts re-test and standby on the design-builder or its sub; an owner-furnished genset that will not parallel is the owner's dispute with the OEM under a warranty that started at delivery (§7), and the design-builder's standby becomes an owner delay; a utility switching restriction and the CxA's extra days are the owner's.

The commissioning agent

Independence. The CxA is engaged by and reports to the owner under a separate agreement, so its findings cannot be suppressed by the entity whose work is judged; Topic 1 made the argument, this is the mechanics. ACG (the AABC Commissioning Group) enforces it structurally: its CxA credential is for professionals who are "independent and work on behalf of building owners," and member firms "cannot have ties to contractors, manufacturers, or other" interested parties (ACG).

The other parties. The owner's engineer protects the owner's technical interest through design intent and submittal review: independent, but design-focused not test-focused. The design-builder's QA/QC checks the builder's own work before offering it for test: a self-check. The engineer of record owns and stamps the design and is conflicted as a witness of it.

Credentials. ACG's CxA, BCxA's CCP (Certified Commissioning Professional), NEBB's certified commissioning professional and ASHRAE's BCxP. Employers routinely accept 8–10 years of field experience in lieu of a degree and value a PE and NFPA 70E. ACG's CxA exam passed 84 of 145 candidates, 58%, in the year to 31 December 2025 (ACG). A design-builder's in-house commissioning is tolerated only for lower-criticality scope or where the owner cannot fund an independent CxA; the reference hall does not use it.

Uptime TCCF versus commissioning, and the TIA-942 alternative

The Tier Certification of Constructed Facility is an independent, witnessed demonstration that the built plant performs to its claimed Tier: a layer on top of commissioning, not a substitute. Uptime does not accept the Cx report as proof; it requires the Level 5 IST complete, then re-runs the demonstrations live. "Uptime Institute consultants observe the Operations team as it performs the different demonstrations, such as interrupting the incoming utility power supply to see that the UPS carries the critical load until the engine generators come on line... and that the cooling system maintains the critical load during the transition," plus "removing redundant capacity components from service and showing that... N engine generators can support the data center critical load or that the redundant chilled water distribution loop can be removed from service while still sufficiently cooling the data center" (Uptime Institute). The verdict is binary and the team also records discrepancies between drawings and installed equipment. The visit runs "3–5 days, generally a little more than a month after construction of critical systems ends" (Uptime Institute). The IST scenarios "often serve as both the commissioning test and the Tier witness" (Anvilfield), and the load banks are reused.

Acceptance mechanics and the handover package

Substantial versus final completion. Substantial completion means the facility is usable for its purpose; it entitles the contractor to the balance less the value of punch-list work, starts the workmanship warranty and stops delay liquidated damages. Final completion means every punch item is closed and final payment is due. Contracts should tie substantial completion to defined milestones, integrated testing, energization, PUE and uptime metrics, not "generic 'substantial completion' language" (Gravel2Gavel). The reference hall's definition names the IST passed, energization, PUE ≤ 1.4 at design load and 10 MW delivered.

Retainage. Typically 5–10% of completed-work value; a common pattern is 5%, half released at substantial completion and half at the end of a 12-month defects liability period (Gravel2Gavel; contract practice). The reference hall uses it.

Liquidated damages. Delay LDs are a daily or weekly sum for missing substantial completion; performance LDs apply to a missed output such as capacity or PUE. The distinctive risk is the LD gap: SLA damages owed to tenants can far exceed construction LDs, and the owner "carries the liability gap between both" (Munich Re). Topic 1's delay-LD range, 0.1–0.5% a day capped at 5–10%, still applies.

The OFE warranty gap. OEM and workmanship warranties differ in term and start date. Long-lead owner-furnished gear delivered at T+30–34 may see its OEM warranty start at delivery and run out around the time the hall is live at T+42, while the design-builder disclaims owner-supplied kit. The research names the gap, not the fix; the course proposes negotiating warranty start at energization, or an extended term priced into the slot, at Topic 3's T+3–6 reservations when the owner has leverage (course proposal, unsourced).

Performance guarantees and close-out. Capacity and PUE ≤ 1.4 are tested in design-load thermal runs and can condition final payment or trigger performance LDs. Close-out ends with the final account, record documents and release of retainage. Topic 12 follows a dollar through this chain.

The handover package. O&M manuals; record ("as-built") drawings; the Cx report; the systems manual (an ASHRAE Guideline 1.x deliverable); operator training hours; increasingly a BIM or digital-twin model. What most often goes missing: corrected as-builts with field changes back-drafted; final BMS and EPMS point lists and sequences; vendor start-up reports; settings and coordination-study files (research synthesis). These are what the first fault needs, which is why the operations team is embedded during Levels 4–5 and keys, credentials and a first set of critical spares change hands at the same table.

Procedures and incidents

SOP, MOP, EOP. BICSI 009 requires all three. A standard operating procedure is the repeatable way routine work is done, rounds, tickets, BMS operation: "The goal of an SOP is consistency." A method of procedure is a step-by-step script for one action that changes the state of a critical component: "The purpose of an MOP is to control actions to ensure the desired outcome" (Uptime Institute). An SOP may contain several MOPs. An emergency operating procedure is the response to an abnormal condition, safe state first, redundancy restored, fault isolated, and must be usable under pressure. A good MOP carries objective, scope, roles, document control, prerequisites and approvals, tools, steps with an expected result each, and rollback. Change-bearing MOPs pass a change advisory board (CAB) and are reviewed on a cycle and after any failed check, incident or system change. A 10 MW Tier III hall carries dozens: UPS to bypass, generator test, chiller changeover, ATS operation, leak response, EPO, fire alarm (Uptime Institute; BICSI 009).

Worked example: MOP-EL-014 Rev 3, "Move UPS Block A to maintenance bypass".

  • ID and dates: MOP-EL-014 Rev 3; effective 2026-01-15; next review 2026-07-15.
  • Objective: transfer UPS Block A to maintenance bypass for filter and capacitor PM while conditioned power stays on the critical load.
  • Scope: UPS Block A, one of the six 2.5 MW blocks, and its static and maintenance bypass; Hall 1 A-side only.
  • Roles: lead technician executes; second technician verifies each step; shift engineer authorizes; NOC monitors; OEM field engineer on call.
  • Redundancy state: A-side load on utility-derived bypass for the window, so redundancy is reduced; B-side confirmed healthy; no B-side work permitted. CAB-approved; low-risk window.
  • Prerequisites: B-side UPS normal at full capacity; batteries healthy; EOP for "loss of bypass source" staged; comms check with the NOC; PPE per NFPA 70E; no concurrent A-side MOPs.
  • Tools: approved single-line, torque tools, meter, breaker racking tool.
  • Steps, each with expected result and verifier's initials: (1) notify NOC and tenants; (2) confirm B-side healthy [B-side at 100%]; (3) verify UPS A load and bypass source live [voltage present]; (4) command UPS A to bypass per OEM sequence [load on static bypass, no disturbance]; (5) rack and close the maintenance bypass breaker [closed, confirmed]; (6) open UPS input and output isolators [UPS isolated, load on maintenance bypass]; (7) confirm critical bus stable [no drop; NOC concurs]; (8) perform the PM; (9) reverse to normal [UPS online, bypass open, redundancy restored].
  • Rollback: at any failed check, stop and revert to last known-good per the embedded EOP; escalate to shift engineer and vendor.
  • Completion: NOC notified; log updated; redundancy confirmed restored.

Incidents. Severity levels and root-cause analysis close at the CAB with a revised procedure, a changed setting or a stocked spare. The reference data is Uptime's Annual Outage Analysis 2025 (6 May 2025). For 2024: power the leading cause of impactful outages (54% of operators), cooling about 13%, network about 12%; across all outages, IT and networking 53%, attributed to change management and misconfiguration; "outages from IT and networking issues increased in 2024, totaling 23% of impactful outages." "Nearly 40% of organizations have suffered a major outage caused by human error over the past three years. Of these incidents, 85% stem from staff failing to follow procedures or from flaws in the processes and procedures themselves." DPS Telecom's secondary report: "58% of human error-related outages were caused by staff failing to follow established procedures. That's a significant jump up from 48% the year prior" (verify: secondary reporting). "50% of data centers experienced at least one impactful outage over the past three years, down from 53% in 2024," and only about 10% of outages are serious or severe (Uptime Institute, 2025). Per-incident cost is not in the research.

The monthly tenant report. Power availability against the SLA (target and actual minutes); temperature and humidity band compliance; PUE and WUE actuals against the OPR (≤ 1.4, ≤ 0.2 L/kWh); incidents and RCAs; completed and upcoming maintenance with MOP references and redundancy-reduced windows; the generator test log; capacity and utilisation; SLA credits owed.

Maintenance and operations certification

Philosophy. OEM intervals set the floor; mature operators layer reliability-centred (RCM) and condition-based maintenance (CBM). Maintenance is itself an outage cause, through human error during work, so every task is a risk trade governed by a MOP and concurrent maintainability. Tier III means any component can be removed without dropping IT load, but only if the MOP, the isolation and the redundancy are respected; this is where Topic 6's design meets discipline.

Electrical. NFPA 70B became a standard with mandatory language in its 2023 edition; electrical maintenance is no longer merely recommended practice. Infrared scanning and breaker servicing follow ANSI/NETA MTS-2023; work needs an outage window under a MOP or live-work PPE under NFPA 70E.

Generators. NFPA 110 Chapter 8: weekly inspection; a monthly run of at least 30 minutes at at least 30% of nameplate (or the manufacturer's minimum exhaust temperature) to avoid wet stacking; monthly ATS operation; an annual supplemental load-bank test where building load does not reach 30%, 50% for 30 minutes then 75% for 1 hour per the 2025 edition (verify: 2025-edition figures); a 4-hour test every 36 months for Level 1 systems. The hot-metro catch is the air permit's run-hour cap (Topic 3), which limits how much the six diesels can run for testing and makes the schedule a permit question.

Batteries. VRLA needs weekly electrolyte and voltage checks per NFPA 110 and loses roughly half its life per ~8 °C above 25 °C. The reference hall's LFP (Topic 6) is watched by its battery management system with thermal-runaway detection under NFPA 855 and OEM guidance; lithium-ion is increasingly the UPS chemistry per BICSI 002-2019. UPS filters and capacitors are serviced on bypass under MOP (§8). Cooling work covers chiller changeover, loop chemistry, filter differential and CDU cleanliness; hot work beside live pods is refused (§12).

Operations certification. Uptime's TCOS (Tier Certification of Operational Sustainability) is for TCCF holders, rated Bronze, Silver or Gold, on management, operations and building characteristics, two-year cycle. The M&O (Management and Operations) Stamp of Approval is independent of Tier, covers staffing and organisation, maintenance and operations, management and planning, two-year validity; "More than 400 data centers globally have been awarded the M&O Stamp of Approval," and the enhanced program of 7 November 2024 added seven scope areas and a staff-assessment tool (Uptime Institute) (verify: dated). BICSI 009, 2019 and updated 2024, is the open-consensus operations standard complementing BICSI 002, covering procedures, staffing, maintenance and assessment. The course proposes TCCF at T+43 as Topic 2 locked, the M&O Stamp in year one, TCOS as a later option.

Staffing, vendors and the SLA

The shortage. Uptime forecast staff requirements rising "from about 2.0 million full-time employee equivalents in 2019 to nearly 2.3 million in 2025," roughly 300,000 net new (Asia-Pacific 140,000, North America 84,200, EMEA 81,500) (Uptime Institute, Global Data Center Staffing Forecast 2021–2025, 26 January 2020) (verify: dated). In 2025 operations management was the worst skills gap, cited by 39% of operators, and nearly two thirds report difficulty retaining staff, finding candidates, or both (Uptime Institute, 2025). CBRE flags labour as a top-three risk on every major build; about 42% of operators lost key staff to 15–25% raises, notably in Northern Virginia and Phoenix (CBRE, 2025).

Headcount per MW. Automated hyperscale campuses above 100 MW run with as few as 20–30 permanent staff per 100 MW; enterprise and high-density colocation need substantially more. A 24/7 site needs 4–5 shift crews to cover 168 hours with holidays and leave, and that floor sets the count at 10 MW. Many operators outsource facilities management to CBRE, JLL and similar, trading cost and flexibility against direct control of procedure adherence. Remote hands and a remote NOC are real; lights-out critical operations are not mainstream Tier III in 2026, and Uptime's 2025 survey finds operators trust AI for sensor analytics and predictive maintenance but not for configuration changes, control or managing staff.

Vendors and spares. Service contracts carry response times, often 15–30 minutes for critical issues, with OEM coverage for gensets (Cummins, Caterpillar), UPS (Schneider, Vertiv, Eaton) and chillers. Switchgear, transformers and large UPS modules have lead times of months to years (Topic 3), so critical spares are stocked on site or contractually guaranteed, and the first set arrives at handover.

The SLA and credits. A colocation SLA typically promises power availability (often 99.999%, under about 5 minutes a year), a temperature and humidity band (commonly ASHRAE allowable, 18–27 °C) and service credits as the remedy, for example 1/30th of the monthly recurring charge per breach, escalating with duration. Once accepted, the operator carries operational risk and credits flow to tenants from its opex, which makes procedure discipline a financial lever. The chapter's scale sets 99.999% (about 5.3 minutes a year) against the legacy indicative Tier figures Uptime removed in 2009 (Tier IV 26 minutes, Tier III 96, Tier I 1,729; Topics 2 and 6). The comparison is definitional; the research does not cover SLA measurement exclusions, so the eighteen-fold gap is a shape, not a contract term.

Decommissioning

The ITAD flow. Decommission ticket → de-install → serialised inventory → sanitisation or destruction → value recovery or recycling → certificate of destruction and downstream disclosures, every outcome recorded against the serial. Refresh cycles: servers 3–5 years, AI and GPU gear compressing to 18–36 months; storage and network similar or longer. Residual value matters because "server residual values fall monthly while a project deliberates" (Powergen). The decommissioning services market was cited at $12.95B in 2026, growing about 7.37% a year (verify: market-research source).

NIST SP 800-88 Rev. 2. Published 26 September 2025, with Rev. 1 (2014) withdrawn the same day (NIST) (verify: dated). Rev. 2 keeps Clear, Purge and Destroy but removes the media-specific technique tables, deferring to IEEE 2883-2022 and NSA/CSS specifications; it is built around a documented sanitisation program, expands cryptographic-erase guidance, and separates verification (the process ran) from validation (nothing is recoverable). It confirms a single-pass overwrite suffices to Clear a hard disk, prefers Purge over Clear where possible, and states that overwriting SSDs is inadequate because wear-levelling and over-provisioning hide blocks, so flash Purge relies on cryptographic erase or firmware sanitise. Degaussing is not a Destroy technique under Rev. 2 and does nothing to flash. Tenant contracts typically require Rev. 2-compliant sanitisation plus a serialised certificate.

R2v3 (SERI) covers environmental practice, custody and downstream vendor management; e-Stewards adds export bans; NAID AAA (i-SIGMA) runs unannounced audits of security and destruction efficacy. "NIST 800-88 establishes what was done, NAID AAA establishes that someone outside your vendor verified it." Most ITAD breaches occur at downstream vendors and subcontractors; a certification held by the vendor "doesn't extend to subcontractor operations," which is why the reference hall's lease names R2v3 and NAID AAA and requires subcontractors to be disclosed. Where colocation custody ends and ITAD custody begins at the cage door is an unresolved exposure.

Facility end of life. MEP spans (verify: indicative, unsourced): UPS electronics 8–15 years, batteries far sooner (VRLA 4–5, capacitors 5–7); chillers 15–20 with a rebuild at 10–15; CRAC and CRAH about 12; generators 25–30; switchgear 15–25; chilled-water piping about 30. Topic 1's mid-life MEP refresh at about 12% of capex is the years 8–15 band. Repurposing for density is the dominant driver: converting air halls for liquid-cooled pods means new CDUs, piping and power, and hot taps beside live white space (§12). Make-good obligations return leased space to a defined condition. Environmental obligations cover refrigerants (EPA Section 608), diesel tanks (SPCC, closure), lithium-ion batteries, SF6 in older switchgear, coolants and e-waste. Who pays is set in the lease; the ITAD contract allocates liability, requires certifications and defines the deliverables.

Worked example: a 50-rack tenant move-out.

  1. Notice and scope: decommission ticket; 50 racks inventoried to serial; ITAD vendor with R2v3 and NAID AAA engaged; custody plan agreed.
  2. Data first: sanitise per Rev. 2, SSD and NVMe by cryptographic erase or firmware sanitise, HDD by single-pass overwrite or Purge; witnessed on-site shred for anything leaving unsanitised; verification and validation; serialised certificates. Nobody else walks the space before this step.
  3. De-install: power down per MOP; for liquid racks, drain and cap CDU manifolds under MOP, flame-free where tie-ins are needed; remove cabling; protect adjacent live racks.
  4. Custody and transport: sealed totes, serialised manifests, downstream disclosures.
  5. Value recovery: remarket viable gear promptly; recycle the rest through certified partners; nothing landfilled.
  6. Make-good: restore the cage per the lease; return power and cooling capacity to the campus pool for re-lease or denser repurposing.
  7. Close-out: certificates, asset-disposition report and updated DCIM records to the tenant; liability released per the ITAD contract.

The feedback loop and building beside a live hall

Lessons learned. Hyperscalers run reference designs; colocation operators run product platforms. Operations feeds the next phase's OPR and BOD through Cx findings databases (recurring defects become design changes), operating data (PUE and WUE actuals, failure history) and digital twins updated from as-builts and BMS telemetry. The point of a phased campus is that Phase 1's findings and first-year experience harden Phases 2–4, which is why the chapter's timeline hands the first-year findings to Phase 2's OPR at year one.

Phases 2–4 beside a live Hall 1. Construction and IST happen next to operating white space under isolation of shared systems, temporary barriers, vibration, dust and EMI control, and MOP-governed tie-ins. Hot work in a live hall is "an extraordinary operational risk and often an unacceptable process" (Data Center Frontier), pushing operators to press-fit and flame-free joining for liquid loops. The new phase's IST must prove its load rides failures without disturbing the live phase; shared utility, generator plant and the chilled-water headers Topics 5 and 7 cast for 40 MW on day one make the pull-the-plug test far more constrained than on a greenfield, and the redundancy Hall 1's tenants pay for is what the new phase's test must never borrow.
