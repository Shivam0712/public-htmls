Topic 7 — Cooling and Thermal Management for a New-Build Data Center (2025–2026)

The Desktop Research report for Topic 7, run 2026-09-25 from briefs/ and published as delivered: no editing, no lint. The chapter and appendix were distilled from it; where they and this report disagree, the chapter is the course's position.

Reference facility (locked, all worked numbers "indicative"): 1,000-rack Phoenix colocation campus, ~40 MW IT, PUE ≤ 1.4 (~56 MW at the fence), Tier III concurrently maintainable, design-build with owner-furnished long-lead gear, built in ~10 MW phases, near-zero-water heat rejection (WUE ≤ 0.2 L/kWh). Density mix: 300 racks @ 8 kW, 300 @ 15 kW, 200 @ 40 kW, 200 @ 120 kW = 38,900 kW ≈ 39 MW; the 120 kW AI pods are 20% of racks and 61% of load.

TL;DR

  • Split the plant, not just the hall. The winning 2026 Phoenix design runs the 61% of load in the 120 kW AI pods on a warm-water (≈32–45 °C) direct-to-chip loop rejected by dry coolers with near-zero compressor hours, and reserves mechanical chillers for the ~39% air-cooled and trim load. That is how a ≤ 1.4 PUE survives a 45 °C dry bulb and a near-zero-water mandate at once.
  • Liquid cooling is now the mainstream path, not an exotic. NVIDIA GB200 NVL72 (~120–132 kW) and GB300 NVL72 (~132–142 kW) racks mandate direct-to-chip liquid; 70–80% of pod heat goes to liquid and 20–30% stays in the room as a residual air load the fan walls must still carry. ASHRAE's W-classes (W17–W45, W+) and the H1 air class are the binding vocabulary.
  • The schedule risk is the CDU and the switchgear, not the chiller. CDUs run 26–52 weeks — and per Dell'Oro Group the Data Center Direct Liquid Cooling (DLC) market surged 156% year-over-year in 2Q 2025 (projected to near $6 billion by 2029) — while data-center-grade medium-voltage switchgear runs 44–80+ weeks; the classic failure mode is "the rack arrives before its CDU."

1. Heat and airflow fundamentals

1.1 The identity the chapter opens on

Every watt of IT power becomes a watt of heat: IT kW in = heat kW out. The unit conversions that anchor all sizing: 1 kW = 3,412 BTU/h = 0.284 tons of cooling. A 15 kW rack therefore rejects 51,180 BTU/h ≈ 4.26 tons.

Airflow is governed by the sensible-heat relation ΔT = 3.1 × W ÷ CFM at sea level (Upsite Technologies), i.e. CFM = W ÷ (0.318 × ΔT°F). This yields the field rules of thumb:

  1. 20 °F (11 K) rise → ~157 CFM/kW (the classic convention).
  2. 25 °F rise → ~125 CFM/kW.
  3. 30 °F rise → ~105 CFM/kW (Upsite Technologies).

Worked (indicative): a 15 kW rack needs 15 × 157 = 2,355 CFM at 20 °F, 1,875 CFM at 25 °F, or 1,575 CFM at 30 °F. Widening ΔT from 20 to 30 °F cuts required airflow by ~33% — the entire economic case for containment. A best-in-class raised-floor tile delivers ~1,900 CFM (ASHRAE 2021), so a single tile cannot feed a 15 kW rack at 20 °F — one reason dense racks abandon raised floor.

Fan power follows the cube law: power ∝ speed³. Dropping fan speed from 100% to 80% cuts fan power ~50% (SubZero Engineering, per fan affinity laws). Static pressure must be just enough to overcome the airflow path; over-pressurising drives bypass (cold air short-circuiting to the return) and starving drives recirculation (hot exhaust re-entering intakes) — the two airflow faults containment exists to kill.

1.2 ASHRAE TC 9.9 Thermal Guidelines (5th ed., 2021) and the W-classes

The 5th edition of Thermal Guidelines for Data Processing Environments (March 2021) is the current authoritative release. Air classes: A1–A4, with a recommended envelope of 18–27 °C at the server inlet; A4 allowable reaches 45 °C (ASHRAE 2021). The edition added class H1 for high-density equipment — allowable 15–25 °C, recommended 18–22 °C — meant to be segregated into a controlled zone.

The liquid-cooling classes were renamed to their upper supply-water temperature, all carrying a 2 °C lower limit: W17 (was W1), W27 (W2), W32 (W3), W40 (new), W45 (W4), and W+ (>45 °C, was W5) (ASHRAE, Emergence and Expansion of Liquid Cooling, 2021). W17/W27 still need chillers; W32 runs chiller-free in most climates; W40/W45/W+ reject heat on cooling towers or dry coolers year-round, eliminating the chiller — "the energy prize." The 5th edition also tightened compliance: a W-class now requires full, unthrottled operation across the entire class temperature range.

Humidity control moved from relative humidity to dew point because RH at a fixed value means different absolute moisture at different temperatures; dew-point control targets the actual ESD (too dry) and hygroscopic-dust/corrosion (too wet) risks directly. The recommended dew-point band is roughly −9 °C to 15 °C (ASHRAE 2016/2021).

Phoenix against the envelope: Phoenix summer dry bulb reaches ~45 °C — at or above the A4 ceiling — so direct free-air economization is unavailable at peak; but Phoenix is dry, so wet-bulb rarely exceeds the low-to-mid 20s °C, which keeps evaporative and dry-cooler approaches viable for the warm-water loop. Air-side or water-side economization is available roughly 40% of the year (Phoenix modeling studies).

1.3 Delivery and risk

The thermal criteria are written into the Basis of Design (BOD) by the owner's design engineer (Topic 2 owns OPR/BOD). The chosen ASHRAE class binds the tenant: an A1/H1 hall commits the operator to a tighter inlet band (and higher energy) than an A2/A3 hall, and colocation SLAs historically excluded water at the rack — a clause that AI density is now forcing open.

2. Air-side systems

2.1 CRAC vs CRAH vs in-row vs fan wall

See Appendix Table A1 for the full comparison. In brief:

  • CRAC (DX): self-contained compressor + refrigerant per unit; practical for small/legacy or chiller-less rooms; least efficient at hyperscale because every unit carries its own refrigeration cycle.
  • CRAH (chilled water): coil + fan fed from a central chilled-water plant; efficient, modular, the workhorse of moderate-density raised-floor halls.
  • In-row: cooling placed between racks, close-coupled to the load; good for spot high density (20–60 kW rows).
  • Fan wall: a floor-to-ceiling modular array of EC plug fans + chilled-water coils forming a mechanical-gallery "air backbone," each unit rated to ~800 kW (Munters).

Why the fan wall won the hyperscale hall: it scales cleanly with 5–10 MW+ halls, uses low-static horizontal airflow, offers fan-level N+1 redundancy, and — with EC fans at ~100% penetration in new Tier III/IV builds since 2024 — reaches the low fan energy a PUE ≤ 1.3 requires. LONGWELL reports EC fan walls cutting CRAH fan energy by 38%. The room-air slice still matters: even when liquid takes the largest share, the absolute air load grows with density (LG/IEA), so a perfectly good liquid loop cannot rescue an undersized air side.

2.2 Containment as the default

Containment is mandatory practice. Hot-aisle containment (HACS) is preferred over cold-aisle (CACS) for new builds: a Schneider Electric white paper (SPD_DBOY-7EDLE8, Dunlap/Niemann) found the choice of hot-aisle over cold-aisle containment can save 43% in annual cooling system energy cost, corresponding to a 15% reduction in annualized PUE — while holding the uncontained data center area to 24 °C/75 °F. Measured gains: higher supply temperatures, lower fan energy (cube law), doubled effective cooling capacity, and elimination of hot spots (SubZero: top-to-bottom rack ΔT fell from >10 °F to 1 °F). Discipline is the price — blanking panels, sealed cable cutouts, and no open rack gaps, or bypass/recirculation returns instantly. California Title 24 codifies this: it requires air/water economizers capable of 100% of load, variable-speed fan controls, and containment in large high-density air-cooled rooms; the 2025 standards take effect January 1, 2026.

2.3 Economization and what "near-zero-water" costs in Phoenix

Modes, ranked by climate fit:

  1. Air-side (direct outside air): cheapest where air is clean and mild; needs filtration and smoke handling; poor at a 45 °C peak.
  2. Indirect evaporative / adiabatic: heat exchanger keeps outside air out of the hall; wins in dry climates like Phoenix; uses water.
  3. Water-side economizer: plate-and-frame HX, condenser water to chilled water, triggered on wet-bulb.
  4. Adiabatic dry coolers: dry most of the year, water-assisted spray only on peak days.

Phoenix modeling (ScienceDirect, hot-arid study) gives roughly 3,057 h/yr of air-side and ~1,077 h/yr of water-side economizer availability, ~40% of the year combined. "Near-zero-water" implies dry rejection, which at a 45 °C dry bulb has a poor approach and drives compressor hours up for the air-cooled load — a design PUE of 1.4 is defensible only because warm-water liquid loops (§4.3) recover it: the 61% of load on 32–45 °C facility water rejects on dry coolers with near-zero compressor hours, dragging the blended PUE back under target. Reclaimed water for peak-day adiabatic assist is the safety valve; blowdown to sewer (fixed in Topic 3).

2.4 Delivery and risk

Fan walls and CRAHs are typically contractor-furnished (mechanical scope), though hyperscale owners increasingly make large air handlers owner-furnished (OFE) to lock factory slots. The containment fit-out boundary with the tenant must be explicit (who supplies doors/roofs/blanking), and blanking-panel discipline is a commissioning and operations hand-off (Topic 11).

3. The chilled-water plant

3.1 Chiller types and the refrigerant transition

See Appendix Table A2. Air-cooled chillers avoid cooling-tower water (aligned with near-zero-water) but are less efficient in high ambient; water-cooled centrifugals are the most efficient at design but consume tower water. Compressor types run screw → centrifugal → magnetic-bearing oil-free; the last (Danfoss Turbocor, York YZ/YMC²) eliminates lubricating oil, excels at part load and low lift, and enables warm-water setpoints. Johnson Controls' York YVAM/YDAM air-cooled magnetic-bearing centrifugals deliver 3.5 MW per unit on low-GWP R-1234ze, fit a 53 ft flatbed, restart to full load in ~3 minutes, and support 45 °C warm-water cooling — purpose-built for exactly this facility's AI loop. LG launched a 480–600 RT air-cooled magnetic-bearing centrifugal (Sept 2026) with refrigerant free-cooling claimed ~30% below hydronic free-cooling.

Refrigerant transition: the AIM Act (US HFC phase-down) and EU F-gas rules are pushing chillers to A2L and low-GWP fluids — R-1234ze, R-513A, R-515B (Vertiv redesigned all lines for low-GWP by early 2025; ACHR News). R-515B is A1 (non-flammable), simplifying code compliance vs mildly flammable A2Ls. A near-zero-water site chooses air-cooled magnetic-bearing chillers on low-GWP refrigerant plus hybrid dry coolers.

3.2 Supply temperatures and what they buy

The industry shift is from 7 °C chilled water to 18–30 °C for air loops and 30–45 °C facility water for liquid loops. Every degree warmer buys economizer hours and lets the chiller trip off. On W40/W45 water a Phoenix site runs compressor-hours near zero for the liquid load. The trade-off (ASHRAE 2021): warmer coolant carries less heat per unit flow, so dense racks need bigger pumps/heat exchangers or fewer blades — and rising chip power with falling case temperatures may regress supportable water back toward W32/W27 over time.

3.3 Plant sizing, redundancy, parasitics, and ride-through

Sizing rules of thumb: heat to reject = IT load + in-hall electrical losses (UPS/PDU/lighting, ~6–8% of IT) + fan/pump heat. N+1 chillers and pumps for each 10 MW hall and at the 40 MW campus; headers and isolation valving arranged for concurrent maintainability (Tier III). Pipe-sizing rule of thumb keeps velocity ~1.5–3 m/s to bound pressure drop and erosion. The plant's own parasitic (electrical) load — pumps, chiller compressors, dry-cooler fans — is the mechanical load component that PUE and ASHRAE 90.4's annualized MLC measure.

Thermal storage for ride-through: on utility loss, generators start in seconds but chillers take ~5–15 minutes to restart (USPTO transient-cooling patents); the gap where active cooling is unavailable is ~10–45 seconds (Bendel). A stratified chilled-water thermal-storage tank is the thermal flywheel that bridges it. This matters acutely at 120 kW: rack thermal time constants are seconds, so at high density inlet temperatures rise measurably in under a minute of lost airflow — the tank must be sized in minutes (typically enough to cover generator start + chiller restart, ~5–15 min).

3.4 Delivery and risk

Chillers are owner-furnished (OFE) long-lead items — 20–40 weeks in 2025–26 (Appendix Table A4); piping is contractor scope. Who owns the ASHRAE 90.4 compliance calculation must be named in the BOD — normally the mechanical engineer of record; 90.4-2022 uses part-load-aware MLC/ELC ratios checked against climate-zone maxima, and its addendum g gives credit for recovered heat.

4. Liquid cooling as the mainstream path for high density

4.1 The ladder of methods

See Appendix Table A1. In ascending density and share of heat to liquid:

  1. Rear-door heat exchanger (RDHx): liquid coil on the rack rear; passive (server fans) or active (EC fans); 20–80 kW/rack (nVent RDHX Pro: 68 kW at 14 °C water, 44 kW at 24 °C; Motivair ChilledDoor: up to 75 kW, 100% heat removal). Captures rack sensible heat to water; the room sees near-neutral return.
  2. Direct-to-chip (DTC) cold plates, single-phase (water-glycol): the 2026 mainstream; 70–80% of rack heat to liquid, 20–30% residual air.
  3. DTC two-phase (dielectric): boils fluid at the plate; ~80% capture; under a PFAS regulatory cloud (§8).
  4. Single-phase immersion: whole board in dielectric oil; ~100% heat to liquid, PUE 1.03–1.10, densities to 100 kW+ (Vertiv cites potential to ~900 kW/rack).
  5. Two-phase immersion: boiling dielectric; PUE ~1.02–1.05; PFAS-constrained.

At a 120 kW rack the split is 70–80% liquid / 20–30% air. The residual 24–36 kW of air load per pod is what keeps fan walls in the AI hall — it does not vanish.

4.2 The loops, in order

  1. Chip → cold plate (Topic 8's side, up to the QD).
  2. Rack manifold → quick disconnects (QDs) — blind-mate UQD couplings (NVIDIA GB200 uses Blindmate UQD04).
  3. CDU (in-rack, in-row, or facility-scale) — the heat exchanger that hydraulically isolates the Technology Cooling System (TCS, high-purity server loop) from the Facility Water System (FWS), with variable-speed pumps, filtration, and dew-point-aware control to prevent condensation (Vertiv, Stulz, Specifying Engineer). Ratings span 22 kW liquid-to-air (Eaton 10U) to 1 MW (LiquidStack, ~1.25 LPM/kW at 4 °C approach) and 5 MW facility distribution units (DCX FDU, ~4 °C approach, N+1 four-pump). HPE ships a 1.3 MW in-row CDU serving up to 8 racks.
  4. CDU → facility water → dry cooler/chiller.

Approach temperatures: commercial CDUs achieve ~3–4 °C TCS-to-FWS approach (Eaton ROL4000: 2 MW at 3 °C; DCX FDU: 5 MW at ~4 °C). Flow: the CDU rule of thumb is ~1.15–1.5 LPM/kW (LiquidStack). Fluids: 25–30% water-glycol (PG25) is standard; a wetted-materials compatibility list, leak detection with automated isolation valves, and clean-flush at commissioning are mandatory (ASHRAE Liquid Cooling Guidelines; OCP ACS). NVIDIA specifies ≤25 µS/cm conductivity and <50 µm particulate for TCS quality.

4.3 Facility water at 30–45 °C — warm loop vs cool loop

Running FWS at 30–45 °C (W40/W45) takes compressor hours near zero but demands a server rated to that W-class. The tension: a warm loop maximizes efficiency and heat-reuse potential; a cool loop preserves silicon headroom. GPU inlet limits set the floor.

NVIDIA rack requirements (indicative): - GB200 NVL72: ~120 kW/rack (~1,200 W/GPU × 72); air cooling not viable; coolant flow ~2 L/s (~120 LPM) into the cabinet (NVIDIA via Fibermall); typical facility supply 18–25 °C, return 40–50 °C in third-party guides; cold plates rated to 1,200 W at 30–45 °C inlet. - GB300 NVL72 (Blackwell Ultra, GTC March 2025): ~132–142 kW/rack (~1,400 W/GPU); Lenovo's product guide lists facility water 2–50 °C (ASHRAE W45); NVIDIA's stated rack-scale design point is 45 °C warm-water supply; flow rises steeply toward the hot end (Lenovo curve: ~177 LPM/rack at 45 °C); Microsoft Azure cites ~136 kW of heat per rack; ~90% of heat to liquid.

4.4 How a 120 kW AI pod is cooled end to end (indicative, reference facility)

BOUNDARY: Topic 7 owns cold-plate-outlet → outside air, including the CDU and FWS. Topic 8 owns the chip/server up to the QD.

  1. Rack QDs → rack manifold collect ~120 kW into the TCS at, say, ~40 °C return / ~32 °C supply (8 °C rack ΔT), flow ~150–180 LPM (~1.25–1.5 LPM/kW).
  2. TCS → in-row/facility CDU: heat crosses the HX at ~3–4 °C approach into the FWS.
  3. FWS supply ~36–45 °C (W40/W45) → adiabatic dry coolers on the roof; in Phoenix these run dry most of the year, water-assisted on peak days, compressor-free.
  4. Residual ~24–36 kW/pod of air load → fan-wall chilled water (§2.1), the only part of the AI pod that touches a chiller.

4.5 Delivery and risk

CDUs are variously OFE, contractor-furnished (CFE), or IT-vendor-furnished — this must be pinned in the BOD because it sets who owns the QD interface and the CDU commissioning. Interface responsibility at the quick disconnect and the CDU is the classic gray zone. The TCS loop is commissioned jointly by the mechanical contractor, the CDU vendor, and the IT/rack integrator (names of parties only; levels in Topic 11). CDU and manifold lead times are the schedule long pole (§6.3, Table A4). Overhead liquid pipe competes with cable pathways (Topic 9 — named conflict only).

5. Water and heat reuse

5.1 WUE, defined once

Water Usage Effectiveness (WUE), The Green Grid (WP#35, 2011): WUE = annual site water use (L) ÷ IT energy (kWh), units L/kWh. Site WUE counts water at the facility (cooling towers, evaporative, humidification); source WUE adds the water embedded in generating the electricity (via the Energy Water Intensity Factor). Consumptive use is water evaporated/lost; non-consumptive is withdrawn and returned. WUE gives no credit for reclaimed water or beneficial blowdown reuse (DCD).

Typical values (Appendix, and see below): the LBNL 2024 US Data Center Energy Usage Report finds industry-wide average site WUE held just over 0.36 L/kWh through 2023 (the widely quoted 1.8–1.9 L/kWh "industry average" traces to 2025 Data Center Knowledge / EESI guides, not LBNL's WUE data); evaporative cooling runs to 2.5 L/kWh; air/dry-cooled approaches ~0 L/kWh. Equinix's 2025 Sustainability Report gives a global portfolio WUE of 0.91 L/kWh (1.41 for evaporative-cooling sites), alongside a 1.37 global PUE. The reference facility's OPR target of ≤ 0.2 L/kWh — "less than a cup per kWh" — sits well below industry medians and is achievable only with dry/near-zero-water rejection.

5.2 Zero-water designs in 2025–26

Microsoft's next-generation design (announced Dec 2024, effective for all designs from August 2024): closed-loop, chip-level cooling that "consumes zero water for cooling," recycling water filled once at construction. As Steve Solomon (VP, Datacenter Infrastructure Engineering) put it on the Microsoft Cloud Blog (Dec 9, 2024): "While water is still used for administrative purposes like restrooms and kitchens, this design will avoid the need for more than 125 million liters of water per year per datacenter." Microsoft's WUE fell from 0.49 L/kWh (2021) to 0.30 (2024), targeting near zero; pilots are in Phoenix, AZ and Mt. Pleasant, WI, coming online from late 2027. The cost is paid in PUE and capex: dropping evaporative cooling raises energy use (Microsoft notes higher operating temperatures let more-efficient chillers offset part of it) and adds heat-exchanger/dry-cooler capital. Vantage's Phoenix campus similarly advertises near-zero WUE via a closed-loop air-cooled-chiller design with air-side economization.

5.3 Heat reuse

Reuse plausibility is set by return temperature. Air-cooled halls exhaust ~35–45 °C air — too low-grade to be useful without heat pumps. Liquid loops return 40–65 °C water, which is why liquid makes reuse plausible and air does not. Meta's Odense (Denmark) data center captures server heat into the district-heating network run by Fjernvarme Fyn; ammonia heat pumps lift ~40 °C water to 70–75 °C; Ramboll designed a ~45 MW heat system. Recovery figures rose with expansion — Meta's own figures cite ~165,000 MWh/yr warming ~11,000 households (earlier phase: 100,000 MWh, ~6,900 homes). LRZ SuperMUC-NG (Germany) runs direct 40–45 °C warm water into adsorption chillers, partial cooling PUE < 0.07 (ASHRAE 2021).

Regulation: the EU Energy Efficiency Directive (Directive (EU) 2023/1791, Art. 12) requires data centers ≥500 kW to report energy, WUE, ERF and waste-heat data annually to a European database, and centers >1 MW to run a cost-benefit analysis of waste-heat recovery and implement it unless infeasible. Germany's Energieeffizienzgesetz (EnEfG, in force Nov 2023) goes further with hard Energy Reuse Factor quotas: 10% for centers starting operation on/after 1 July 2026, 15% from 1 July 2027, 20% from 1 July 2028, plus PUE ≤ 1.2 for new builds.

Why Phoenix has no viable heat customer: a hot arid climate has essentially no district-heating demand and no nearby heat sink that wants 45–65 °C water; heat cannot be transported far without loss. Reuse is therefore a non-starter here — the heat is rejected to air. (This is why the EU/German mandates do not translate to Arizona.)

5.4 Delivery and risk

WUE and any heat-reuse commitments are OPR terms and often permit conditions; missing them exposes the owner to permit and contractual liability. Water sourcing/rights/NPDES are Topic 3 (referenced only).

6. How it assembles: the reference hall's Phase 1 cooling (worked example)

6.1 Plant sizing for Phase 1 (10 MW IT in Hall 1) and growth to 40 MW

Heat load (indicative): 10 MW IT + ~6–8% in-hall electrical losses ≈ ~10.7 MW to reject = ~3,040 tons (10,700 × 0.284). Assume Phase 1 mirrors the campus mix: ~6 MW on DTC liquid (warm-water, dry-cooled) and ~4 MW air + trim (chilled water).

  • Liquid side (~6 MW): FWS 36–45 °C (W40/W45) → adiabatic dry coolers, N+1; near-zero compressor hours; served by ~6 × 1 MW CDUs (N+1) or facility-scale CDUs.
  • Air side (~4 MW + trim): air-cooled magnetic-bearing chillers, 3.5 MW class (York YDAM), N+1 — e.g. 2 duty + 1 standby — feeding fan walls; low-GWP R-1234ze.
  • Pipe sizing (indicative): primary headers sized ~1.5–3 m/s; a 10 MW loop at ΔT 10 K needs ~240 kg/s ≈ ~14-inch mains, tapering to row branches.
  • Thermal storage: stratified chilled-water tank sized for ~5–15 minutes ride-through (generator start + chiller restart).
  • Supply/return: air loop ~18–24 °C supply; liquid FWS ~36–45 °C supply / ~50–55 °C return.

Growth to 40 MW: repeat the module per 10 MW hall; campus-level N+1 across four halls, with one optional 2N suite; concurrently maintainable headers.

6.2 Per-zone cooling method

See Appendix Table A3.

  • 300 racks @ 8 kW (2.4 MW): room air with cold/hot-aisle containment on fan walls or CRAHs; ~1,250 CFM/rack at 20 °F.
  • 300 racks @ 15 kW (4.5 MW): containment + fan walls, higher ΔT; ~2,355 CFM/rack.
  • 200 racks @ 40 kW (8.0 MW): rear-door heat exchangers or in-row; RDHx at 40 kW sits mid-range (nVent/Motivair rate 44–75 kW).
  • 200 racks @ 120 kW (24.0 MW): direct-to-chip with CDUs on warm water; ~150–180 LPM/rack; residual ~24–36 kW/pod on fan-wall air.

PUE build-up (indicative, Phoenix year): liquid side (61% of load) dry-cooled with near-zero compressor hours contributes a very low partial PUE (~1.05–1.10); air side (39%) with chillers in high ambient contributes higher (~1.3–1.4). Blended annualized ≈ 1.2–1.3, comfortably under 1.4. Monsoon week (higher wet-bulb, adiabatic assist working hardest, chillers loaded): blended ~1.35–1.45 — the target is at risk only in the worst week, which is why dry coolers get reclaimed-water assist.

6.3 Delivery and risk — the scope split

  • Owner-furnished (OFE): chillers, CDUs, dry coolers (long-lead; lock factory slots).
  • Contractor-furnished (CFE): piping, headers, valves, fan walls/air handlers, containment.
  • Tenant/IT-vendor: rack manifolds and QDs may be tenant or IT-vendor scope — must be pinned.
  • Wholesale vs retail fit-out boundary: in wholesale, the tenant owns inside-the-rack and often the manifold; in retail colo the operator provides more. The "rack that arrives before its CDU" is the signature failure: GPU racks (short IT lead time) landing before the 26–52-week CDU is installed, piped, charged, and commissioned — stranding capital. Mitigation: order CDUs at land-close, reserve slots, and stage commissioning (Topic 11 hand-off).

7. Verdict on the two course thresholds

7.1 Cooling scale by kW/rack (Topic 2)

Course scale: open air to ~10, containment 10–25, rear-door/in-row 20–80, direct-to-chip 40–175, immersion 100–250, marks at 20, 45, 80, 100, 132, 246.

Verdict: broadly right, lightly dated. Where it's right: open air to ~10 kW; RDHx/in-row genuinely spanning 20–80 kW (vendor-confirmed to 75 kW); DTC 40–175 and immersion 100–250 are sound; the marks at 120/132 (GB200 NVL72) and the 246 upper reach (future two-rack/next-gen) are real. Where it's off for a 2026 Phoenix engineer: - Raise the practical air ceiling note: containment realistically reaches ~30 kW with disciplined airflow before RDHx is compulsory; the 20 kW mark is the comfort limit, not the hard wall. - DTC is now effectively mandatory above ~50–80 kW, not merely "available at 40." Air/RDHx and DTC overlap 40–80 kW; above 80 kW DTC is the only practical path. - Corrected bands: open air ≤10; containment 10–30; rear-door/in-row 20–80; DTC 50–200+ (GB300 at 142, roadmap beyond); immersion 100–250 (Vertiv notes potential to ~900). Keep marks at 20, 45, 80, 120/132, and add 142 (GB300).

7.2 PUE by regime (Topic 2)

Course scale: room air 1.4–1.7; containment 1.25–1.45; rear-door 1.2–1.35 (50–80% to liquid); direct-to-chip 1.1–1.25 (70–80% to liquid); switching at 20, 45, 80 kW.

Verdict: right for a temperate site, optimistic for Phoenix air and pessimistic for warm-water liquid. Corrections: - Room air in a 45 °C dry climate with near-zero-water dry rejection can exceed 1.5 on peak days — widen to 1.4–1.6 for Phoenix air. - Rear-door captures more like 80–100% of sensible heat, not 50–80% — its PUE band (1.2–1.35) is fair. - DTC on warm water (W40/W45) dry-cooled reaches 1.1–1.2 annualized — the low end is very achievable in Phoenix precisely because the compressor is off; immersion 1.03–1.10. - Keep switch points at ~20, ~45, ~80 kW but add that regime PUE depends more on water/rejection strategy and climate than on the density band alone.

8. Open questions and what is changing fast in 2026

  1. Two-phase cooling under a PFAS/dielectric cloud. Per its Dec 20, 2022 announcement, 3M said it "will exit per- and polyfluoroalkyl substance (PFAS) manufacturing and work to discontinue the use of PFAS across its product portfolio by the end of 2025" (including all fluorinated fluids); the last order deadline for Novec fluids was March 31, 2025. The EPA hazardous-substance designation and EU restrictions leave two-phase immersion/DTC with "immediate risk of obsolescence" (Motivair/DCD). Single-phase DTC (water-glycol, PFAS-free) is now the dominant liquid method (~55% share, 2026). Hydrocarbon two-phase fluids are lab-credible but not commercially qualified as of mid-2026. TSCA PFAS reporting for operators is pushed to Jan 31, 2027.
  2. Facility water temperatures. The tug between warm (efficiency/reuse) and cool (silicon headroom) is unresolved; ASHRAE warns supportable W-classes may regress as chip power rises and case temps fall.
  3. CDU supply-chain constraints. CDUs 26–52 weeks; the DLC market grew 156% YoY (Dell'Oro, Q2 2025); vendor consolidation (Trane/LiquidStack, Schneider/Motivair, Vertiv/ThermoKey) is shrinking sourcing options.
  4. Zero-water commitments. Microsoft's closed-loop design (Phoenix pilot) sets a template but shifts cost to PUE and capex; the water-vs-energy trade is now a board-level ESG decision.
  5. Heat-reuse mandates. EU EED reporting and German EnEfG ERF quotas (10/15/20% by 2026/27/28) are live in Europe; no US analog, and no Phoenix heat customer.
  6. Refrigerant rules. AIM Act / F-gas phase-down is forcing A2L/low-GWP (R-1234ze, R-513A, R-515B) and, in California, SF6-free switchgear from 2025 — adding cost and recertification.

Glossary

  • PUE — Power Usage Effectiveness: total facility energy ÷ IT energy.
  • WUE — Water Usage Effectiveness: annual site water (L) ÷ IT energy (kWh).
  • MLC — Mechanical Load Component: ASHRAE 90.4 ratio of cooling/fan/pump/heat-rejection energy to IT energy.
  • ASHRAE class (A1–A4, H1) — air-inlet temperature/humidity envelopes for IT gear.
  • W-class (W17–W+) — liquid supply-water temperature classes named by their upper limit.
  • CRAC / CRAH — computer-room air conditioner (DX) / air handler (chilled water).
  • Fan wall — modular EC-fan + coil array forming a hall's air backbone.
  • Containment — physical separation of hot and cold aisles to stop mixing.
  • Economizer — free-cooling mode using outside air or water instead of compressors.
  • RDHx — rear-door heat exchanger; liquid coil on a rack's rear.
  • DTC — direct-to-chip cold-plate liquid cooling.
  • CDU — coolant distribution unit; isolates TCS from FWS via a heat exchanger.
  • TCS / FWS — Technology Cooling System (server loop) / Facility Water System (rejection loop).
  • QD — quick disconnect; blind-mate coolant coupling at the rack.
  • Approach temperature — temperature difference across a heat exchanger.
  • ΔT — temperature rise across IT gear (air) or the cold plate/loop (liquid).
  • Immersion (single/two-phase) — servers submerged in dielectric fluid; two-phase boils it.
  • PFAS — per-/poly-fluoroalkyl "forever chemicals" used in two-phase dielectric fluids.
  • A2L / low-GWP refrigerant — mildly flammable, low-global-warming refrigerants (R-1234ze, R-513A, R-515B).
  • ERF — Energy Reuse Factor: share of energy exported as reused heat.
  • Thermal storage / ride-through — stored chilled water bridging the gap until chillers restart.

What surprised the researcher

  1. The CDU, not the chiller, is the schedule killer. Historically a late-stage IT item, the CDU is now MEP critical-path with 26–52-week lead times and a DLC market up 156% in a single year — and it must be piped, charged, and commissioned before GPUs energize.
  2. Warm water is a feature, not a compromise. NVIDIA making 45 °C (W45) the GB300 design point means the densest racks in history can be cooled chiller-free — the opposite of the intuition that hotter chips need colder water.
  3. Two-phase cooling was effectively regulated out of the mainstream mid-transition. A single 2022 corporate decision (3M's PFAS exit) plus EPA/EU rules pushed hyperscalers back to more complex single-phase DTC water loops — a rare case of chemistry policy redirecting data-center architecture.
  4. Air never leaves. Even at 120 kW with 70–80% heat to liquid, 24–36 kW/pod of residual air load keeps fan walls in the AI hall — the "all-liquid data center" is a myth at the room level.
  5. Phoenix's dryness, not its heat, is the asset. A 45 °C dry bulb looks hostile, but low wet-bulb makes dry coolers and warm-water loops viable, letting a near-zero-water site still chase PUE ≤ 1.4.
