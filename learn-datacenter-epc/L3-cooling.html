Cooling and thermal management

Deep detail and sources: the appendix for this topic. Short version: sixty words. The research report this chapter was distilled from. Earlier version: archived 2026-09-26.

Almost every kilowatt (kW) used by information technology (IT) equipment ends up as heat. Cooling carries that heat from a rack to outdoor air; it does not make the heat disappear. Our running example is a Phoenix-area colocation campus, where tenants rent space and power: 1,000 racks, 38.9 megawatts (MW) of IT draw, planned as roughly 40 MW in four approximately 10 MW phases. Its Tier III-class design must allow plant maintenance without shutting the hall down. Power usage effectiveness (PUE) compares total facility power with IT power; the design limit of 1.4 means no more than about 56 MW at the site boundary when IT draws 40 MW. Can both the electricity and water budgets survive a hot afternoon?

Start with the heat

Before choosing cooling machines, see where the IT heat originates. The 120 kW artificial intelligence (AI) racks use cold plates, metal plates that transfer chip heat into flowing coolant. They still send some heat into room air. A 75% liquid-capture assumption gives us a first map.

Takeaway: The 200 dense racks dominate IT load, but only 18 MW of the 38.9 MW, about 46%, enters their cold-plate loop. The other 20.9 MW needs a different path, including the 6 MW left in those same racks. Electrical and fan losses add heat beyond these IT figures; the cooling appendix, APX-cooling §1, gives the conversions.

Check the rack arithmetic
  • The four groups draw 300 × 8 kW = 2.4 MW, 300 × 15 kW = 4.5 MW, 200 × 40 kW = 8 MW and 200 × 120 kW = 24 MW. Together they draw 38.9 MW.
  • One kilowatt of heat equals about 3,412 British thermal units (BTU) per hour, or 0.284 tons of cooling. A 15 kW rack therefore rejects about 4.26 tons.
  • The 56 MW figure is a site electricity limit at the stated IT load and PUE. It is not the heat load of the room-air chillers.

Follow two paths to outdoor air

Heat from ordinary servers warms an enclosed hot aisle. A fan wall, a large bank of fans and chilled-water coils, takes heat from that air; a chiller uses a compressor to move the water's heat outdoors. In dense racks, a rack manifold collects coolant through quick disconnects (QDs), sealed rack connectors. A coolant distribution unit (CDU) pumps server coolant past a heat exchanger that transfers heat into separate facility water. Roof dry coolers, outdoor coils with fans, then release it to the air. The picture's 36 degrees Celsius (°C) facility-water supply is a normal-hour target, not a dry-only promise for a 45 °C day.

Takeaway: The routes share a building but not a refrigerator: the warm-water route can often avoid a compressor, while the room-air route needs one in hot weather. Liquid-cooled racks still rely on that air route for heat their cold plates did not capture (APX-cooling §4).

What an economizer can and cannot do

An economizer uses suitable outdoor conditions to reduce compressor use. Direct outdoor air cannot maintain a normal server-inlet temperature at a 45 °C peak. Indirect evaporative methods keep outdoor air out of the hall but use water. The roof dry coolers serve the warm facility-water circuit, not the high-purity server circuit.

Stop air from mixing

Server fans only cool chips if air enters rack fronts instead of taking a shortcut to the return. Hot-aisle containment uses doors, a roof and blanking panels, plates closing empty rack slots, to trap exhaust. The American Society of Heating, Refrigerating and Air-Conditioning Engineers (ASHRAE) recommends 18–27 °C at common server inlets; its H1 high-density air class recommends 18–22 °C. This pictured 15 kW zone takes 24 °C air from a fan-wall coil supplied with 20 °C water. Its electronically commutated (EC) speed-controlled fans sit beside a mechanical, electrical and plumbing (MEP) service gallery.

Takeaway: Sealing stops hot exhaust returning to server intakes and cold supply bypassing the racks. A raised-floor tile is a perforated outlet from an underfloor air supply, with limited flow. Takeaway: Containment makes the larger air-temperature rise feasible without overheating intakes. Less required airflow can cut fan energy, but the table does not promise the same percentage saving for the entire cooling plant (APX-cooling §3).

Air units and the airflow rule
  • A computer-room air conditioner (CRAC) has its own direct-expansion compressor. A computer-room air handler (CRAH) is a fan and chilled-water coil; the fan wall is a hall-scale version. In-row units put a cooling coil between racks.
  • At sea level, required airflow is roughly rack watts divided by 0.318 times the air rise in °F. Fan power varies approximately with speed cubed, so 0.8 cubed is 0.512. Real savings also depend on the airflow path and controls.

Pick cooling by rack, not fashion

Our four rack groups do not need identical hardware. An in-row unit puts a chilled-water air coil between racks; a rear-door heat exchanger bolts a water coil behind a rack and catches its warm exhaust. Both can serve the 40 kW zone, but our default is a rear door on the chilled-water plant. Direct-to-chip cooling circulates liquid through cold plates for the 120 kW pods. Immersion, submerging electronics in electrically insulating liquid, is another option but not this multi-tenant design. The bands overlap because each equipment maker sets its own airflow and coolant limits.

Takeaway: We contain even the 8 kW racks to share a hall layout. The 40 kW doors send heat to the chilled-water plant, not to the pods' warmer circuit. At 120 kW, cold plates typically collect 70–80% of rack heat; the remaining air load does not disappear (APX-cooling §8).

Why the method bands are not warranties
  • Rear doors can serve roughly 20–80 kW per rack; in-row air cooling is usually a lower-density option within that overlap. A tenant's certified temperature and flow limits determine the actual choice.
  • Two-phase cooling boils a specialised fluid instead of simply warming it. Many historic fluids used per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances (PFAS). Supplier withdrawal and changing rules create fluid-qualification risk, not a blanket ban on every two-phase system.

Cross the liquid boundary

A 120 kW pod needs two coolant circuits so clean server coolant does not mix with outdoor plant water. Its technology cooling system (TCS) runs through cold plates and rack connections to the CDU. Across the CDU's heat exchanger, the facility water system (FWS) accepts heat but not server coolant. At this example operating point, about 90 kW enters water at roughly 150–180 liters per minute (L/min); about 30 kW still warms room air. The crowded ceiling also holds a busway, an enclosed power conductor, beside a tray for data cables.

Takeaway: The picture's Topic 8 marker identifies the course's rack-to-building hand-off at the connectors, not a break in the heat path. Follow the temperatures across the CDU: the liquids stay separate while heat passes to the cooler stream.

Takeaway: The gap between 40 °C server supply and 36 °C facility supply is 4 °C; the return streams are 2 °C apart in this example. This is a heat-exchanger operating point, not permission to give every tenant rack 40 °C coolant (APX-cooling §9, APX-cooling §10).

Protect the server circuit

Specify rack-approved coolant and wetted materials, filtration, leak detection, automatic isolation and a clean flush before filling. The two circuits can have different water-quality requirements because they do not mix. The vendor must verify flow and heat-exchanger performance at the stated temperatures.

Check the 45 °C hour

ASHRAE's water classes name the highest coolant temperature allowed at qualified IT equipment inlets: W40 reaches 40 °C and W45 reaches 45 °C. They do not rate the facility pipe or guarantee that a roof cooler works in every kind of weather. A dry cooler cannot deliver 36 °C facility water from 45 °C outdoor dry-bulb air, the ordinary thermometer reading, by itself. An adiabatic cooler sprays water to cool its incoming air by evaporation. Its limit is wet-bulb temperature, the evaporative limit that rises with humidity.

The operating modes show why a rack's temperature rating and the available outdoor heat sink are separate questions.

Takeaway: Phoenix's dryness often helps the spray, but humidity and the water budget can take that advantage away. The pictured chilled plant has not been sized to refrigerate every pod as a fallback; that option would require added capacity. An approved warmer rack setting helps only if the entire path can reject its heat (APX-cooling §2, APX-cooling §4, APX-cooling §6).

Check the equipment, not just the class name

A W-class applies to water supplied to certified IT equipment; certification requires full operation across the class range. Some rack configurations need substantially cooler facility water than this course's example, while others are designed for warmer operation. Even a W45-approved server needs facility water cooler than its own supply across the CDU, so a 45 °C outdoor reading is not by itself a dry-cooling solution.

Put the plant on the site

The reference design puts adiabatic dry coolers on the roof for the warm loop and air-cooled chillers in the yard for the chilled loop. A cooling tower, which continuously evaporates water to reject heat, is not our default. Three 3.5 MW chillers appear in the drawing: two are needed and one is a spare. This is N+1, meaning the duty units plus one standby, if the duty units still meet the full hot-day load. The tank's ten-minute label is a starting ride-through target, and reclaimed water assists the dry coolers on hot days.

Takeaway: Water usage effectiveness (WUE) divides annual site water, in liters (L), by annual IT energy, in kilowatt-hours (kWh). The campus target is at most 0.2 L/kWh; reclaimed-water spray still counts. Model all site water over a year rather than treating one dry hour as proof (APX-cooling §11).

Water, refrigerant and operating cost
  • Site WUE counts reclaimed water used on site. Peak spray, other site water uses and any permitted discharge need an annual water balance; the yard drain also needs a site-approved route. Phoenix has no heat customer in this reference design, so recovered heat is rejected outdoors rather than sold.
  • Specify a low-global-warming-potential refrigerant and check its applicable safety rules. Confirm chiller output at the selected chilled-water temperature and 45 °C outdoor air, not just its nameplate capacity (APX-cooling §5).
  • The course budgets roughly $34 million of its $65 million annual operating cost for electricity across the campus. The breakdown is illustrative, not the calculation behind that budget.

Assemble the first 10 MW

Scale the campus by about a quarter. Roughly 50 Phase 1 racks draw 120 kW each, making 6 MW of IT heat: about 4.5 MW to cold plates and 1.5 MW left for fan-wall air. Other racks add roughly 4 MW. Allow about 0.7 MW of electrical losses inside the hall, and the chilled path serves approximately 6.2 MW while the warm path serves 4.5 MW. The chosen magnetic-bearing chillers have oil-free compressors, but their stated capacity is not a Phoenix peak-hour guarantee.

Takeaway: The chillers have only 0.8 MW of nominal duty capacity beyond the estimated chilled-side load, before hot-air derating or extra fan heat is checked. They cannot be assumed to cool all 4.5 MW of pod liquid too. Confirm the racks' warm-water rating and peak spray capacity, or purchase enough additional refrigeration for tenants needing colder water. Valves, pumps and roof coolers also need maintainable paths as phases are added (APX-cooling §7, APX-cooling §12).

Why Phase 1 is called 10 MW

One quarter of the listed rack mix is approximately 75 racks at 8 kW, 75 at 15 kW, 50 at 40 kW and 50 at 120 kW. Those racks total 9.725 MW; 10 MW is the rounded phase design load. Their non-pod IT draw is about 3.7 MW, rounded here to roughly 4 MW. Final selections need manufacturer ratings at the actual outdoor design condition and allowance for fan and pump heat.

Keep cooling through a power cut

A utility outage is a thermal event, not just an electrical one. At 120 kW per rack, inlet temperatures can rise in under a minute if airflow stops. A stratified tank keeps colder water beneath warmer return water, providing a reserve for the chilled circuit during chiller restart. It does nothing if the water stops circulating. The warm circuit has no compressor to restart, but its CDU pumps and roof fans still need power.

Takeaway: Ten minutes is a preliminary tank target, not a fail-safe: a 15-minute restart could outlast it. Test generator transfer, pump and fan power, rack inlet temperatures and full-load chiller recovery together (APX-cooling §7).

What ten minutes represents

At a 6.2 MW chilled-side load, ten minutes requires about 1.03 megawatt-hours (MWh) of usable stored cooling before design margins. The corresponding tank volume depends on allowed water-temperature rise, mixing and pump flow. A water-energy calculation alone cannot establish safe rack temperatures during a transfer.

Buy the connections before the racks

This design-build project uses one contracted team for engineering and construction, while the owner reserves long-lead owner-furnished equipment (OFE): chillers, dry coolers and CDUs. The basis of design (BOD), the written equipment and performance rules, must lock rack coolant limits before those units are ordered. A CDU takes about 26–52 weeks; electrical switchgear, which routes large power supplies, can take 44–80 weeks or more. Delivery is not readiness: each 10 MW phase needs connected, tested loops before tenant racks energise.

Takeaway: In wholesale colocation, a tenant rents a dedicated suite and may supply its manifolds; in retail colocation, the operator supplies more rack-side connections. Phase 1 commits about $180–230 million, including shared site and shell work for all four halls, before tenants can use it.

The rack-to-plant contract boundary

This campus assigns CDUs to the owner. Specify who supplies rack manifolds and quick disconnects, maintains coolant quality and leak isolation, and powers pumps during an outage. The builder, CDU vendor and rack integrator test together. Otherwise, racks arriving before their CDU or needing colder water than the plant can provide cannot run (APX-cooling §9, APX-cooling §12).
