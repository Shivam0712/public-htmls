Why can one contiguous parcel with room for a substation and future phases be more useful than the same total acreage divided by roads, easements, or separate ownership?

Total acreage does not tell a developer how much usable space is together. One unbroken parcel can accommodate the data-center buildings, an on-site substation or switchyard, and later campus phases. Card 10 of 100mechanism

Why does industrial zoning alone not establish that a data center can be built, and what further land-use approvals or constraints may apply?

Industrial zoning is only an initial land-use screen, not permission to build. A project may still need rezoning, a conditional or special-use permit, height variances for cooling or electrical equipment, setbacks, noise compliance, site-plan approval, and building permits. Even correctly zoned land may fail because power or water is unavailable, flood risk is unacceptable, or local opposition defeats approval at a public hearing.

Card 11 of 100methodology

How do high-risk flood zones and the 500-year floodplain enter site screening, financing, insurance, and tenant approval?

Developers generally screen out sites in FEMA floodways or high-risk 100-year flood zones. Even if only part of a parcel is exposed, flood risk can complicate insurance, financing, and tenant approval. For mission-critical data centers, serious developers also check the 500-year floodplain—an area with a 0.2% annual chance of flooding—to identify risks beyond the standard high-risk zone.

Card 13 of 100mechanism

What are free-cooling hours, and why can a hot, arid site face a trade-off between cooling electricity and cooling water?

Free-cooling hours are the hours each year a site can cool without mechanical refrigeration. Cooler, drier climates generally offer more of them, lowering cooling energy use. At a hot, arid site, fewer such hours make cooling more demanding: evaporative systems can use less electricity but more water, while air-cooled systems save water at an energy penalty. Climate therefore affects both operating cost and water needs.

Card 16 of 100foundation

Why is connecting a data center treated as large-load interconnection rather than generator interconnection, and how have their regulatory processes differed?

A data center is a large electricity consumer, so connecting it is a *load* interconnection, not a generator interconnection. Historically, large-load connections have been handled at the state level, often through utility-specific, ad hoc procedures. New generators, by contrast, use federally regulated processes standardized by FERC. The distinction can blur when a data center proposes on-site generation alongside its load.
