From a business case to a buildable data center

Deep detail and sources: the appendix for this topic. Short version: sixty words. The research report this chapter was distilled from. Earlier version: archived 2026-09-26.

Imagine an owner outside a hot city such as Phoenix or Dallas renting room, power and cooling to several customers. A rack is a cabinet for computers and network gear. In colocation, a large tenant may rent an entire suite, called wholesale, while a small tenant rents a few racks, called retail. The owner's promise must cover what can run, what stays on during maintenance and how the finished site will prove it. Power delivered to the tenant's equipment is the information technology (IT) load. We will follow that promise from the business case to handover.

The campus the owner wants to sell

Our example holds 1,000 racks and grows in roughly 10-megawatt (MW) increments. One MW equals 1,000 kilowatts (kW); these units measure power, not a year's electricity use. Power usage effectiveness (PUE) divides total site power by IT power under the same conditions. The owner's design target is PUE no greater than 1.4 at design load: about 40 MW IT therefore calls for up to about 56 MW at the utility connection.

Takeaway: Cooling and power losses account for much of the difference between tenant power and site power. The electricity budget does not mean the site draws its design maximum every hour. The worked arithmetic is in the appendix at APX-requirements §12.

Why 38.9 and 56 can both be right

Multiplying the unrounded 38.9 MW by 1.4 gives about 54.5 MW. The course calls this an approximately 40 MW campus, so 40 × 1.4 gives approximately 56 MW at its planning boundary. The annual operating budget is a separate model of real operation, not an extrapolation from continuous design draw.

Write the requirement before choosing equipment

Before anyone selects a generator, the owner writes the Owner's Project Requirements (OPR): what the site must do and how success will be checked. The engineer writes a Basis of Design (BOD), naming systems, assumptions and calculations that can meet those requirements. Commissioning is the planned inspection and functional testing of installed systems. This chain shows how a business promise becomes evidence at handover.

Takeaway: 'Reliable' tells a tester nothing. 'The first phase supplies 10 MW of IT capacity while an agreed power or cooling component is isolated for maintenance' can be designed and demonstrated. The owner writes the OPR before design; the engineer develops the BOD through design, and both are revisited at decision points. If the OPR stays outside the signed contract to avoid conflicting documents, its targets and tests must still enter the contract. See APX-requirements §1 and APX-requirements §2.

What goes into the two documents

The OPR states tenant mix, day-one and ultimate IT capacity, density by zone, maintainability, design PUE conditions, budget, schedule and desired certificates. The BOD records electrical and cooling arrangements, equipment sizing, assumptions, site-specific code editions and how each requirement will be checked. Specify how to demonstrate design-load performance before tenants bring equipment; some claims require calculations rather than a promise of measured annual results.

Why an average rack misleads

Rack density is the planned electrical power per rack, in kW, not a claim that every rack draws it all the time. The owner needs a density profile because tenants bring different equipment. A pod is a group of racks planned for a shared power and cooling limit. Our 1,000 positions include 300 racks at 8 kW, 300 at 15 kW, 200 at 40 kW and 200 racks in artificial intelligence (AI) pods at 120 kW. The bar splits their 38.9 MW of IT power.

Takeaway: A fifth of the racks needs most of the power, so the mix must become a floor plan rather than one 38.9 kW average. White space holds racks; gray space houses power and cooling equipment. A busway carries electricity along a row, a manifold branches cooling liquid to racks, and a coolant distribution unit (CDU) manages liquid flow and heat transfer. This plan reserves 15–20% of white and gray space for reconfiguration.

Takeaway: Only the 200 AI racks need those 120 kW feeds and liquid connections; calling the entire hall 'AI-ready' without ratings would mean little. Reserved space lets the owner change the mix without equipping every ordinary rack for 120 kW. See APX-requirements §5 and APX-requirements §12.

Why a site's average may not predict a new build

A 2025 Uptime Institute survey of existing facilities found a common rack density near 9 kW. Other industry surveys reported 16 kW in 2025 and 27 kW in 2026 among their respondents. The surveys represent different populations; none replaces the four rack ratings actually sold in this campus. See APX-requirements §4.

Let the heat follow the rack plan

Nearly all power a rack uses becomes heat. At 15 kW, aisle containment separates cold supply air from hot exhaust. At 120 kW, direct-to-chip liquid cooling uses cold plates fixed to processors to move much of the heat into pipes. The rack comparison makes that different plumbing visible. Its floor tag of 1,500 kilograms (kg) is an illustrative allowance for a heavy rack, not a typical rack weight or a floor-strength specification.

Takeaway: Eight times the power in roughly the same footprint needs bigger electrical feeds, pipes and a floor checked for concentrated loads. A rear-door heat exchanger is a water-cooled coil behind a rack that removes hot exhaust. The scale shows plausible cooling choices, not guaranteed maximums.

Takeaway: Air becomes difficult to move beyond roughly 20 kW per rack, while this campus plans chip liquid for its 120 kW racks. The overlapping middle calls for engineering at local outdoor temperatures, not a universal cutoff. See APX-requirements §6.

Why the floor and cooling numbers are ranges

The research mentions loaded AI racks around 1,500 pounds, about 680 kg, while the existing picture says 1,500 kg. Read the picture's number as a conservative equipment allowance to confirm with a structural engineer; a floor rating also needs rack footprint and point-load checks. Published cooling limits vary by airflow, water temperature and equipment, so the scale is a planning guide. Concentrating more IT in less floor area may save shell cost before denser power and liquid systems push cost back up.

Build the shape, then buy the machines

A finished hall is not useful if the utility cannot supply it. The site and shell allow for the eventual 40 MW IT, but only the first roughly 10 MW of mechanical, electrical and plumbing (MEP) plant is installed now. An uninterruptible power supply (UPS) bridges the time before backup generators start; a chiller cools circulating water. Pipe stubs are capped future connections, valved taps can be isolated, and empty conduit reserves a protected cable route. This cutaway shows one hall in the four-hall campus; its 'Slab for 40 MW' label means eventual campus IT capacity, not slab strength.

Takeaway: The hard-to-change shell and connection points are ready for expansion, but future transformers, chillers and generators remain unpurchased. The $180–230 million first phase includes shared site and shell work for all four halls, so later phases do not repeat that cost. Customers' leases and utility delivery determine when each block is installed.

Takeaway: If a row has free space but its electrical feed is full, that space cannot be sold. This is stranded capacity: something paid for but unusable because another resource has run out. See APX-requirements §7 and APX-requirements §8.

Capacity is not one number

A nameplate rating is the maximum printed on equipment; adding nameplates overstates what normally runs together. The engineer chooses a diversified design load with appropriate reserve. Actual draw changes with tenant occupancy and activity, so track usable rack space, power and cooling together.

What can stay live during maintenance

Customers care about planned service as well as failures. Concurrent maintainability means the operator can take a component of the power or cooling system out for maintenance without stopping the IT load; the Uptime Institute calls that Tier III. Tier IV adds fault tolerance: it is designed to survive one covered site-infrastructure failure without stopping that load. Takeaway: The standby route is one illustrative Tier III layout, not a requirement that every Tier III design look exactly like this. Nor does the picture promise that a Tier III fault causes no interruption. The annual times below are indicative arithmetic, not a guarantee of actual uptime.

Takeaway: Our mixed-tenant owner chooses Tier III's maintainability rather than paying the Tier IV premium for every hall. A design review and an inspection of the constructed facility are different certificates. If the site is marketed as certified, ask for the constructed-facility certificate for the actual hall. See APX-requirements §9.

What a tier does and does not measure

The commonly quoted 99.982% for Tier III and 99.995% for Tier IV give the times in the table when applied to 8,760 hours in a year. Uptime certifies site-infrastructure topology, not those percentages as a service promise. TIA-942-C uses Rated 1–4; ISO/IEC 22237 uses Availability Classes 1–4. Similar numbers across frameworks do not mean interchangeable certificates.

Which rules bind the site

What applies near Phoenix may differ from what applies near Dallas. The authority having jurisdiction (AHJ) is the local official who enforces adopted rules and amendments. The National Electrical Code (NEC) is an electrical code; a published standard can also bind when a code or contract adopts it. ASHRAE publishes data-center energy standard 90.4. It also publishes Guideline 0, a process guide for requirements and commissioning. This figure separates four ways requirements reach the project.

Takeaway: The owner's design PUE limit of 1.4 is not the energy-code compliance calculation. Standard 90.4 assesses cooling and electrical-chain losses separately. The engineer records locally adopted editions, including battery-fire requirements, before ordering equipment. See APX-requirements §10.

How two common code details work

ASHRAE 90.4 uses a mechanical load component (MLC), cooling-system energy relative to IT energy, and an electrical loss component (ELC), electrical-chain losses relative to IT energy. Limits depend on the adopted edition and, for cooling, the climate. NEC Article 645 offers optional wiring methods for qualifying IT rooms only if its conditions are met, including relevant fire, access and emergency power off (EPO) provisions. A newly published edition does not automatically replace the one enforced locally.

What auditors need after opening

A tenant with payment-card systems will ask for controlled entrances and evidence of who entered a sensitive room. The Payment Card Industry Data Security Standard (PCI DSS) sets physical-access conditions for those areas. A System and Organization Controls 2 (SOC 2) report covering an operating period checks whether security controls worked over time. Leadership in Energy and Environmental Design (LEED) is a green-building rating, while the ENERGY STAR data-center label depends on measured operation. The figure distinguishes what must be built now from what staff must prove later.

Takeaway: Doors, leak sensors and meters are physical choices for the owner and builder now. Audit logs, staff procedures and actual energy performance arrive only after opening. The contract can require the hardware and a record handoff, but cannot make a future operator's audit report appear at construction handover. See APX-requirements §11.

Security depends on the tenant and room

For in-scope payment-data areas, PCI DSS can require monitoring entry and keeping specified footage or access records for at least three months; check the current control and the exact room. ISO/IEC 27001 Annex A.7 covers physical controls. The Federal Risk and Authorization Management Program (FedRAMP) can draw on National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) 800-53 physical and environmental controls for a federal tenant. A building supports, but does not itself earn, these operational authorizations.

Who pays for an unclear promise

Who pays if the owner meant one thing and the drawing specifies another? In design-bid-build the owner separately hires designer and builder, and usually pays to correct gaps in its drawings. In design-build one firm designs and builds, but undefined scope, meaning the work actually agreed, can still become an owner change. An engineering, procurement and construction (EPC) turnkey firm can guarantee the stated result. These dots compare obligations actually written down, not wishes the owner never specified.

Takeaway: This campus uses design-build with owner-furnished equipment, long-lead items the owner orders early for the builder to install. The contract assigns delivery, installation, testing and delay risks rather than assuming one party controls them all. The OPR may stay outside signed construction documents to avoid conflicting terms, but its measurable requirements must be incorporated in the agreement. See APX-requirements §3.

Write the handover bargain now

For each roughly 10 MW phase, specify zone capacities, the maintenance test, design PUE conditions and, if sold as certified, the constructed Tier III certificate for the named hall. Assign utility and owner-furnished deliveries, and how delays change price or schedule. A fixed price cannot cover an unspoken requirement.

What late changes can cost

An AIA study across building types found change orders averaging about 4–5% of project value, with many arising in the final half of construction. That is a warning about clarity, not a forecast for this campus. Sign off changes to owner requirements and the engineer's design basis at each stage, before equipment orders lock in decisions.

From promises to proof

A promise needs an agreed test before the owner accepts a phase.

Takeaway: A design claim is not proof from the finished hall. Use the figures to check six decisions.

What do the OPR, BOD and commissioning each do?

State owner outcomes, design the solution, and test installed systems.

How many racks and how much IT power make up the AI pods?

200 of 1,000 racks need 24 of 38.9 MW, about 62% of IT power.

What cools the 15 kW and 120 kW racks?

Contained air and direct-to-chip liquid cooling, respectively.

What is reserved for future phases, and what plant arrives later?

Shell, structural headroom and pathways now; transformers, UPS units, chillers and generators are added in roughly 10 MW blocks.

What does Tier IV add to Tier III, and at what approximate capital premium?

Tier III allows maintenance without shutting IT down; Tier IV survives one covered failure and costs about 25–60% more.

Under design-build, who carries defined design and construction risk, and who produces operating audit records?

The builder carries defined delivery risk; the operator provides the later SOC 2 or ENERGY STAR evidence.
