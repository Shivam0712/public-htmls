The Power Chain from Utility Fence to UPS Output: A 2025–2026 Working-Knowledge Chapter

The Desktop Research report for Topic 5, run 2026-09-25 from briefs/ and published as delivered: no editing, no lint. The chapter and appendix were distilled from it; where they and this report disagree, the chapter is the course's position.

All worked numbers below are labeled indicative and are built on the locked reference facility: a 1,000-rack, ~40 MW-IT colocation campus outside a hot US metro (Phoenix/Dallas); density 300 racks @ 8 kW, 300 @ 15 kW, 200 @ 40 kW, 200 @ 120 kW (≈39 MW; the AI band is 20% of racks and 61% of load); design PUE 1.4 → ≈56 MW at the fence; Tier III concurrently maintainable with one optional 2N suite; design-build with owner-furnished long-lead gear (OFE); ~10 MW phases, Phase 1 ≈10 MW IT in Hall 1.

TL;DR

  • The chain is six links — utility substation → large power transformer → MV switchgear → (generators in parallel) → MV/LV unit substations → UPS — and in 2026 the binding constraint is not engineering but lead time: power transformers average 128 weeks and GSUs 144 weeks per Wood Mackenzie's Q2 2025 survey ("large units remain locked above two years, with power transformers averaging 128 weeks and GSUs 144 weeks in WoodMac's second quarter 2025 survey," POWER Magazine, "Transformers in 2026: Shortage, Scramble, or Self-Inflicted Crisis?"), data-center-spec MV switchgear 52–84+ weeks, large diesel gensets 50–110 weeks, UPS 26–52 weeks. Reserve factory slots before the design freezes; this is the single most important delivery decision.
  • The explorer's two rules are half-right. The ~2.5 MW UPS/generator block is a defensible UPS sweet spot but too small for modern generators (2.5–3.25 MW diesels are standard, so gensets are ~1:1 with UPS blocks, not sized off the same 2.5 MW brick); and the grid-voltage-by-load thresholds are roughly right at the boundaries but a 2026 engineer would connect the 56 MW reference campus at 115–138 kV via a dedicated customer substation, not at distribution voltage.
  • For the 40 MW campus, indicative fence load is ≈56 MW at PUE 1.4; it takes utility service at 115–138 kV, steps down to a 34.5 kV campus MV bus, and lands on 480Y/277 V unit substations feeding 2–3 MW UPS blocks with 5-minute lithium (LFP) ride-through. Phase 1 (10 MW IT) needs ~5 UPS blocks N+1 and ~5–6 generators; the campus grows to ~40 MW by repeating the block, not by resizing Phase 1.

1. Utility Service and the Customer Substation

1.1 What the interconnection delivers, by load

Service voltage tracks load. The industry pattern, corroborated across engineering and utility sources (2025–26):

For the reference campus at ≈56 MW at the fence, the defensible 2026 choice is a dedicated customer substation at 115 or 138 kV — comfortably above the distribution ceiling, below the transmission-interconnect threshold. (Indicative.)

1.2 Ownership and demarcation

At any voltage, the boundary is the demarcation point — usually the line-side terminals of the incoming structure or the high-side breaker. The utility owns the line, the revenue metering CTs/PTs, and typically the first high-side protective device; the customer owns everything downstream, including the step-down transformers and MV switchgear. Under design-build with OFE, the customer's EPC/design-builder engineers and builds the customer-side substation while the utility builds (or the customer builds and deeds back) the utility-side per an interconnection agreement. Revenue metering sits at the demarcation; sub-metering downstream is the customer's.

1.3 What "two feeds" really means

"Dual feed" is a spectrum of independence, and buyers routinely overpay for the label without the substance:

  1. Two feeders from the same substation bus — protects against a feeder fault; buys little against a substation or grid event. Weakest.
  2. Two feeders from different substations — protects against a single substation loss; better.
  3. Feeds from different transmission paths / grid injection points — protects against a transmission contingency; strongest, rarest, most expensive.

Crucially, Uptime Institute requires nothing of the utility side. Per Tier Standard: Topology, "the only reliable source of power for a data center is the engine-generator plant… the number of utility feeds, substations, and power grids… neither predicts nor influences" the Tier. Utility power is treated as an economic convenience, not a reliability source. So dual feeds are a cost/availability optimization, not a Tier requirement.

1.4 Substation layout, protection, footprint

A customer substation for a 40–60 MW campus contains: incoming line structure(s) and disconnects; high-side breakers (dead-tank or GIS); step-down power transformers (see §2); protective relaying (transformer differential, overcurrent, bus differential, breaker-failure); revenue metering; and a MV bus arranged for maintainability. Common bus arrangements, in ascending resilience/cost: radial → main-tie-main (double-ended) → ring bus → breaker-and-a-half. For Tier III concurrent maintainability, main-tie-main / double-ended is the workhorse: either transformer or bus section can be taken out with the tie closed and no load drop.

  • Footprint (indicative): a 40–60 MW campus customer substation typically occupies on the order of several acres including transformer pads, firewalls, control building and clearances; the amcompute rule that "at 20 MW or more a data center requires its own dedicated substation" holds here.
  • Campus MV voltage choice — 13.8 vs 34.5 kV: 34.5 kV "has become the workhorse distribution voltage for large campuses because it moves more power with less copper and fewer feeders" (ATK Energy, 2026). 13.8 kV is simpler and cheaper for smaller sites and shorter runs. For a 56 MW campus with distributed 10 MW halls, 34.5 kV on the campus loop is the defensible 2026 choice; each hall steps 34.5 kV → 480 V. (Indicative.)

1.5 Delivery and risk

Under design-build/OFE, the design-builder owns the customer-side substation design, procurement (often OFE for the LPTs and high-side breakers), construction and commissioning; the utility owns its side and the energization decision. Schedule risk sits overwhelmingly on two items: the utility interconnection/system-impact study timeline (frequently multi-year) and the large power transformer lead time (§2). Substation construction alone runs 2–4 years when utility scope is included.

2. Transformers

2.1 The chain from fence to UPS

Two transformer populations matter:

  1. Large power transformer (LPT): HV → MV, e.g., 138 kV → 34.5 kV, typically 30–100 MVA per unit, oil-filled, outdoor, in the customer substation. The reference campus needs on the order of two to three of these (N+1) for 56 MW.
  2. MV/LV unit substation transformers: MV → 480Y/277 V, typically 1,000–3,000 kVA, feeding UPS and mechanical plant. Dozens across the campus. These are the ones the DOE efficiency rules bite on.

2.2 Oil-filled vs dry-type; impedance

  • Oil-filled wins on efficiency, cooling and power density but triggers NEC Article 450 vault/containment/fire separation indoors; it is standard outdoors for LPTs. Dry-type is the standard indoor choice for unit substations feeding UPS — no flammable liquid, no containment sump, lower maintenance (visual/thermal inspections in years vs oil testing in months). Ester fluids (Cargill FR3 / Eaton Envirotemp) are a less-flammable middle path.
  • Impedance (%Z) sets fault current and voltage regulation. Per IEEE C57.12.00, nominal %Z for a 2,500 kVA unit is 5.75% (Federal Pacific catalog confirms 2,500 and 3,000 kVA = 5.75%). The relationship: available fault kVA ≈ transformer kVA ÷ %Z (per-unit).
    • Worked (indicative): a 2,500 kVA, 480Y/277 V unit at 6% Z. Full-load amps = 2,500,000 ÷ (1.732 × 480) = 3,007 A. Bolted secondary fault ≈ 3,007 ÷ 0.06 = ≈50,100 A (≈50 kA). At 5.75% it rises to ≈52 kA. Lower %Z → higher fault current (bigger breaker AIC) but better voltage regulation; higher %Z → the reverse. Engineers size breaker interrupting ratings against the −7.5% impedance tolerance (worst case).

2.3 Losses, efficiency and the DOE rule

Transformers lose energy two ways: no-load (core) loss, constant 24/7 whenever energized, and load (copper) loss, which scales with current squared (at 50% load, copper loss = 25% of its full-load value).

  • DOE 10 CFR 431: The distribution-transformer standard in force was set by the 2016 rule (from the April 2013 final rule). DOE published a new final rule in the Federal Register on April 22, 2024 (effective July 8, 2024) with mandatory compliance April 23, 2029, tightening medium-voltage dry-type (MVDT) losses ~10% and extending coverage. Important 2025 caveat: under the Congressional Review Act (Pub. L. 119-8), at 90 FR 43371 (Sept. 9, 2025) DOE removed certain Dec. 2024 amendments to Part 431, reverting affected sections to the Dec. 22, 2024 version. Net effect for a buyer specifying now: the 2016 efficiency floors apply to units installed before 2029; the tighter 2029 floors are the forward target — confirm the current CFR text at time of order.
  • Efficiency levels: MVDT efficiency is tested at 50% load; DOE 2016 gives ~98.9% for a 500 kVA/high-BIL unit rising to 99.3–99.5% for 1,500–2,500 kVA units; a 2,500 kVA liquid-immersed unit is 99.49% at 50% load.
  • What losses cost the reference facility. The campus electricity bill is ≈$34M/yr. Take a single 2,500 kVA dry-type unit substation transformer at ~4 kW no-load and ~27 kW full-load copper loss, 50% load factor, $0.10/kWh: annual loss cost = (4 × 8,760 + 27 × 0.25 × 8,760) × $0.10 ≈ $9,400/yr per transformer (indicative). Across ~20+ unit substations plus the LPTs, transformer losses run into the low-single-digit millions per year — a real, PUE-visible line item. A 100 W no-load reduction saves ~$88/yr per unit at $0.10/kWh, more at data-center rates of $0.12–0.15/kWh.

2.4 Sizing: from 10 MW IT to a transformer rating

The chain of multipliers (indicative):

  1. Start with IT load: 10 MW.
  2. Apply PUE 1.4 for total electrical load: 10 × 1.4 = 14 MW at the hall.
  3. Split: ~10 MW critical (UPS-backed) + ~4 MW mechanical/other.
  4. Apply power factor (~0.9–0.95 at the UPS input) and diversity (power block sized to diversified design load, not nameplate sum).
  5. Add redundancy (N+1 at unit-substation level) and future headroom.
  • Phase 1 (10 MW IT / 14 MW hall): at 34.5 kV → 480 V, using 2,500 kVA unit substations, 14 MW ÷ (2.5 MVA × ~0.9 pf) ≈ 6.2 → 7–8 unit substations including N+1. The upstream LPT feeding Hall 1 sees ~16–18 MVA including losses and margin → one 20–30 MVA class LPT (with a second for concurrent maintainability at campus level). (Indicative.)
  • 40 MW campus / ≈56 MW fence: ≈56 MVA ÷ transformer size → two to three 30 MVA-class LPTs (N+1) at the substation, each feeding a 34.5 kV bus section.

2.5 Lead times and mitigations (dated)

See the consolidated table in §7. For LPTs the 2025 reality (Wood Mackenzie Q2 2025 survey) is 128 weeks average for power transformers, 144 weeks for GSUs; substation-class 5–50 MVA units 75–110 weeks; three-phase padmount/unit-substation transformers 40–65 weeks. Mitigations the specifier controls: standard designs (avoid bespoke), dual-rated units (e.g., 34.5/13.8 kV), and reservation slots booked before design freeze.

2.6 Delivery and risk under OFE

When the owner furnishes transformers, it takes on: the design freeze (specs locked early to hold the factory slot), storage (units often arrive before the pad is ready — who insures/energizes preservation heaters), warranty administration, and the factory acceptance test (FAT) hand-off (named only). The classic dispute: if the pad is not ready when the unit ships, the owner-as-supplier eats demurrage/storage — a risk that in design-bid-build would sit with a contractor. Contractual clarity on ship-date vs pad-ready-date is essential.

3. Medium-Voltage Switchgear

3.1 AIS vs GIS

  • Air-insulated switchgear (AIS): larger footprint, lower cost, easy maintenance; the default indoor MV lineup for most data centers.
  • Gas-insulated switchgear (GIS): compact, sealed, used where space is tight or the environment is harsh (dust, salt), and at higher voltages. Historically insulated with SF6.

3.2 The SF6 problem and SF6-free alternatives (2025–26)

SF6 100-year GWP is ~23,500 per IPCC AR5-era figure (arXiv 2308.13667: "SF6 has a GWP of about 23500"); IPCC AR6 GWP-100 values are compiled in the GHG Protocol's August 2024 IPCC GWP table (AR6, 2021). Regulation is forcing it out:

  • EU F-Gas Regulation (EU) 2024/573 bans SF6 in new MV switchgear up to 24 kV from January 1, 2026 and up to 52 kV by 2030.
  • US: California and New York have state-level bans on new SF6 gear starting 2025; EPA is tightening reporting.
  • Alternatives: vacuum interruption + dry/clean air (Siemens "blue" GIS, ABB) and fluoronitrile mixtures (C4F7N / Novec 4710, "g3") from GE Vernova/Hitachi Energy. A key US caveat: some SF6-free lines launched 50 Hz IEC-only, not yet 60 Hz US-rated (per EPA assessment) — verify 60 Hz availability. SF6-free MV gear commands a 12–18% price premium (Dataintelo) but is now commercially mainstream, and many operators specify it voluntarily under SBTi commitments.

3.3 Arc-resistant gear (IEEE C37.20.7)

IEEE C37.20.7 tests switchgear for internal arcing faults, redirecting hot gases away from personnel. Accessibility types: Type 1 (front), Type 2 (front/sides/rear), Type 2B (with control-compartment doors open), 2C (between compartments). Data centers specify arc-resistant gear (commonly Type 2B) for personnel safety during energized work — central to concurrent maintainability, where staff operate live gear. It carries a cost and footprint premium over standard metal-clad gear but is near-universal in modern mission-critical MV lineups.

3.4 Protection and selective coordination

The governing principle: a fault must clear at the nearest upstream breaker only, isolating the smallest possible zone. This requires a selective coordination study (relay curves, breaker interrupting ratings, arc-flash) so that downstream devices trip before upstream ones. Breaker types: vacuum (dominant in MV), with microprocessor relays (IEEE device functions 50/51 overcurrent, 87 differential, 27/59 under/over-voltage). Main-tie-main and double-ended line-ups are the physical mechanism of concurrent maintainability: open a main, feed both bus sections through the tie, maintain the de-energized side.

3.5 Delivery and risk

MV switchgear is the other long-lead item — 52–84+ weeks for data-center-spec gear (arc-resistant, paralleling, custom) vs a ~44-week all-class average; 38 kV can reach 96–120 weeks. Under design-build the coordination study is a deliverable someone must explicitly own — usually the EOR or a specialist — and it cannot be finished until the OFE gear ratings are frozen, creating a dependency loop with procurement.

4.2 EPA Tier 2 vs Tier 4 Final

  • Tier 2 is allowed for true emergency standby only. The moment a genset runs for demand response, peak shaving, or bridge power, federal rules push it to Tier 4 Final with SCR (and often DPF) after-treatment — a real capex and footprint adder. This occurred in Virginia in June 2025, when Tier 2 diesels ran for demand response during a heatwave.
  • The air permit shapes testing: allowable run-hours (emergency vs non-emergency), monthly test caps, and reporting obligations are all set in the permit. In non-attainment zones the permit can gate the whole project.

4.3 Sizing and architecture

  • kW vs kVA: generators are rated in both; at ~0.8 pf, 700 kW needs 875 kVA. Data-center load runs ~0.8–0.9 pf. Get pf wrong (assume 0.9, actual 0.75) and you undersize by >15%.
  • Block and step loads / the UPS transient: a UPS-fed load presents a difficult transient to a genset — on transfer, the UPS rectifiers draw a large step plus recharge current, and the genset must accept it without excessive frequency/voltage dip. This drives generator oversizing relative to steady load and careful governor/AVR specs (ISO 8528-5 transient class).
  • N+1 at generator level; paralleling vs isolated-redundant. Paralleling switchgear synchronizes multiple sets onto a common bus (flexible, N+1 economical); isolated-redundant dedicates a standby set. Paralleling gear is itself long-lead.
  • NFPA 110 Type 10: power restored to the transfer switch load terminals within 10 seconds of utility failure — the standard for critical facilities.

4.4 Fuel and testing conventions

  • The "72-hour" convention: many data-center specs and NFPA 110 Level 1 practice target on-site fuel for extended autonomy — NFPA 110 references 96-hour storage for some Level 1 systems; hyperscale commonly targets 24–72 h. This is stricter than Uptime's Tier requirement: Uptime's Tier Standard: Topology requires a 12-hour minimum for all Tiers ("The Uptime Institute Owners Advisory Committee defined 12 hours of minimum fuel storage as a starting point… at 'N' load while meeting the facility's stated topology objective," Uptime Institute Journal, "Data center fuel system design and reliability"). So "72 hours" is an owner/market convention, not an Uptime rule.
  • Belly vs bulk tanks: sub-base ("belly") tanks under each genset for immediate supply; bulk storage plus day tanks for the full autonomy target, with fuel polishing (filtration to prevent microbial/water degradation) and resupply contracts.
  • Testing: monthly no-load/light-load exercise runs and annual load-bank tests (to full rated load) to prevent wet-stacking and prove capacity.

4.5 Delivery and risk

Generators and paralleling gear are OFE long-lead items (large diesels 50–110 weeks; paralleling switchgear at the long end of MV switchgear ranges). The air permit is a gating item — construction can be halted for emissions non-compliance. Under design-build/OFE, delay risk on the genset package typically sits with the owner-supplier; the permit risk sits with whoever holds the air-permit application (often owner + environmental consultant).

Static double-conversion (VFI, per IEC 62040-3) is the workhorse. DRUPS (Piller, Hitec, Euro-Diesel) integrates flywheel + diesel, ride-through 13–40 s, units to ~2,500 kVA paralleled to 30 MVA+.

5.2 Eco-mode and the efficiency-gap debate

Running double-conversion in eco/multi-mode feeds the load through the bypass (conditioned only on disturbance), lifting efficiency from ~94–97% to 98–99%. Schneider's Neil Rasmussen pegs the saving at ~2.3% (~$15,000/yr for a 1 MW site at 50% load, $0.10/kWh). The debate: the load sees raw mains in normal operation, so surge protection becomes mandatory and some operators refuse it for risk reasons; adoption is higher in Europe. Newest VFI designs (SiC devices) reach ~99% without eco-mode, which may make the debate moot. Schneider's eConversion is now the default mode on Galaxy V.

5.3 Modular UPS and the block sweet spot

Modular UPS build from hot-swappable 15–500 kW modules, N+1 in the frame (e.g., Schneider Galaxy VXL 500–1,250 kW; Vertiv, Eaton). The practical block sweet spot in 2025–26 is 2–3 MW — large enough for economy of scale, small enough to be a granular failure/maintenance domain and to match a genset and a unit-substation transformer. This is where the explorer's ~2.5 MW rule is genuinely right for UPS.

5.4 Output voltage

UPS output is either 480/277 V (traditional North America) or 415/240 V (increasingly common for efficiency — 240 V to the server PSU). The choice hands the distribution designer (Topic 6, downstream) the voltage the PDUs and busway must carry. For the reference hall, 480Y/277 V UPS output is the defensible base.

5.5 UPS and AI load

A GPU cluster ramps tens of MW in seconds as training jobs start, checkpoint and stop; synchronized AllReduce operations create periodic second-scale swings, and clusters can hit 150% of steady-state briefly. This matters because the transient can exceed the UPS's ability to source/condition power, forcing it onto stored energy, and can destabilize gensets. Proposed fixes: oversizing UPS, energy buffering (supercaps, extra battery, Tesla Megapack-class BESS targeting swings up to ~30 Hz), software load-smoothing (power capping, dummy workloads, staged ramps — NVIDIA-SMI limits), and grid-side coordination (EPRI DCFlex). This is the fastest-moving design problem in the chain.

5.6 The utility-failure sequence, second by second (reference hall)

For Hall 1 (10 MW IT), indicative time budget to the UPS output:

  1. t = 0: Utility drops. MV detects loss of voltage/frequency.
  2. 0 – ~20 ms: UPS transfers critical load to battery/inverter with no break (VFI); the UPS output bus stays clean throughout. This is the ride-through layer.
  3. ~1–3 s: Generators receive start signal, crank, come up to speed.
  4. ≤10 s: Gensets reach rated voltage/frequency (NFPA 110 Type 10); paralleling gear synchronizes them onto the MV bus.
  5. ~10–15 s: ATS/closed-transition transfer moves the hall's mechanical + UPS-rectifier load onto generator power; UPS batteries stop discharging and begin recharge.
  6. Utility returns: controls verify stable utility for a set time, then retransfer (often closed-transition) back to utility; gensets cool down and stop.

Battery only has to cover steps 2–5, i.e., ~10–15 s of the ~10 min it can supply — the deep margin is for genset start failure and re-tries.

5.7 Delivery

UPS is a long-lead OFE (or CFE) item — 26–52 weeks for 500 kVA–1.5 MW frames in 2026; IGBT/SiC component lead times (26–40 weeks) sit underneath. The factory witness test is the named hand-off. Battery cabinets are a second queue inside the UPS order (§6).

6.2 Codes: NFPA 855 and UL 9540A

  • UL 9540A is the fire-propagation test method; UL 9540 is the system listing; NFPA 855 is the installation standard. NFPA 855 requires any Li-ion system >20 kWh to be UL 9540 listed and UL 9540A tested.
  • NFPA 855 editions: 2020, 2023, 2026 (released Sept 2025). The binding edition is whatever the AHJ adopted (IFC 2021 → 855-2020; IFC 2024 → 855-2023). It sets spacing (≈3 ft), per-unit kWh caps, ventilation, deflagration/explosion control, suppression (sprinklers per NFPA 13), and separation — but UL 9540A large-scale-fire-test data can justify closer spacing and larger units. The 2026 edition shifts from prescriptive tables toward an analysis-and-testing approach.
  • Layout impact: these codes moved Li-ion out of the white space into dedicated, ventilated, fire-rated battery rooms with explosion control — a real change to UPS room layout vs the old VRLA-in-the-UPS-room norm. Clean-agent suppression (Novec/FM-200) does not stop thermal runaway because the reaction self-supplies oxygen.

6.3 Delivery and approval risk

The owner (OFE) or UPS vendor buys/installs battery cabinets; warranty and end-of-life/recycling (especially retired VRLA) must be assigned. The sharpest risk is approval when the AHJ is new to lithium: because UL 9540A has a test method but historically no fixed pass/fail targets, AHJs interpret results locally, causing "a protracted period" of case-by-case review (Data Center Frontier). Budget schedule for AHJ education.

6.4 BESS beyond the UPS

Larger battery energy storage (beyond ride-through) is entering the one-line for peak shaving, demand response, grid services, and AI-transient smoothing:

  • Peak shaving/demand charges: a BESS discharges behind the meter to hold grid import below a target. Example economics (C&I): a 5 MW/10 MWh BESS cut a facility's demand charges 35%, saving >$500k/yr (SunLith, 2025). But 24/7 data centers have no off-peak trough, so classic load-shifting is weaker than for intermittent loads.
  • Flexible interconnection: a Duke University study (Feb 2025) found ~100 GW of new load could connect with minimal grid impact if flexible; BESS makes that feasible without curtailing uptime. This is the strongest 2026 economic case — it can shorten the interconnection wait (median ~5 years; some PJM >8).
  • Position in the one-line: BESS sits at the MV bus (grid-services/peak-shaving) or at the UPS/LV level (transient smoothing). Fluence and Tesla Megapack are targeting the data-center-buffer role.
  • Do the economics work? For peak shaving alone at a flat 24/7 load, marginally. For interconnection acceleration + transient smoothing + demand response stacking, increasingly yes in 2025–26.

6.5 The boundary: what "clean" means at the UPS output bus

Topic 5 ends and Topic 6's downstream distribution begins at the UPS output bus. "Clean" there means, indicatively:

  • Voltage: regulated 480Y/277 V (or 415/240 V), ±1% steady-state, tight transient bounds.
  • Frequency: 60 Hz, tightly held (VFI output is frequency-independent of input).
  • Waveform: low THD sine (typically <2–3% THDv), conditioned regardless of upstream disturbances.
  • Availability: continuous through utility loss (battery ride-through) and genset transfer — this is the whole point of the chain.

The distribution designer downstream inherits a bus that is always energized, always in tolerance, and coordinated — and must maintain that A/B independence and selectivity from there to the rack (out of scope here).

8. Worked Example: Phase 1 One-Line, Fence to UPS Output (10 MW IT, Hall 1)

All numbers indicative. Arithmetic chain:

  1. IT load: 10 MW. × PUE 1.4 = 14 MW hall load. Critical (UPS) ≈10 MW; mechanical ≈4 MW.
  2. Service voltage: campus takes 138 kV at the customer substation (sized for the full ≈56 MW build, not just Phase 1).
  3. LPTs: campus substation with 2 × 30 MVA (N+1) 138/34.5 kV oil-filled LPTs energized day one (concurrent maintainability at the substation from Phase 1). Phase 1 draws ~16 MVA of that.
  4. Campus MV: 34.5 kV double-ended (main-tie-main) MV switchgear, arc-resistant (IEEE C37.20.7 Type 2B), SF6-free.
  5. Generators: Phase 1 critical+mechanical ≈14 MW. Using 2.5–3.0 MW diesel gensets (Cat C175-16 / Cummins DQKAN class) at N+1: 14 ÷ 2.75 ≈ 5.1 → 6 × 2.75 MW gensets (5 needed + 1) on paralleling switchgear, NFPA 110 Type 10, Tier 4F or Tier 2+HVO per air permit, 24–72 h fuel.
  6. Unit substations: 34.5 kV → 480Y/277 V, 2,500 kVA dry-type, 5.75–6% Z. Hall load 14 MW ÷ (2.5 MVA × 0.9) ≈ 6.2 → 7–8 unit substations incl. N+1. Secondary fault ≈50 kA → breakers rated ≥65 kA AIC.
  7. UPS blocks: critical 10 MW at 2–3 MW blocks: 10 ÷ 2.5 = 4 → 5 × 2.5 MW UPS (4 needed + 1, N+1), double-conversion VFI, 480Y/277 V output.
  8. Battery: LFP lithium, 5 minutes at full load per block, UL 9540A-tested, in dedicated NFPA 855 rooms.

Tier III concurrent maintainability choices: double-ended MV switchgear (maintain one main/bus at a time via tie); N+1 at LPT, genset, unit-substation and UPS levels; dual-corded downstream (out of scope). The optional 2N suite doubles the UPS/distribution path for one hall.

Growth to 40 MW without stranding Phase 1: the 138 kV substation and 2 × 30 MVA LPTs are sized for the full build from day one (add a third 30 MVA LPT as load passes ~30 MW to keep N+1). Each subsequent ~10 MW hall repeats the Phase-1 block — its own unit substations, genset group and UPS blocks off the shared 34.5 kV double-ended bus — so Phase 1 equipment is never resized or replaced, only joined. This is why the power block is sized to the diversified design load per hall, not the campus nameplate sum.

9. Verdict on the Explorer's Two Rules

Rule 1 — "UPS and generator come in ~2.5 MW blocks; N+1 blocks = ceil(IT MW ÷ 2.5) + 1." - Right for UPS: 2–3 MW is the genuine 2025–26 UPS block sweet spot; 2.5 MW is a fair midpoint. The N+1 formula is a reasonable planning heuristic for UPS blocks. - Wrong/incomplete for generators: modern data-center diesels cluster at 2.25–3.25 MW (Cummins DQKAN 2.5 MW, Cat C175-16 3.0 MW, Rehlko KD3250 3.25 MW, Generac 2.25–3.25 MW), so a genset is roughly one per UPS block (~1:1), but gensets must carry mechanical load too, so the genset count is driven by total hall load (IT × PUE), not IT alone. Corrected: UPS blocks = ceil(IT MW ÷ 2.5) + 1; gensets = ceil((IT × PUE) MW ÷ ~2.75) + 1. For 10 MW IT: 5 UPS blocks, ~6 gensets — not 5 of each.

Rule 2 — "Grid connection by load: <15 MW distribution 12–35 kV; 15–80 MW dedicated substation 69–138 kV; >80 MW transmission 230 kV+." - Roughly right at the boundaries, but the middle band is too wide and the top threshold too high for 2026. Corrected pattern: <~15 MW → distribution 12.47–35 kV; ~15–50 MW → dedicated customer substation at 69–138 kV; ~50–150 MW → 138–230 kV; >~200 MW → 230–345 kV. The reference 56 MW campus lands firmly in the 115–138 kV dedicated-substation band — the rule's "15–80 MW → 69–138 kV" would put it there too, so the rule isn't wrong for this facility, but it overstates how far 138 kV stretches (many 60–80 MW sites now go to 230 kV) and understates how early transmission-level interconnect appears. Every utility sets its own thresholds; the interconnection study governs.

10. Open Questions and What's Changing Fast in 2026

  • SF6-free gear: mainstream in EU (Jan 1 2026 ban at ≤24 kV) but 60 Hz US availability lags for some lines — a real specification risk this year.
  • Lithium share: crossing from challenger to default at hyperscale (~55%); VRLA still majority overall. NFPA 855 2026 edition's shift to analysis-based approval could accelerate or complicate this depending on AHJ uptake.
  • Gas as primary power: the biggest structural shift — multi-GW prime/bridge gas orders (INNIO–VoltaGrid, Wärtsilä 507 MW) as interconnection queues stall. Whether this is a bridge or a permanent architecture is the open question.
  • AI transients: no settled design standard yet. Oversizing, energy buffering, and software smoothing all compete; EPRI DCFlex and vendor BESS (Tesla, Fluence) are racing to define best practice.
  • Lead times: the master variable. No near-term relief for LPTs (128 wk) and MV switchgear; OEM capacity expansions (Siemens Georgia plant, ABB vacuum-interrupter line) won't bite until 2027+.
  • DOE transformer rule: the CRA reversal of Dec-2024 amendments (Sept 2025) leaves the exact forward efficiency floor genuinely uncertain — confirm the live CFR text at order time.

11. Glossary

  • Demarcation point: the ownership boundary between utility and customer, usually at the incoming structure or first breaker.
  • Large power transformer (LPT): HV→MV substation transformer, typically 30–100 MVA, oil-filled, outdoor.
  • Unit substation: packaged MV→LV (e.g., 34.5 kV→480 V) transformer + switchgear feeding UPS/mechanical.
  • %Z (impedance): transformer property setting fault current (≈ kVA ÷ %Z) and voltage regulation.
  • Main-tie-main / double-ended: two incoming sources and a bus tie enabling maintenance of one side without load loss.
  • AIS / GIS: air- vs gas-insulated switchgear.
  • SF6-free: MV switchgear using vacuum + clean/dry air or fluoronitrile (g3) instead of SF6.
  • Arc-resistant (IEEE C37.20.7): switchgear tested to vent internal-arc energy away from personnel.
  • Selective coordination: protection scheme where the nearest upstream breaker clears a fault, isolating the smallest zone.
  • NFPA 110 Type 10: emergency power restored to the transfer switch within 10 seconds.
  • Prime / standby rating: continuous-duty vs emergency-only generator rating.
  • HVO: hydrotreated vegetable oil, a drop-in renewable diesel (~90% lifecycle CO2 cut).
  • VFI / VI / VFD: IEC 62040-3 UPS classes — double-conversion / line-interactive / offline.
  • Eco-mode: high-efficiency UPS mode feeding load through bypass, ~98–99%.
  • DRUPS: diesel rotary UPS — flywheel + diesel, no battery.
  • VRLA / LFP / NMC: valve-regulated lead-acid / lithium iron phosphate / nickel-manganese-cobalt batteries.
  • UL 9540A: fire-propagation test method for energy storage; NFPA 855: installation standard.
  • BESS: battery energy storage system (beyond UPS ride-through).
  • OFE / CFE: owner-furnished / contractor-furnished equipment.
  • Diversified design load: the realistic simultaneous peak, less than the nameplate sum, used to size the power block.

12. What Surprised the Researcher

  1. Lead time, not engineering, is the story. In 2026 the 128-week transformer and 84-week switchgear queues dictate the schedule more than any design choice; "reserve the slot before you finish the drawing" is now standard advice.
  2. Uptime requires nothing of the utility. The Tier standard treats the grid as unreliable by definition and counts only on-site generation — so the expensive "dual transmission feed" is an economic choice, not a Tier requirement.
  3. The DOE transformer efficiency rule was partly reversed in Sept 2025 under the Congressional Review Act — a genuine live uncertainty a specifier must check, not the settled march-to-efficiency the industry assumed.
  4. Gas went from backup to primary. Multi-GW prime-power gas orders for AI campuses (Oracle/VoltaGrid 2.3 GW) mark a structural break from the diesel-standby-only model — driven entirely by interconnection queues, not fuel economics.
  5. AI load can exceed the UPS's ability to condition it, pushing it onto stored energy 150% above steady state — the load is now stressing the very equipment meant to protect it, and there's no settled standard fix yet.
  6. Batteries shrank to 5 minutes. Reliable 10-second genset starts made hours of autonomy pointless; the modern battery just covers the transfer, and lithium's real selling point is footprint and cycle life, not runtime.
