From Empty Field to Energized Substation: Siting, Grid, Water, Incentives and Long-Lead Procurement for a New-Build Data Center (2025–2026)

The Desktop Research report for Topic 3, run 2026-09-24 from briefs/ and published as delivered: no editing, no lint. The chapter and appendix were distilled from it; where they and this report disagree, the chapter is the course's position.

TL;DR - Power is now the whole game. As of 2025–26, deliverable megawatts on a credible timeline is the number-one site-selection criterion — CBRE says the ability to secure 300-MW-plus deliveries in under 36 months now supersedes fiber, which was traditionally #1. Grid interconnection is a multi-year contractual/engineering process (roughly 2–5+ years for large transmission-level loads) and is almost always the critical path; everything else — land, water, permits, procurement — runs in parallel behind it. - You commit money before you have certainty, and you order equipment before design is finished. Large power transformers run ~128 weeks (Wood Mackenzie Q2 2025), generators and switchgear 50–110+ weeks, so developers reserve factory slots with 10–30% deposits at land-close, often 6–12 months before groundbreaking. Water and community/political risk have become gating "can-you-even-build-here" screens, not afterthoughts. - The regulatory ground is shifting fast. In 2025–26 utilities and regulators imposed hard financial guardrails on large loads (Virginia's $1.5M/MW collateral and 14-year GS-5 contracts; AEP Ohio's 85% take-or-pay; Georgia's 100-MW customized-contract rule; ERCOT's SB 6), interconnection queues ballooned to multiples of system peak (ERCOT ~230 GW, Dominion ~70 GW), and behind-the-meter gas went from curiosity to mainstream bridging strategy. Expect the rules to keep changing under you.

1. What a Site Has to Provide, in Priority Order

1.1 The 2025–26 ranking: power first, and it isn't close

For a self-learner, the single most important mental adjustment is this: data-center site selection has inverted. For decades the checklist led with fiber/connectivity; land and power were assumed solvable. That assumption is dead.

CBRE's 2026 outlook states it plainly: "Power availability and certainty are now primary site selection criteria, overshadowing traditional connectivity considerations… Fiber availability and network redundancy, long the top of the checklist, are now secondary to power cost and delivery timelines." CBRE frames the new bar as the ability to secure 300-MW-plus deliveries in under 36 months. JLL's 2026 Global Data Center Outlook similarly calls power "the primary site-selection factor, with average grid-connection wait times in primary markets exceeding four years." Bloom Energy's 2025 Data Center Power Report Mid-Year Update (released June 17, 2025), a survey of 44 hyperscaler and colocation developers, found "availability of power was the number one consideration for new site selection, with 84% of respondents placing that in the top 3" at an average rating of 7.8/10. Note: Bloom's later Nov 2025 survey (N=92) found the top-three share dropped to 51%.

The practitioner ranking in 2025–26, with evidence: 1. Power — availability, timeline, and cost (CBRE, JLL, Bloom Energy, above). The industry term is "power-first development." 2. Fiber/network — still essential but demoted (CBRE H2 2025; see §1.4). 3. Land — large, contiguous, appropriately zoned tracts (§1.2). 4. Water — now a gating screen in many basins (§3). 5. Hazard exposure — flood/seismic/wildfire/heat (§1.3). 6. Tax/incentive climate — a tiebreaker, occasionally decisive (§4). 7. Workforce — construction labor and O&M staffing. 8. Community acceptance / political risk — now a formal screen after $64B+ of projects were blocked or delayed (§4.3).

A useful synthesis from CBRE H1 2025: developers "are now sourcing parcels within close proximity to substations and locking in power purchase agreements before finalizing land acquisition. The most sought-after sites are those that can offer power within 18-24 months." Note the tension: CBRE quotes an 18–24-month aspiration for the best sites while JLL cites 4+ year waits in primary markets — both are true, describing best-case powered-land sites versus typical constrained-market timelines. I trust both; the gap is the story.

1.2 Land: acreage per MW, contiguity, and why "industrial-zoned" isn't enough

Rules of thumb (2025–26): - Acreage: A single large building might sit on 20–50 acres; a 100-MW facility "might occupy roughly 20 to over 100 acres" depending on density (Passive Investments, summarizing developer practice). Multi-phase hyperscale campuses assemble 100–1,000+ acres. Cushman & Wakefield data (via TechTarget) shows the average data-center land transaction reached 224 acres in 2024, up 144% since 2022; weighted-average land cost was ~$244,000/acre as of October 2024 (Gravel2Gavel). - Density is rising, which can reduce acres per MW as rack densities climb (CBRE notes immersion/direct-to-chip could shrink footprints), but power, not floor space, is the binding number. - Contiguity matters more than raw acreage: "large, unbroken tracts are far more valuable than the same acreage split by roads, easements, or separate parcels" (Passive Investments). Room for future phases and on-site substation/switchyard is assumed.

Why industrial zoning alone is insufficient: A data center is typically permitted under industrial zoning or a purpose-built data-center designation, but zoning is only the first gate. Even industrial-zoned land routinely requires rezoning, special/conditional-use permits, height variances (cooling equipment and switchgear are tall), noise-ordinance compliance, and setbacks — and CBRE's 2025 outlook explicitly warns that development pushing outside urban cores "will require rezoning and entitlements." The land can be zoned correctly and still be undevelopable because of power, water, flood exposure, or an organized community that turns the conditional-use hearing into a denial (see §4.3). Industrial zoning gets you to the starting line, not across it.

1.3 Climate and natural hazards in site scoring

Hazard exposure enters scoring as both a hard disqualifier and a soft cost driver: - Flood: FEMA floodways and high-risk (100-year / Special Flood Hazard Area) zones are "generally disqualifying"; even partial exposure "complicates insurance, financing, and tenant approval" (Passive Investments). Serious developers screen to the 500-year (0.2%-annual-chance) floodplain for mission-critical assets. - Seismic, wind, wildfire: Seismic zones raise structural cost and are avoided where possible; wildfire-prone areas threaten both the asset and, importantly, wildfire smoke, which fouls air-side economizers and forces air intake to close, degrading free-cooling. Extreme-heat exposure raises cooling energy and water use precisely when the grid is most stressed. - Cooling economics at the site stage — "free-cooling hours": Climate directly affects operating cost via how many hours a year the site can cool without mechanical refrigeration. Cooler/drier climates yield more economizer hours and lower PUE; hot-arid climates (Phoenix) push toward either high water use (evaporative) or higher energy (air-cooled) — a documented tradeoff: "Phoenix measurements show water-cooled chillers achieve lower energy but higher water use, and air-cooled systems the inverse" (arXiv survey of 15 US climate zones). At the site stage the reader only needs to know this shapes the water-vs-energy decision; the plant design comes later.

1.4 Fiber and latency

Even demoted, connectivity is still a real screen: - Carrier diversity: You want multiple carriers, not one, to avoid monopoly pricing and single-provider outage risk. - Two physically diverse fiber entries: The bedrock requirement is two physically separate fiber paths entering the campus from different directions, so a single backhoe or cut cannot isolate the facility. "Diverse" on paper but sharing one conduit or one bridge crossing is a classic hidden single point of failure. - Distance to internet exchanges / long-haul routes: Proximity to major exchange points and long-haul routes reduces latency and backhaul cost. Notably, hyperscaler/AI demand is now creating fiber where it was thin — CBRE H2 2025 documents providers (Fiberlight, Zayo, Flo Networks and others) building "thousands of route miles of new fiber" in West Texas to connect new power-rich sites to core networks. This is why power-rich but fiber-poor locations are now viable: the fiber follows the power.

2. Grid Interconnection as a Multi-Year Process

2.1 This is a LOAD interconnection — and why that distinction matters

A data center is a large load, not a generator. This matters because the two processes have different owners and legal homes. As White & Case summarized the 2025 state of play: "interconnection processes for new generating facilities are under FERC jurisdiction while the interconnection of large loads is conducted at the state level." Generator interconnection was standardized federally (FERC Order 2023, cluster studies); load interconnection historically had no such uniform process — RMI and GridLab both note utilities have handled large loads "on an ad hoc basis," which is exactly what broke down under the AI-driven surge. They overlap where a large load triggers the same transmission network upgrades a generator would, and increasingly where projects file co-located load + generation together (a 2025–26 innovation pushed by DOE/FERC).

2.2 The steps, and typical durations (2024–26)

Drawing on RMI's six-step framing and utility/EPC sources, a transmission-level load interconnection runs: 1. Application / load study request — developer (usually via the local utility) submits to the transmission owner, who coordinates with the RTO/ISO. AEP Ohio's tariff illustrates real fees: tiered study fees of $10,000 / $50,000 / $100,000 by request size. 2. Load / feasibility study — can existing facilities serve the load? 3. System impact study (SIS) — models voltage, thermal, and reliability effects; identifies required network upgrades. This is where big surprises (and big bills) appear. 4. Facilities study — engineers the specific substation, tie lines, metering, and prices them. 5. Interconnection / construction agreement — fixes scope, cost responsibility, milestones, and the energization date; triggers major financial security/collateral. 6. Construction and energization — utility builds its upgrades; customer builds on-site substation/switchyard; testing, utility witness testing, energization.

Durations: Sources converge on twelve months to more than three years for the process, and JLL/LBNL data push typical total time-to-power in primary markets to 4–5+ years. LBNL's "Queued Up 2025" (generator data, but the wires constraint is identical) is a sobering backdrop: of capacity that requested interconnection 2000–2019, only 13% had reached commercial operation by end-2024; 77% withdrew. The lesson developers repeat: treat interconnection as a "day-one workstream," file early, and overlap the study phases with substation design and long-lead procurement.

2.3 Who does what, and who pays

  • Roles: The developer/customer builds and often owns the on-site substation and pays for utility-side work attributable to its load. The local utility / transmission owner performs studies, owns/builds network upgrades, and serves as lead processor. The RTO/ISO (PJM, ERCOT, etc.) coordinates transmission-level impacts and regional planning. Where there's no RTO (much of the Southeast, West), the vertically integrated utility does it all.
  • What the developer pays — CIAC: Contribution-in-aid-of-construction is the non-refundable payment a customer makes toward utility-owned facilities built to serve it — dedicated substation, transmission taps, feeders, metering. Indicative magnitudes (Terrapin CG, 2026): $75k–$400k for 1–3 MW distribution service; $400k–$2.5M for 5–20 MW with a line extension; $3M–$30M+ when a dedicated substation or transmission tap is required. For hyperscale loads the CIAC can run to eight or nine figures.
  • Refund mechanisms: Classic CIAC tariffs refund a portion if other customers later attach to the extension the first customer funded (e.g., 30% refunds within 3 years; per-customer refunds up to the original contribution). For large loads these are increasingly blended: part non-refundable CIAC, part refundable if minimum-load thresholds are met. Texas SB 6's proposed standard is instructive: a $50,000/MW non-refundable interconnection fee, customer pays 100% of direct interconnection costs (radial lines, substations) with no usage credit, and on withdrawal the utility keeps 80% of posted security (20% refunded).

2.4 Substation options: utility- vs customer-owned, transmission- vs distribution-level

  • Service level by load size (rule of thumb): Below ~20 MW you may take distribution-level service; most facilities in the 20–50 MW range and above require a dedicated substation, and 100+ MW campuses almost always need transmission-level service with dual feeds (American Power Engineers, 2026). Transmission-level means interconnecting at 115–345 kV (or higher) rather than distribution primary (typically ≤ ~35 kV).
  • Ownership: A customer-owned substation gives the developer schedule control (you can procure and build faster than the utility) but puts the transformer/switchgear procurement risk on you. A utility-owned substation shifts that build to the utility — but the utility's queue and procurement then set your date, and you fund it via CIAC. In 2025–26, schedule-desperate developers increasingly self-build and self-procure to escape the utility's own equipment backlog.

2.5 Large-load tariffs and the 2025–26 regulatory response

This is the fastest-moving area in the whole report. Utilities and PUCs, alarmed by speculative queues many times system peak, imposed hard financial guardrails. As of September 2026, EEI's tracker showed 25 states had approved at least one large-load tariff, with 7 more pending; the CoBank/DELTa database counted large-load tariffs rising from ~20 approved in 2024 to 53+ by 2025. Common features: minimum-take (take-or-pay) provisions, long contract terms (10–20 years), heavy collateral, ramp schedules, and exit fees.

Where queues actually stand and what the rules now say:

Northern Virginia / Dominion (PJM DOM zone): Dominion's large-load queue reached ~70 GW of delivery-point requests (Feb 2, 2026 SCC filing) — nearly 3× its all-time peak of 24,678 MW (set Jan 23, 2025). About 25 GW has projected connection dates through 2031; ~45 GW is still under study. The formal queue process applies at ~100 MW+. Virginia's SCC approved a new GS-5 rate class (Nov 25, 2025) for customers ≥25 MW taking service after Jan 1, 2027: 14-year contracts, pay for ≥85% of reserved transmission/distribution capacity and 60% of reserved generation regardless of use, and collateral of $1.5 million per MW. The Data Center Coalition warned the queue as proposed "can take up to 15 years to cycle through."

Texas / ERCOT: The large-load queue exploded to ~230–233 GW by late 2025 (ERCOT board, Dec 9, 2025), up ~300% year-over-year from 63 GW at end-2024; >70% is data centers, and only ~7.5 GW had actually connected. (A later Utility Dive headline cited a 438-GW figure as the queue kept growing.) Senate Bill 6 (signed June 21, 2025) applies to loads ≥75 MW: standardized interconnection, mandatory remote-disconnect ("kill switch") capability for ERCOT emergencies, demand-management participation, and study/deposit rules. ERCOT moved to batch cluster studies (Batch Zero), with a final transmission plan expected fall 2027.

Columbus / AEP Ohio (PJM): After a 28-month moratorium on new data-center connections, PUCO approved AEP Ohio's landmark Data Center Tariff on July 9, 2025 for new loads >25 MW: minimum demand charge up to 85% of contracted capacity, ~12-year contract terms (some reporting minimum 8 years after a 4-year ramp), collateral for un-rated customers, and exit fees of ~3 years of minimum charges. The speculative queue reportedly dropped from ~30 GW to ~13 GW once firm financial commitments were required — a clean illustration of how much of a "queue" is speculative.

Atlanta / Georgia Power (vertically integrated, Georgia PSC): The development pipeline swelled from ~12 GW to 36.5 GW during 2024, but this is highly speculative — since Q2 2024, 19 canceled/withdrawn AI projects accounted for 83% of the MW removed from the queue. Georgia Power's 2025 IRP (PSC-approved July 15, 2025) forecasts ~8,500 MW of new load growth through ~2030–31, ~90% from data centers; the PSC on Dec 19, 2025 certified 9,885 MW of new generation to serve it. The 100-MW rule (effective Feb 1, 2025) forces any new ≥100 MW customer into a customized 15-year contract with minimum-billing, collateral, and termination costs, filed with the PSC ≥30 days before signing. Actual contracted load was far smaller — ~2 GW across 29 customers (incl. QTS, Meta, Microsoft, Google, X, Walmart) by late 2025.

Phoenix / APS & SRP: APS reported ~10 GW of pending data-center requests (Nov 2024) it "cannot commit to serving," later refined to 4.5 GW committed + ~20 GW potential; current data-center load is only ~400–500 MW. APS's June 2025 rate case sought a ~47% revenue increase for its extra-large-load (XHLF) class. SRP's story is the sharpest queue-attrition example on record: per Pinal Post (Aug 2026), SRP's first cluster study (Oct 2025) identified "83 required network upgrades across the SRP, APS and WAPA transmission systems, totaling $12.8 billion"; each project had to post "a deposit covering 30 percent of its share of those costs, as an irrevocable letter of credit, by Feb. 2"; a July 15 restudy "cut the needed upgrades from 83 to eight and the transmission bill from $12.8 billion to $1.5 billion," with the combined request falling from 7.2 GW to ~1 GW (three projects). SRP's actual 2025 data-center peak was 441 MW (5.1% of its 8,542-MW system peak). SRP's E-67 plan requires ≥20 MW customers to meet minimum billing tied to 80% of forecast demand.

Federal overlay: DOE directed FERC (Oct 23, 2025 letter) to accelerate large-load interconnection and allow joint co-located load+generation filings; FERC took "aggressive targeted action" after its June 18, 2026 meeting, directing the six RTOs to write region-specific large-load rules. The direction of travel is faster studies but firmer financial commitments.

Non-US markets (site-decision relevance): - Dublin: EirGrid's de-facto moratorium on new Dublin grid connections (in place since 2021–22) was lifted in December 2025 — but new data centers must now install on-site generation or storage able to meet full demand and export to the grid when needed, plus (per some reporting) source ~80% of energy from new Irish renewables. Data centers already consume ~21–24% of Irish electricity. - Amsterdam: The Netherlands' 2019–2020 moratorium was lifted but left lasting drag — total Dutch data centers actually fell (189 in 2019 → 187 in 2023) and Amsterdam added far less new capacity than rival FLAP-D markets. - Singapore: The 2019 moratorium was lifted in phases from 2022; the DC-CFA process now awards limited capacity (initial ~80 MW to four operators; later tranches ~120–300 MW) against strict sustainability gates (PUE ≤1.3, 100% RECs, liquid cooling for ≥60% of IT load). Growth is now quality-constrained, not volume-driven.

2.6 Behind-the-meter and bridging power

Because the grid can't deliver fast enough, on-site generation went from niche to mainstream in a single year. Cleanview found 92% of the behind-the-meter (BTM) projects it identified (~82 GW) were announced since the start of 2025; AI labs alone have signed leases for >10 GW of BTM power. The logic (Green Gas Turbines, Jefferies): grid interconnection takes 4–7 years, but a gas plant can be permitted and built in 18–30 months, so on-site generation is the "bridge" to grid power — and increasingly permanent prime power.

  • Technologies: simple-cycle/aeroderivative and reciprocating gas engines (VoltaGrid's modular RICE units can reach 200 MW "under a minor source air permit"; GE Vernova is supplying 29 aeroderivative turbines (~1 GW) to Project Stargate), fuel cells (Bloom), and batteries (BESS) to bridge the 30–120 seconds between a grid event and turbine start.
  • Costs (indicative): A 50-MW simple-cycle aeroderivative install runs ~$40–60M including balance-of-plant; broader estimates span ~$0.7M–$1.4M/MW depending on simple- vs combined-cycle plus site-specific balance-of-plant and gas interconnection.
  • Permitting is the catch: BTM does not escape air permitting. The binding constraint is often the air permit — Microsoft/Nebius's 400-MW onsite gas plant in New Jersey has struggled to obtain one, jeopardizing tranches of a $17.4B deal. Tier 4 emissions packages add queue time; "minor source" permits (staying under thresholds) are the fast path.
  • Genset supply is itself rationed: Per Cummins executive Jonas Arens (MarketBeat/Yahoo Finance, Feb 2026): "Cummins is taking orders for its QSK95 generator solution into the second half of 2028," with customers unable to obtain the 95-liter unit "selecting smaller 78-liter, 60-liter and 50-liter options, reflecting supply constraints for the larger engine through the second half of 2028." Cummins targets 55 GW of high-horsepower capacity by 2030. Caterpillar CEO Joe Creed, on the Q1 2026 earnings call (April 30, 2026), reported a record enterprise backlog of "$63 billion, up 79%" year-over-year (~$28B higher than Q1 2025), driven largely by the data-center-fed Power & Energy segment; Creed noted "customers are committing to longer-term orders with some orders well into 2028." So BTM trades a grid queue for an equipment queue.

2.7 Power purchase and renewable matching (only as it affects siting)

PPAs affect where you build in three ways relevant here: (1) developers now lock PPAs before finalizing land (CBRE H1 2025), making renewable/firm-generation proximity a siting factor; (2) 24/7 matching and additionality commitments (Google, Microsoft) push developers toward grids/regions where new clean generation can actually be added, interacting with interconnection; and (3) new tariff structures like Georgia's Customer Identified Resource (CIR/"bring-your-own-clean-energy," approved April 2026, capped at 3 GW through 2035) and Nevada clean-transition tariffs let large customers fund their own generation to unlock service. Detailed sustainability accounting is out of scope.

3. Water: Sourcing, Discharge and Community Limits

3.1 Where the water goes, and how much

The site team needs to understand cooling only at the level of water consumed, which is driven by cooling architecture: - Evaporative cooling towers (open-loop): the historical default; highly water-intensive. Uptime Institute benchmarks a conventional evaporative system at "millions of gallons of water per year for each megawatt of IT load." Typically 70–80% of withdrawn water is lost to evaporation (consumptive); 20–30% leaves as blowdown/effluent. - Closed-loop: circulates sealed fluid, rejecting heat to air; consumes only ~5–10% of an equivalent open-loop system's withdrawal. Vantage's Wisconsin closed-loop campus: peak ~22,000 gal/day (≈65 homes) vs. ~5,000,000 gal/day for a comparable evaporative campus. - Air-cooled chillers / "zero-water": almost no on-site water, at an energy penalty. Microsoft's Aug 2024 zero-water design eliminates evaporative cooling and saves ~33 million gallons/facility/year (deployed in Phoenix). - Liquid-to-chip: removes heat at the chip but the facility must still reject it, often via evaporative or dry coolers — so it doesn't automatically eliminate water.

Indicative numbers (WUE, liters per kWh): hyperscale water-efficient designs 0.09–1.80 L/kWh; traditional evaporative 1.99–4.00 L/kWh; industry average often cited ~1.8–1.9 L/kWh; AWS reports ~0.15 L/kWh (arXiv survey; Introl; Avanza). At facility scale, a medium evaporative site consumes ~300,000 gal/day; large hyperscale in hot climates 1–5 million gal/day (Google Council Bluffs withdrew ~3.9 M gal/day, consuming ~2.8 M). Consumptive vs non-consumptive is the key distinction for communities: withdrawal that returns to the system stresses infrastructure; evaporated water is gone locally until it rains.

3.2 Sourcing options

  • Municipal potable: simplest and highest-quality (less scaling/corrosion), and per a CRS-cited analysis, public water systems supplied ~97% of US data centers' onsite water as of 2025. But it's the most politically exposed (competes with drinking water).
  • Reclaimed / greywater ("purple pipe"): increasingly preferred where infrastructure exists; requires a separate non-potable line and may have competing users.
  • Groundwater / surface withdrawals: may require the developer to hold water rights directly and face state/local permitting.
  • On-site storage: buffers peak-day demand.
  • Water rights — western US vs elsewhere: Most western states use prior appropriation ("first in time, first in right") — rights are assigned by priority date regardless of land ownership, and junior rights holders are curtailed first in a shortage. A data center on junior rights faces real curtailment and financing risk. Eastern states use the riparian doctrine (reasonable use tied to waterfront land). California and others are hybrids. This is why Southwest projects run "aquifer-specific modeling, sustainable yield analysis… priority, transferability, place-of-use limitations and curtailment history" during diligence (Gravel2Gavel).

3.3 Discharge and permitting

  • Blowdown (concentrated mineral-laden water purged from evaporative loops) must be discharged, typically to sewer or surface water. Surface discharge requires an NPDES-type permit (National Pollutant Discharge Elimination System, under the Clean Water Act) with temperature and chemistry limits — thermal pollution and biocides/anti-scalants are the concerns.
  • Sewer capacity is a hidden constraint: wastewater capacity scales roughly linearly with withdrawal. One under-construction facility was allocated 8 MGD water / 4 MGD wastewater; a smaller one 2 MGD / 0.57 MGD (arXiv). A municipality with water but no sewer headroom can still stop a project.

3.4 Community response and what developers changed

Water is the single most effective rallying point for opposition. Documented 2023–26 cases and responses: - Moratoria/denials tied to water: the Ypsilanti Community Utilities Authority (Michigan) imposed a 12-month moratorium on supplying water to data centers (April 2026); numerous rezoning denials cite water (Data Center Watch catalogs many). - Developer response — "zero-water" and WUE commitments: Microsoft's zero-water design (2024), Vantage's closed-loop Wisconsin campus, AWS's 0.15 L/kWh, and Oracle's closed-loop AI centers are all explicitly positioned as answers to water opposition. Avanza's framing: on water, "the technical problem is already solved" at the frontier (12× gap between average and best-in-class) — the challenge is deploying it and making binding commitments before opposition forms.

4. Taxes, Incentives and Permitting Risk

4.1 Property-tax abatements and sales-tax exemptions

Incentives are usually a tiebreaker, occasionally decisive, and almost always negotiated in advance and conditioned on investment/job/wage targets: - Sales-tax exemptions on equipment (servers, switchgear, sometimes electricity) are the most common tool. The scale is enormous: in FY2025, Virginia data-center operators reported ~$33.2 billion of exempt equipment/software investment, for a reported ~$1.9 billion single-year tax benefit — an exemption originally projected to cost $1.5M/year. - Property-tax abatements / PILOTs are usually local (county/IDA), and can value equipment at salvage (West Virginia's 2025 law: ~5% of value). Nevada offers up to 75% abatements; Texas pairs a 10–15-year sales-tax exemption (≥$200M investment, ≥20 jobs) with local Chapter 312 property abatements. - Per-MW worth: highly variable and best expressed via the reported aggregates above; a rough read is that sales-tax exemption on the ~$25–40M/MW of equipment in an AI build can be worth low-single-digit $M/MW where state+local rates approach ~6–8%. I treat all single-number "$/MW incentive" claims cautiously because property tax is administered locally and often hidden in NDAs. - States reconsidering (2025–26): the incentive tide is turning. Florida (June 30, 2025) limited its exemption to ≥100 MW IT-load facilities; Virginia's exemption is now stalling state budget negotiations with Democrats seeking to redirect the revenue; 300+ state data-center bills were filed across 30+ states in early 2026, shifting from incentives toward oversight; New York's Responsible Data Center Development Act passed both chambers June 5, 2026 (awaiting the governor's signature — would be the first statewide moratorium).

4.2 Permitting sequence and where projects stall

Typical sequence: zoning/rezoning → conditional/special-use permit → environmental review → site-plan approval → building permits → air permits (generators/BTM) → noise/other operating permits. Where projects actually stall: - The rezoning / conditional-use hearing is the most common kill point — it's the public's veto moment, and organized opposition concentrates there. - Air permits for generators/BTM gas (see §2.6) — the Nebius/New Jersey 400-MW plant is the marquee example of an air permit gating a multi-billion-dollar deal. - Environmental review and water/sewer approvals. Delays average 18–24 months where opposition forms (Introl/Data Center Watch), and cost overruns can kill project economics.

4.3 Community and political risk as a formal site criterion

This has become a first-class screening factor. Data Center Watch (10a Labs) documented ~$64 billion of US projects blocked or delayed by bipartisan local opposition (May 2024–March 2025), rising to ~$98–100 billion in a single quarter (Q2 2025) and, by its June 2026 update, ~$130+ billion cumulatively, with active opposition groups more than doubling in a quarter (396 → 833 across 49 states by March 2026). Cancellations went from 2 (2023) → 6 (2024) → 25 (2025). Opposition is bipartisan (~55% Republican, 45% Democrat among opposing politicians). The arguments used, in rough order: electricity-rate impacts, water, noise, property values/green space, and the low job-to-investment ratio. Documented denials include Peculiar, MO (Sept 2024); Culpeper/Brandy Station, VA ($12B, denied June 2024); and Tract's $14B Goodyear/Buckeye, AZ (withdrawn after rezoning blocked). Developers now screen for organized opposition, engage communities early, and offer binding water/noise/PILOT commitments before filing.

This is corroborated by POWER Magazine and EEPower citing Wood Mackenzie's Q2 2025 survey: power transformers averaging 128 weeks (~2.5 yrs) and GSUs 144 weeks (~2.8 yrs); WoodMac Senior Analyst Ben Boucher cites "an estimated 30% shortfall for power transformers and 10% for distribution units" in 2025, and notes that since 2019 GSU demand grew 274% and substation power transformers 116%. Wood Mackenzie calls the transformer market "structurally out of balance." Note source-quality caveat: many lead-time figures come from procurement consultancies and OEM-adjacent trackers; I anchor to the Wood Mackenzie Q2 2025 survey (128/144 weeks) as the most-cited primary benchmark and treat the "60-month" and "4–5 year" figures as the constrained tail, not the median.

5.2 Why you order before design is done

Because lead times now exceed design-and-permit timelines, procurement has moved to the front of the project. Hyperscale developers reserve factory slots with deposits of 10–30% of equipment cost at land-close, 6–12 months before groundbreaking, using ratings frozen at ~60% single-line design completeness (Global Data Center Hub). Instruments: reservation agreements, slot purchases, and framework/programmatic agreements with OEMs spanning multiple sites. A subtle trap: the manufacturing clock starts at Release to Manufacture (RTM), which can sit a quarter behind the PO — a January PO with RTM held to April loses 12 weeks before fabrication begins. - Vertical integration / pre-buying: hyperscalers and large colos pre-buy capacity and even manufacture in-house (Anthropic is hiring a "Global Power Delivery Equipment Lead" to run pooled/framework buys and slot reallocation across sites; SAVRN/Giga-type players manufacture and pre-position gear to compress timelines to 6–8 months). - What a smaller owner can do: standardize designs across projects; enter framework supply agreements directly with OEMs and then free-issue equipment to contractors or use nominated subcontractors (King & Spalding); qualify Tier-2 manufacturers (Efacec, SGB-SMIT, CG Power quote 24–36 months vs Tier-1's 48–60) and second-source every critical category; consider pre-engineered/modular skids (quoted 26–40 weeks).

5.3 Procurement models and delay risk (OFE vs CFE)

  • Owner-furnished equipment (OFE): the owner buys long-lead gear directly (to grab slots early and control the supply chain) and assigns/free-issues it to the EPC or design-build contractor, who installs it. The owner then carries the delivery-delay and defect risk for that equipment — if the transformer is late, the EPC's schedule (and any liquidated-damages claim) is affected but the cause sits with the owner. This is the dominant model for the scarce electrical gear precisely because owners won't cede slot control.
  • Contractor-furnished equipment (CFE): the EPC procures everything under a turnkey wrap — cleaner single-point responsibility, but the contractor prices in the schedule risk and you may lose slot priority.
  • Interaction with EPC / design-build: EPC gives a turnkey outcome with minimal owner involvement; design-build keeps more owner oversight and often leaves specialized systems in the owner's hands (i.e., OFE). Lump-sum contracting is "increasingly difficult" when copper/equipment/labor prices swing (Gravel2Gavel), so GMP (guaranteed-maximum-price) and blended models are common on sub-$500M builds.
  • Procurement obligations that come with OFE: factory acceptance testing (FAT) (witnessing the unit pass tests before it ships), warranty management (and the gap between OEM workmanship warranty and a contractor "wrap" warranty), storage/laydown (if gear arrives before the site is ready, the owner must store, insure, and maintain it), and delay liquidated damages (commonly 0.1–0.5%/day, capped at 5–10% of contract value).

5.4 Supply-chain concentration and tariff exposure

  • Concentration: two to three global producers dominate high-voltage transformers (ABB, Siemens Energy, Hitachi Energy, GE Vernova), constrained by grain-oriented electrical steel (GOES) — Japan, South Korea, Germany make ~70% of global GOES. Vertiv, Eaton, and Schneider dominate UPS/switchgear/distribution. Gensets are effectively a Caterpillar/Cummins oligopoly (both booked into 2028).
  • Competing demand: utilities' own grid-modernization, renewable/BESS interconnection, electrification, and reshoring compete for the same factories — "any AI campus depending on utility-supplied transformation is competing not just with other data centers, but with every utility upgrade in the country" (SAVRN). US transformer demand is projected to exceed domestic manufacturing capacity by ~30–40% through at least 2028, and a new transformer plant takes 3–5 years to build.
  • Tariff exposure: buyers "scrambling for imports and factory slots" (Reuters) face tariff risk on imported transformers/steel, adding cost and timing uncertainty — one more reason to lock price and slot early.

6. How It All Sequences

The mental model: interconnection is the critical path; land, water, permits, and procurement are parallel tracks that must not fall behind it. "Power infrastructure is no longer a dependency of development" — substations, transmission, and time are the critical path (DataCenters.com). Buildings can be erected in months; the power to energize them cannot.

Parallel tracks, from site option to energized substation for a 50–100 MW campus: - Track A — Interconnection (critical path): application → studies (12–36 months) → agreement → utility build & energization. ~2–5+ years total. - Track B — Land: option → diligence (title, geotech, flood, fiber, water) → purchase/close → rezoning/entitlement. - Track C — Water: source diligence → supply agreement / rights → discharge (NPDES) & sewer capacity. - Track D — Permits: rezoning/CUP → environmental review → building & air permits. - Track E — Procurement (starts at land-close, before design is final): reserve transformer/switchgear/generator/UPS slots → RTM → FAT → delivery → install.

The developer commits real money before certainty at each gate: option payments and study deposits before entitlement; CIAC and interconnection security before the agreement is final; 10–30% equipment deposits before design is complete; land close before permits are in hand. The art is sequencing so that the long-lead transformer arrives just as the utility upgrades and the on-site substation are ready — and so you haven't spent nine figures before you know the site will actually get power.

OPEN QUESTIONS / CHANGING FAST IN 2026

  • How much of the queue is real? ERCOT ~230 GW, Dominion ~70 GW, Georgia 34+ GW — but SRP's queue collapsed from 7.2 GW to ~1 GW once deposits were required, and AEP Ohio's from ~30 to ~13 GW. Nobody knows the true "build rate," and LBNL's 13%-completion history for generators looms over every headline number.
  • Will FERC federalize load interconnection? DOE's Oct 2025 directive and FERC's June 2026 actions could standardize (and speed) large-load rules — or fragment them region by region. The load-vs-generator jurisdictional split (§2.1) may not survive.
  • Do the new tariffs kill the goose? VA's $1.5M/MW + 14-year GS-5, Ohio's 85% take-or-pay, Georgia's 15-year contracts — the Buckeye Institute argues these charge the most mobile customers the most and will drive them away. Whether they protect ratepayers or repel investment is genuinely unresolved.
  • Behind-the-meter's ceiling: BTM went to ~82 GW announced, but air permits (Nebius/NJ) and genset shortages (Cummins into 2028) may cap actual deployment far below announcements — Cleanview models 5 GW vs 13 GW by end-2027 depending on delays.
  • Will incentives survive the backlash? New York's possible first statewide moratorium, Florida's 100-MW gate, Virginia's budget fight, and 300+ bills in early 2026 suggest the incentive era is turning to an oversight era.
  • Does liquid cooling change the water story? "Zero-water" designs exist and work — but adoption speed, and whether they simply move heat/water rejection elsewhere, is unsettled.

GLOSSARY

  • Large-load interconnection: the state-level process to connect a big new electricity consumer (vs. FERC-jurisdictional generator interconnection).
  • System Impact Study (SIS): utility study of how a new load affects grid voltage/thermal/reliability limits and what upgrades it triggers.
  • Network upgrades: transmission/distribution reinforcements required to accommodate a new load.
  • CIAC (contribution-in-aid-of-construction): non-refundable customer payment toward utility-owned facilities built to serve it.
  • Take-or-pay / minimum-take: tariff clause requiring payment for a set % of reserved capacity regardless of actual use (e.g., AEP Ohio 85%).
  • GS-5: Dominion Virginia's large-load rate class (≥25 MW) with 14-yr terms and $1.5M/MW collateral.
  • Behind-the-meter (BTM): on-site generation serving load without passing through the utility meter/grid.
  • Bridging power: temporary on-site generation covering the gap until grid interconnection is complete.
  • Prior appropriation: western US water-rights doctrine — "first in time, first in right," senior rights served first in drought.
  • Riparian rights: eastern US doctrine — reasonable water use tied to owning waterfront land.
  • NPDES: Clean Water Act discharge permit governing effluent (e.g., cooling-tower blowdown) quality/temperature.
  • Blowdown: concentrated water purged from evaporative cooling loops.
  • WUE (water usage effectiveness): liters of water per kWh of IT energy.
  • Consumptive use: water withdrawn and not returned locally (e.g., evaporated), vs. non-consumptive (returned).
  • OFE / CFE: owner-furnished vs contractor-furnished equipment — who buys the long-lead gear and carries its risk.
  • FAT (factory acceptance testing): witnessing equipment pass tests at the factory before shipment.
  • Release to Manufacture (RTM): the point when a factory actually starts building — can lag the PO by months.
  • Slot reservation: a deposit that holds a factory production slot for long-lead equipment.
  • GSU (generator step-up transformer): transformer connecting generation to the grid; longest transformer lead time.
  • GOES (grain-oriented electrical steel): specialized steel for transformer cores; ~70% made in Japan/Korea/Germany.
  • Powered land: a parcel valued primarily for its secured/near-term access to electricity.
  • Free-cooling hours: hours per year a site can cool without mechanical refrigeration; a climate-driven economics factor.

WHAT SURPRISED THE RESEARCHER

  1. How completely fiber was dethroned. Connectivity ran the checklist for decades; in 2025–26 it's explicitly secondary, and fiber now follows power into places like West Texas rather than the other way around.
  2. The queues are largely fiction — and everyone knows it. ERCOT's 230 GW is ~2.7× the state's record peak; Dominion's 70 GW is ~3× its peak. When SRP required a real 30% deposit, 7.2 GW became 1 GW overnight. The "AI power crisis" headline number and the bankable number differ by an order of magnitude.
  3. Equipment, not construction, sets the date. A building goes up in months; the transformer to energize it takes ~2.5 years, and the manufacturing clock doesn't even start at the PO — it starts at Release to Manufacture. Developers now put down 10–30% deposits at ~60% design completion.
  4. Behind-the-meter gas went mainstream in ~12 months — 92% of identified BTM capacity was announced after January 2025 — and the binding constraint turned out to be the air permit, not the turbine.
  5. The water problem is technically solved but politically unsolved. Best-in-class WUE is ~12× better than average and "zero-water" designs exist — yet water remains the most effective argument for stopping a project, because commitments arrive after opposition has already formed.
  6. Regulators put a literal price on the risk. Virginia's $1.5M/MW collateral is the SCC writing down, in dollars, how much it expects ratepayers would eat if a project skips town — a remarkable admission that these are viewed as risks, not prizes.
