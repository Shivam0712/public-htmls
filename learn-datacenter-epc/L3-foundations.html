Foundations, Delivery Models and Project Economics

Deep detail and sources: the appendix for this topic. Short version: sixty words.

A data center is a machine that turns electricity into computation and heat. Nearly every watt that reaches a server leaves again as heat, so the building exists to bring clean power in and move heat out without interruption. Everything else in this course, from cost to schedule to who signs which contract, scales from one number: the IT load in megawatts.

The reference facility, fixed here for every later chapter

1,000 racks, about 40 MW IT, multi-tenant colocation on the outskirts of a hot US metro (Phoenix or Dallas archetype). Density mix: 300 racks at 8 kW, 300 at 15 kW, 200 at 40 kW, 200 at 120 kW AI pods. Design PUE 1.4, so about 56 MW at the fence. Concurrently maintainable, Tier III class. Delivered design-build with owner-furnished long-lead equipment in roughly 10 MW phases. Capex $400–500M ex-IT; opex about $65M a year.

What a data center is

Nearly every watt delivered to IT becomes heat, so the cooling plant is sized by the same number as the compute it serves. The proportions are a design outcome, not a law: a hyperscale hall near PUE 1.09 shrinks the cooling share to almost nothing, while the industry average sits near 1.55. PUE is defined once in APX-foundations §2.

The types differ by who owns what, and that split predicts the contracting model before anyone draws a line. Our reference hall is a provider's building full of tenants' servers, so the owner designs for customers it has not yet signed. Definitions and sources: APX-foundations §1.

The market in 2025–26

Enterprise held over half the world's capacity in 2018 and is falling toward a fifth. The new demand is hyperscale and AI, and it is committed before the concrete cures, which is why a colocation owner in 2026 builds against preleases rather than hope. Market data and sources: APX-foundations §3.

Vacancy, preleasing and energy
  • North American primary-market vacancy fell to a record low 1.4% at year-end 2025, with record net absorption of about 2,500 MW (CBRE, H2 2025). Dallas-Fort Worth passed 1 GW of supply at 2.4% vacancy.
  • 74% (CBRE, H1 2025) to 77% (JLL, 2026 outlook) of capacity under construction is already committed to tenants, mostly cloud and AI.
  • US data centers used 176 TWh in 2023, 4.4% of US electricity; LBNL projects 325–580 TWh by 2028. Globally about 415 TWh in 2024 (IEA), 1.5% of world electricity.
  • Power access, not construction capacity, is now the binding constraint on new supply.

The lifecycle

Power and equipment, not construction, set the schedule: a hall can be finished and sit dark waiting for the utility. The ability to influence the outcome collapses as the project proceeds, so the density, cooling and power decisions must be right at concept. What has stretched and what has compressed: APX-foundations §4.

What stretched, what compressed

Stretched: grid interconnection (4–7 years in constrained regions such as PJM) and long-lead electrical equipment (substation transformers about 140 weeks in 2023, 150 in 2025, over 160 in 2026). Compressed: the build itself, through prefabricated modules, repeatable reference designs and parallel rather than sequential work. Even so, more than half of 2025 projects slipped three months or more despite preordering up to 24 months ahead (JLL, 2026). Skipping AI-readiness at design can cost $10–50M in later retrofits.

Who builds it, and who carries the risk

Risk lives at the boundaries: when drawings and building disagree, the question is which contract the gap falls into. The utility sits beside the chain and sets its pace. Why the commissioning agent must report to the owner: APX-foundations §5.

The model decides who pays when the transformer arrives eight months late: the owner under design-bid-build, the contractor under EPC, whose premium buys that certainty. Contract families: APX-foundations §6.

IPD, where "EPC" comes from, and the contract families
  • Integrated project delivery (IPD): owner, designer and builder share one multi-party contract with pooled risk and reward. Rare in data centers, used by a few sophisticated owners on complex programmes.
  • "EPC" is borrowed from oil, gas and power plants, where the FIDIC Silver Book (1999, 2017) formalised fixed-price turnkey risk transfer. Data center owners keep more control (owner-standard designs, owner-furnished equipment) than a classic EPC allows, so the word is used loosely here.
  • Contract families: AIA (A101, A201, A141) is the US private-work default; ConsensusDocs is the more balanced 2007 alternative; DBIA forms are built for design-build; FIDIC Red, Yellow and Silver books run from owner-designed to full turnkey; NEC is the UK collaborative, option-based suite.
  • Liquidated damages compensate the owner for late completion; force majeure excuses defined uncontrollable events; escalation clauses allocate equipment inflation; contingency is explicit and shared under a GMP but buried inside a lump-sum price.

Cross-sector, pre-AI-crunch data: trust the direction, not the decimals. Design-build wins on speed because design and construction overlap (APX-foundations §7).

The reference hall's delivery choice

Design-build with owner-furnished long-lead equipment. Interconnection and transformers set the schedule, so the owner orders that gear itself, starting the equipment clock alongside design, and hands execution risk to one design-builder. Late owner-furnished gear stays the owner's problem; the contract must say so.

What it costs

Figures that look contradictory differ by scope and density, not by disagreement. Before comparing two quotes, ask: per MW of what, air or liquid, shell or all-in, land in or out? Benchmarks by source, scope and date: APX-foundations §8.

The building is a power-and-cooling machine with a roof: three quarters of the money goes into electrical and mechanical plant. Liquid cooling moves spend from shell and electrical toward mechanical without changing that headline. Air versus liquid, and the cost drivers: APX-foundations §9.

US liquid-cooled builds carry a 7–10% premium over air-cooled at the same IT load (T&T 2025). Beyond density, cost is driven by redundancy (2N roughly duplicates the electrical gear), climate, scarce MEP labour, tariffs and equipment inflation, and speed premiums when a schedule is compressed against fixed lead times.

Electricity dominates, and in colocation it is largely a pass-through billed to tenants at a metered or PUE-marked rate. PUE therefore turns straight into dollars: dropping from 1.5 to 1.2 trims the electricity bill by about a fifth. Opex benchmarks: APX-foundations §10; how tenants pay: APX-foundations §12.

Discounting roughly halves the weight of future opex, which is why owners model it. The spread between $77–94M of revenue and $65M of gross opex is what services the $450M of capex; that utility-like profile is what draws REIT and infrastructure capital. Method and arithmetic: APX-foundations §11.

Where does most of the electricity a data center draws end up, and why does that size the cooling plant?

About two thirds goes to the IT equipment, and nearly all of it leaves as heat. The cooling plant is therefore sized by the same megawatts as the compute it serves.

Under design-bid-build, who carries design risk? Under EPC?

Under design-bid-build the owner does, because it warrants the drawings to the contractor. Under EPC the contractor does, inside one fixed-price turnkey contract.

Roughly what share of construction cost is electrical plus mechanical?

About three quarters: 54% electrical plus 22% mechanical for an air-cooled build. The shell is only about 14%.

Which two items now set the critical path of a new build, and why is neither in the contractor's control?

Grid interconnection (queues over four years) and long-lead equipment (transformers over 160 weeks). One sits with the utility and the other with manufacturers; no construction contract can hurry either.

Two credible sources quote $10.7M and $17.6M per MW. Why are both right?

They measure different products: JLL's $10.7M is a 2025 global average of narrower scope, while Cushman & Wakefield's $17.6M is all-in greenfield for the most modern, AI-capable facilities in 2026. The gap is scope and density, not disagreement.

Which delivery model would the reference hall's owner most likely choose in 2026, and what does it buy them?

Design-build with owner-furnished long-lead equipment. It buys a single point of responsibility and the fastest, most schedule-reliable delivery, while the owner starts the transformer and interconnection clocks itself.
