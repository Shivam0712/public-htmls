Building and physical infrastructure

Deep detail and sources: the appendix for this topic. Short version: sixty words. The research report this chapter was distilled from. Earlier version: archived 2026-09-26.

The site and grid connection are set. What must we build to carry and protect the servers? A rack is a tall cabinet of computing hardware; a data hall is a room containing rows of racks. Our reference is a multi-tenant colocation campus near a hot United States city, where wholesale customers rent large blocks, retail customers rent smaller spaces, and all share building services. Its four halls hold 1,000 racks: 300 at 8 kilowatts (kW), 300 at 15 kW, 200 at 40 kW and 200 at 120 kW. Together they draw about 39 megawatts (MW) of information technology (IT) power, rounded to 40 MW. The 120 kW racks support artificial intelligence (AI) workloads and need liquid cooling. We will follow the building from walls and floors to equipment delivery and final testing.

What the building has to serve

Before counting walls, distinguish rack power from building power. Power usage effectiveness (PUE) is total facility power divided by IT power. At the course design value of 1.4, 40 MW for racks requires about 56 MW at the property boundary. The shell is the building's frame, outer walls and roof; its small cost share hides its effect on everything installed inside.

Takeaway: The first phase is not a quarter of the eventual price because it buys shared work for later halls. Power and cooling installations take much more of the construction budget, yet their rooms depend on openings and supports established by the shell. A published shell-and-core price may include basic electrical or cooling distribution, so it is not the same as this structure-and-envelope share (APX-building §1; APX-building §12).

Construction cost versus running cost

The course also budgets about $65 million a year to operate the completed campus, including about $34 million for electricity. Those recurring costs are outside the $400–500 million construction budget. The source trail and cost scopes are in APX-building §13.

Choose the shell and grid

A single-story hall puts heavy racks on a ground-supported floor; building upward saves land but adds upper-floor structure and freight lifts. A column grid is the spacing of structural posts, chosen to fit rack rows and aisles. Concrete tilt-up casts wall panels on site and lifts them upright; precast panels arrive from a factory. Factory-built modules assemble repeated rooms or plant away from the site. These approaches trade land, enclosure time and freedom to change the layout:

Takeaway: The course building uses one-story tilt-up blocks and roughly 12 metres (m) between column lines. The final grid still has to fit actual aisles and heavy-equipment openings. Dry-in is the point when the roof and walls keep weather out: sensitive indoor work can then expand, although protected installation may begin earlier (APX-building §2; APX-building §3).

When the shell choice changes

Poor soil can change the foundation cost of any option. Expensive or scarce land can justify upper stories, but then floor vibration and the freight lift become equipment constraints. Precast and factory modules can shorten site work only when their dimensions and connections are settled early.

Follow the weight from rack to ground

A rack loads a floor through small feet, not as an even carpet of weight. Pounds per square foot (psf) can describe a load averaged over an area, but a foot also needs a concentrated-load check. Our worked 120 kW rack weighs about 1,360 kilograms (kg) before coolant and 1,500 kg filled, over a footprint of about 0.64 square metres (m²). A coolant distribution unit (CDU) pumps liquid through the rack row and adds a separate equipment load.

Takeaway: The 400 psf bay allowance and the filled rack's 480 psf footprint average answer different questions; neither number alone proves that the floor is safe or unsafe. A quoted 100 psf code minimum for certain computer access floors is not permission to design this hall for 100 psf (APX-building §4; APX-building §5).

The filled rack’s area average, its four foot loads and the loads from moving it into place are different checks on the same floor.

Takeaway: The 1,500 kg filled rack averages about 480 psf across its 0.64 m² footprint, but each of its four feet carries about 375 kg before uneven loading. The moving delivery load and the filled CDU’s roughly 3-tonne load on its own pad also need checks through the reinforced slab to the prepared ground.

The arithmetic is not a structural certificate

The dry rack gives 1,360 kg ÷ 0.64 m², or about 435 psf averaged over its footprint. Filled, 1,500 kg ÷ 0.64 m² is about 480 psf; four equally loaded feet each carry about 375 kg before movement or uneven loading. Slab reinforcement, joints and soil stiffness still determine the real design. An engineer must also check the feet, moving route, coolant-unit pad and ground beneath the concrete.

Choose the floor and service routes

Liquid cooling changes where the hall's services can run. A raised access floor is removable tiles on pedestals above a cavity for air and cables; a slab-on-grade is concrete supported by prepared soil. The working section uses a slab 12–18 inches (in) thick and 6 m of clear height, the usable distance above the finished floor. A busway carries power in an enclosed overhead conductor, cable trays support wiring, and liquid manifolds distribute coolant among racks. A rooftop dry cooler rejects heat to outdoor air.

Takeaway: The cooler sits on steel support frames called dunnage, which transfer its weight into the roof. Keeping liquid manifolds below and apart from electrical routes reduces what lies beneath a leak, but detection and safe drainage remain necessary. Give the systems room to work

Racks occupy only part of a hall. White space is the rack room; gray space holds its electrical and cooling support. Tier III-class concurrent maintainability means planned work on one power or cooling path should not interrupt the racks, which takes room. An uninterruptible power supply (UPS) keeps IT equipment powered through a brief interruption while generators start. A mechanical, electrical and plumbing (MEP) gallery gives staff service access; a network operations center (NOC) monitors operations. A meet-me room connects tenants' network links to carriers. Takeaway: Electrical rooms face the racks they feed, coolant units sit near liquid rows, and secure staging separates deliveries from live equipment. Battery rooms need fire separation under locally adopted rules; the drawing's rack reserve is already within the 32,000 sf of white space. Tenant changes must be checked against these service routes before walls are fixed (APX-building §8).

Why the battery room needs early review

The locally adopted edition of NFPA 855 affects battery-room separation, ventilation and installation limits. Chemistry, tested equipment and approved exceptions matter. A quoted 600-kilowatt-hour room limit should not be copied into every project without checking the actual design and the local fire authority.

Plan the route from truck to pad

Large equipment does not follow the same route as a rack. A rigging path runs from trailer to final pad and is sized for its governing load, the largest and heaviest in-hall unit; the much larger grid transformer stays in the outdoor yard. A lowboy is a low-deck heavy trailer, and a knock-out panel is a removable wall section for equipment too big for the dock. Ordinary racks instead use the course's two dock-high doors, at trailer-bed height, or its grade-level door at ground level; they are unboxed in secure staging. Takeaway: The final pad and its cast-in anchors must fit the supplier's unit. A standard dock or its leveler is not a safe heavy-equipment route just because it looks wide enough. Door, ground-bearing and lift checks belong in the design before the delivery is booked (APX-building §9).

Make the envelope fit the site

The roof and finished floor must withstand the local climate even when the machines are off. Phoenix-like sites bring prolonged heat and dust; Dallas-like sites add serious hail and wind. A 500-year flood elevation represents a modeled 0.2% annual flood chance; freeboard is extra floor height above the chosen level. These threats become early choices about the enclosure:

Takeaway: For the course building, a concrete roof deck is a planning choice, not a rule that every data center needs one. An insurer may require a tougher roof than the building code without universally prohibiting steel decks. The occupancy risk category, a code classification that affects earthquake design, must also be established for the actual site and use, not inferred from Tier III-class operations (APX-building §6; APX-building §10).

Check sound at the property boundary

Fans and cooling equipment can create a continuous hum long after construction ends. Sound is commonly reported in A-weighted decibels (dBA), adjusted toward human hearing; the legal limit and measurement location come from the local ordinance. For the reference design, test 45 dBA at night and 55 dBA by day at the property boundary, then model all operating equipment together. The illustrative ranges show why distance and sound control belong in the site plan:

Takeaway: Unmitigated cooling equipment may exceed the night target even if a daytime reading passes. A tonal hum or low-frequency sound can also prompt complaints that an average reading misses. Quieter fans, acoustic enclosures and setbacks use money, land and service space; generator-testing exemptions differ by jurisdiction (APX-building §11).

Sound is a delivery risk

If a sound model fails after the yard and roof are fixed, moving equipment or adding a wall can reopen permits. Check day, night and tonal sound before fixing setbacks, and confirm local rules for generator testing.

Sequence the build and its handoffs

A finished shell is not a working data center. Under design-build, one contracted team coordinates design and construction; the owner orders some long-lead gear, equipment that takes many months to make. That gear is owner-furnished equipment (OFE), such as major switchgear and generators. Commissioning tests installed power and cooling together, including simulated failures. A hall is ready for service only when it is energized, tested and available to tenants.

Takeaway: These durations overlap and must not be added. Protected interior work may start before complete dry-in, and tenants may install racks while other construction continues. The fragile handoff is factory-made gear meeting pads and openings poured months earlier (APX-building §1; APX-building §12).

Freeze the interfaces before concrete

The contract must say who checks supplier dimensions against openings, pads and power or cooling connections, and who bears a late OFE delivery. Agree a maximum size for early work, then require signed supplier connection details before final anchors are cast. Give dry-in, early access and system testing dated milestones so a missing component does not remain hidden until handover.
