Owner

Decide what to build, who will deliver it and what it will cost.

1Foundations, Delivery Models & Project Economicsdrafted

A data center turns electricity into computing work and heat. It may be enterprise, colocation, hyperscale, edge, modular or an AI factory. An owner works with designers and builders under design-bid-build, design-build or engineering, procurement and construction (EPC) contracts; the contract sets who carries delivery risk. Projects are priced in dollars per megawatt.

2Design Requirements, Capacity & Load Planningdrafted

Before design starts, the owner sets requirements and the designer explains how to meet them. Kilowatts (kW) per rack set power, cooling and floor loads. Tier ratings, TIA-942 (a data center infrastructure standard) and BICSI (a standards body) design classes show how much failure the facility can tolerate.

Project

Find a site, secure power and water, and get the permits to build.

3Site Selection, Grid Interconnection, Water & Procurementdrafted

Land may be easy to find; usable power is not. A site needs grid capacity you can actually secure—often after years, not months—plus fiber, permits to use and discharge water, acceptable hazard risk and community support. Transformers must be ordered while the site is being secured.

4Building & Physical Infrastructuredrafted

The building shell supports the equipment and keeps weather out. Some loaded racks weigh more than a tonne, so floors matter. The plan separates white space (computer equipment), gray space (supporting plant) and galleries for pipes and conduit. Loading docks, access routes, hardening and noise limits all shape the design.

Systems

Put power, cooling, computing, networks and protection in place.

5Power Systems I — Utility to UPSdrafted

Utility power arrives at medium voltage. Transformers step it down; switchgear routes it; generators replace it when the utility fails. An uninterruptible power supply (UPS) keeps equipment running for the seconds between a grid failure and generator start. Without that bridge, the facility can go dark.

6Power Systems II — Distribution & Redundancydrafted

After the UPS, power distribution units (PDUs), busway and panels feed the racks. N, N+1 and 2N describe spare capacity or paths and whether maintenance needs an outage. Dense racks increasingly use 48-volt or higher-voltage direct current (DC). Grounding keeps it safe; emergency power off (EPO) shuts it all down.

7Cooling & Thermal Managementdrafted

Every watt in becomes a watt of heat to remove. At low densities, contained aisles, computer-room air conditioners (CRACs), computer-room air handlers (CRAHs), chillers and free cooling when weather permits can manage it. Above roughly 50 kilowatts per rack, air alone fails; liquid cooling must reach the chips.

8AI/HPC Infrastructure + Compute & Storagedrafted

An artificial intelligence (AI) rack can draw over 100 kilowatts; a typical rack draws eight. That changes the power, cooling, structure and network around it. This topic also covers high-performance computing (HPC). Rack units measure equipment space; servers and direct-attached, networked or object storage are the other basics.

9Network Architecture & Structured Cablingdrafted

In a flat network, leaf switches at every rack connect to spine switches; any two servers are two hops apart. Overlays such as Ethernet virtual private networks (EVPN) and virtual extensible local area networks (VXLAN) separate tenants. AI clusters need their own high-speed fabric. Fiber, copper, pathways, patch fields and a carrier room connect it all.

10Physical Security, Fire Protection, Monitoring & Efficiencydrafted

From fence to cabinet, layers assume the last failed. Detect fire early and suppress it without damaging equipment. Power, temperature and space sensors feed building management systems (BMS), data center infrastructure management (DCIM) and electrical power monitoring systems (EPMS). Power usage effectiveness (PUE), water usage effectiveness (WUE) and carbon usage effectiveness (CUE) track waste imperfectly and can be gamed.

Handover

Prove the facility works, operate it and eventually retire it.

11Commissioning, Operations & Decommissioningdrafted

A finished building may not work yet. Commissioning proves each system works, then tests them together, ending with a deliberate utility-power cut to see how the facility responds. Operations needs written procedures, planned maintenance and controlled changes. At retirement, hardware leaves under chain of custody and its data is destroyed.
