Owner

What you are building, who builds it, and what it costs.

1Foundations, Delivery Models & Project Economicsnot started

A data center is a building that converts electricity into computation and heat. They come as enterprise, colocation, hyperscale, edge, modular, and now AI factories. Someone owns it, someone designs it, someone builds it — under design-bid-build, design-build, or EPC, which decide who eats the risk. It is bought in dollars per megawatt.

What a data center is and the types that exist (enterprise, colocation, hyperscale, edge, modular, "AI factory"); the facility lifecycle from plan to operate; who actually builds one — the contracting models (design-bid-build, design-build, EPC turnkey) and which party carries which risk; and what it costs — capex versus opex, dollars per megawatt, and TCO as a discipline rather than a footnote.

2Design Requirements, Capacity & Load Planningnot started

Before anything is drawn, the owner states what they need and the designer states how they will meet it. The number that matters most is kilowatts per rack: it sets the power, the cooling, and the floor loading. Tiers, TIA-942 ratings, and BICSI classes are ways to say how much failure is tolerable.

The owner's side of the table. Owner's Project Requirements and Basis of Design; density-first capacity planning — deciding kW per rack, because that single number drives power, cooling, and structure downstream; availability frameworks (Uptime Tiers, TIA-942 ratings, BICSI classes) as one design criterion among several rather than the spine; codes; certifications and audits as facility concerns.

3Site Selection, Grid Interconnection, Water & Procurementnot started

Land is easy; power is not. Choosing a site means finding grid capacity you can actually be granted — a process measured in years, not months — plus fiber, water you are permitted to use and discharge, tolerable hazard risk, and a community that will have you. Meanwhile the transformers must already be on order.

Where it goes and whether you can actually get what you need. Location, climate, and natural-hazard factors; grid interconnection as a multi-year process, not a wiring job; water sourcing, discharge permitting, and community moratoriums; taxes, incentives, and permitting risk; and long-lead equipment procurement — why transformers and switchgear get ordered before the design is finished.

4Building & Physical Infrastructurenot started

The shell holds the machines up and keeps the weather out. Floors carry racks that can exceed a tonne each. Space divides into white space for IT, gray space for the plant that serves it, and galleries for pipes and conduit. Docks, routes, hardening, and noise limits are all design constraints.

Shell and core; structural loading, now driven by racks weighing over a tonne; raised floor versus slab; room topology (white space, gray space, MEP galleries); loading docks and equipment logistics; seismic, flood, and wind hardening; roof and envelope; noise as a permitting constraint.

Systems

The plant itself: power, cooling, compute, network, protection.

5Power Systems I — Utility to UPSnot started

Grid power arrives at medium voltage and must be stepped down, switched, and made trustworthy. Transformers convert it, switchgear directs it, generators replace it when the utility fails, and the UPS bridges the gap between those two events — seconds that decide whether the facility rides through or goes dark.

Utility feed and substations; transformers and switchgear; generators and fuel systems; UPS topologies (double-conversion, line-interactive, rotary) and the batteries behind them (VRLA, lithium-ion). Everything between the grid and the point where clean power becomes available to distribute.

6Power Systems II — Distribution & Redundancynot started

From the UPS, power fans out through PDUs, busway, and panels to each rack. Redundancy is described as N, N+1, or 2N — how many spare paths exist and whether maintenance requires an outage. High density is pushing racks toward 48-volt and higher-voltage DC. Grounding keeps it safe; EPO kills it all.

Distribution from UPS to rack: PDUs, busway, remote power panels. Redundancy models (N, N+1, 2N, 2N+1, distributed redundant) and what each actually buys. The shift to 48V and higher-voltage DC for high-density racks. Grounding and bonding; emergency power off.

7Cooling & Thermal Managementnot started

Every watt in becomes a watt of heat out, and it has to go somewhere. Air handles low density: contained aisles, CRACs or CRAHs, chillers, and free cooling when the weather permits. Above roughly fifty kilowatts a rack, air fails and liquid must reach the chip itself.

Heat and airflow fundamentals; CRAC versus CRAH; chilled-water plants; direct and indirect economization; hot/cold-aisle containment; liquid and immersion cooling as the mainstream path for high density, not an exotic appendix — cold plates, CDUs, rear-door heat exchangers; ASHRAE envelopes and humidity control; water use and heat reuse.

An AI rack can draw more than a hundred kilowatts where a normal one draws eight, and that single fact rewrites the power, cooling, structure, and network around it. Underneath sits the ordinary business: racks and rack units, servers, and storage as direct, networked, or object.

AI clusters as a design driver: what megawatt racks change across power, cooling, network, floor loading, and cost. 9Network Architecture & Structured Cablingnot started

Modern fabrics are flat: leaf switches at every rack, spines connecting them, any server two hops from any other. Overlays like EVPN/VXLAN carve it into tenants; AI clusters demand their own high-speed fabric. Beneath the logic lies physical plant — fiber, copper, pathways, patch fields, and the room where carriers land.

The logical layer: spine-leaf (Clos) fabrics, ToR versus EoR, underlay and overlay (BGP, EVPN/VXLAN), AI fabrics (InfiniBand, 800G), peering and data-center interconnect, out-of-band management. The physical layer that carries it: copper and fiber media, TIA-942 spaces (MDA/IDA/HDA/EDA), overhead versus underfloor pathways, cross-connects, labeling, meet-me rooms and carrier entry.

10Physical Security, Fire Protection, Monitoring & Efficiencynot started

Security is layered from fence to cabinet, each ring assuming the last one failed. Fire is detected early and suppressed without ruining the equipment. Sensors watch power, temperature, and space, feeding BMS, DCIM, and EPMS. PUE, WUE, and CUE score the waste — imperfectly, and easy to flatter.

Layered security from perimeter to rack; access control, mantraps, surveillance; fire detection (spot, VESDA) and suppression (pre-action, clean agent); egress and life safety. Then the instrumentation layer: sensors and metering, BMS versus DCIM versus EPMS, alerting, capacity dashboards, asset and change tracking — and PUE/WUE/CUE, their limits, and efficiency levers, taught where they are actually measured.

Handover

Proving it works, running it, and ending it.

11Commissioning, Operations & Decommissioningnot started

A finished building is not a working one. Commissioning proves each system, then all of them together, culminating in deliberately cutting utility power to see what happens. Operations then lives by written procedures, planned maintenance, and disciplined change. At the end, hardware leaves under chain of custody with its data destroyed.

Proving it works, running it, and ending it. Commissioning levels 1–5; factory and site acceptance; the independent commissioning agent and why independence is the point; integrated systems testing (pull-the-plug day); documentation and handover. Then MOPs/SOPs/EOPs, preventive maintenance, staffing and the labor shortage, change and incident management, vendor and spares strategy. Finally decommissioning: ITAD, data sanitization, and chain of custody.
