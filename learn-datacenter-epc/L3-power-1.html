From the utility fence to protected power

Deep detail and sources: the appendix for this topic. Short version: sixty words. The research report this chapter was distilled from. Earlier version: archived 2026-09-26.

Our multi-tenant colocation campus outside a hot US metro sells powered space to many customers. At full build, its 1,000 racks draw about 40 megawatts (MW) of information technology (IT) power. Cooling and support add to that load: power usage effectiveness (PUE), site power divided by IT power, is 1.4, so the utility sees about 56 MW.

The first hall starts at about 10 MW of IT power and 14 MW total. This chapter follows electricity from the utility connection to the output of an uninterruptible power supply (UPS). When grid power disappears, the UPS holds computing loads steady while standby generators start and pick up the rest of the hall.

Begin with the loads, not the equipment

Why does a hall with 10 MW of computing equipment need 14 MW from its supply? The difference includes cooling, pumps and electrical losses, which still matter when the utility fails. Phase 1 is also only part of a campus whose utility connection must allow later halls.

Takeaway: The UPS and generators therefore answer different questions. UPS capacity starts with the protected 10 MW; generator capacity starts with the whole 14 MW, including the equipment needed to keep the hall operating. These are design loads, not a claim that every meter reads its maximum at every moment.

Where the 40 MW comes from

A kilowatt (kW) is one-thousandth of a megawatt. The mix is 300 racks at 8 kW, 300 at 15 kW, 200 at 40 kW and 200 artificial intelligence (AI) pod racks at 120 kW. Their total is 38.9 MW, rounded to about 40 MW; the AI pods are one-fifth of the racks but supply roughly three-fifths of the planned rack load. See APX-power-1 §6.

Follow the normal and standby routes

At the fence, the completed campus accepts 138 kilovolts (kV), where one kV is 1,000 volts, and steps that voltage down twice. A megavolt-ampere (MVA) is a transformer capacity unit, not automatically the same number of MW. A bus is a shared conductor connecting power circuits; the generators join the campus bus rather than sitting in series with the utility.

Takeaway: Cooling branches off ahead of the UPS, so it may pause during a transfer even while computing equipment stays on. The example's rechargeable batteries use lithium iron phosphate (LFP). The site view shows where those branches and their equipment sit (APX-power-1 §4; APX-power-1 §12).

Takeaway: The dashed block is a planning group, not a rule that a 2.5 MVA transformer can deliver 2.5 MW through a UPS at full output. Its capacity and alternate supply path still have to be checked.

Connect the finished campus to the utility

Connecting only the first hall would leave a difficult grid upgrade for later. The 56 MW completed site calls for a dedicated customer substation; 138 kV is this course's service choice. Voltage bands help frame the question, but the utility's interconnection study decides what can actually be built.

Takeaway: The demarcation point is the agreed ownership boundary between utility and customer; the customer builds the power chain downstream of it. Two incoming lines may reduce outages, but their benefit depends on what they share. The Uptime Institute's Tier III concurrent-maintainability objective means planned work without interrupting protected IT service; it does not require two utility feeders (APX-power-1 §1; APX-power-1 §2).

Takeaway: The second line is a purchasing decision about grid exposure, not a substitute for the on-site standby plant. Meter location, asset ownership and the final connection scope belong in the interconnection agreement.

See what a bus tie can and cannot maintain

Suppose one substation transformer needs inspection while the hall stays open. In a main-tie-main arrangement, each transformer normally feeds one switchgear bus section, and a normally open tie can join the sections. The surviving transformer and tie must be capable of carrying the combined load.

Takeaway: With main A open and the tie closed, transformer B keeps both bus sections live, allowing work on A's isolated incoming equipment. Bus A itself is still live: maintaining that section without dropping its loads needs a separate downstream route or a suitable transfer arrangement. One spare transformer alone does not establish full Tier III maintainability (APX-power-1 §3).

Selective coordination means the breaker nearest a fault should open first, limiting the outage. Arc-resistant switchgear directs the effects of an internal arc away from staff; it does not stop the fault from needing to be cleared. Both features must be designed for the actual equipment and tested paths (APX-power-1 §7).

Switchgear choices and an important voltage caveat

Air-insulated switchgear (AIS) takes more space; gas-insulated switchgear (GIS) is compact. Sulfur hexafluoride (SF6) has commonly insulated GIS, while vacuum and dry-air designs can avoid it. The 2026 European restriction on new equipment up to 24 kV does not itself cover this 34.5 kV campus; check applicable local rules and US-frequency product ratings before ordering a compact SF6-free option. An optional suite may use two complete, independently capable paths, called 2N, rather than relying on a single tie. See APX-power-1 §7.

Check transformer capacity in the right units

Large outdoor, oil-filled transformers bring utility power down to campus voltage; smaller indoor, dry-type transformers supply the hall. Power factor is real power in MW divided by apparent power in MVA. At power factor 0.9, a 2.5 MVA transformer supplies at most about 2.25 MW before further losses, not 2.5 MW.

Takeaway: Firm capacity is what remains available with one unit out. Two 30 MVA substation transformers leave one 30 MVA unit for the first hall. Eight 2.5 MVA hall units leave seven, or 17.5 MVA in aggregate; at power factor 0.9 that is 15.75 MW before site derating. Their circuits must also be arranged so a transformer outage does not strand a UPS (APX-power-1 §4; APX-power-1 §6).

Two 30 MVA units are not an N+1 solution for the full 56 MW campus. Even three leave only 60 MVA with one out, about 57 MW at power factor 0.95 before margins. The expansion plan must reserve connections for a fourth 30 MVA unit or use appropriately larger units, subject to the utility and thermal studies.

Losses and the one-to-one block trap

The 4 MW difference in the figure already includes cooling and power-conversion losses; do not add those losses to the 14 MW a second time. A 2.5 MVA hall transformer must not be paired by nameplate alone with a 2.5 MW UPS: check UPS input efficiency, power factor, the loading after one feeder is lost and which transformers supply cooling. Transformers also lose power while energized but lightly loaded. An indicative unit with 4 kilowatts of idle loss and 27 kilowatts of full-load copper loss costs about $6,600 a year at half-load and $0.07 per kilowatt-hour; that example does not establish a campus-wide loss budget. See APX-power-1 §5.

Size standby generators for the whole hall

With utility power gone, batteries hold the computing supply while engines start. The engines must then carry the first hall's 14 MW design load, not only its 10 MW of IT. N+1 means enough capacity remains for that load after any one generating unit is unavailable.

Takeaway: Six 2.75 MW sets fail the arithmetic: five surviving sets provide just 13.75 MW at nameplate. The locked reference hall has six 2.75 MW sets, which are insufficient at 14 MW with one out. A seventh set or larger site-rated sets would require an approved change, with derating, load steps and battery recharge checked. Paralleling switchgear joins the started engines to the campus bus, while fuel and an air permit determine how the plant can operate (APX-power-1 §8).

Why engine ratings and fuel hours need a second check

Hot-weather derating can reduce surviving sets’ output below nameplate; obtain site-rated values before allowing for transients or recharge. The owner therefore needs a site-rated manufacturer proposal and may need larger or more sets. Its 48-hour fuel target exceeds the Uptime Institute's 12-hour Tier minimum at the required load; 72 hours is a common owner choice, not a universal Tier rule. Emergency-engine approval does not automatically authorize demand response or bridge-power operation: check the applicable engine rules, air permit and allowed test hours. A natural-gas plant used while awaiting grid service is a different power architecture.

Let the UPS protect a different load

Generators protect the whole hall after a delay; the UPS must protect computing equipment immediately. In normal double-conversion operation, a rectifier turns incoming alternating current into direct current and an inverter rebuilds alternating current at the output. The LFP battery feeds the inverter when its input disappears; a static bypass is a separate, less conditioned route used under specified faults or overloads.

Takeaway: Five minutes is battery runtime at the specified UPS load, not five minutes of power for every system in the hall. The reference layout places LFP cabinets in a separate fire-rated room, with the final arrangement subject to local approval (APX-power-1 §9; APX-power-1 §11). Why, then, does Hall 1 show six UPS blocks rather than the five that simple N+1 arithmetic suggests?

Takeaway: Six blocks give the five survivors operating headroom rather than forcing them to full nameplate. The distribution must be able to rebalance their load, and that headroom does not automatically cover fast, synchronized computing-load swings.

Battery approval and UPS operating modes

Underwriters Laboratories (UL) 9540A is a fire-propagation test, distinct from a UL 9540 system listing. National Fire Protection Association (NFPA) 855 is an installation standard where applicable; the authority having jurisdiction (AHJ), usually the relevant local fire or building authority, determines the adopted rules and acceptable layout. A separate battery room is this course's design choice, not a universal rule for every lithium UPS installation. A high-efficiency UPS bypass mode changes what disturbances can reach the load, so this example keeps double conversion in normal operation. Measure rapid computing-load changes before deciding whether extra buffering or load smoothing is needed (APX-power-1 §10; APX-power-1 §11).

Watch the first fifteen seconds

A grid failure starts two clocks. The inverter must keep computing power continuous at once, while upstream cooling waits for generator power and the transfer gear. The battery buys time for that start and possible retries, not hours of ordinary operation.

Takeaway: The 20-millisecond marker describes battery takeover inside the UPS, not a 20-millisecond interruption at its output. In this illustration, engines are ready near ten seconds and the hall transfers near fifteen, while the UPS output stays live. On utility return, controls wait for a stable source before transferring back (APX-power-1 §12).

National Fire Protection Association (NFPA) 110 Type 10 is a separate requirement: specified emergency loads must receive power at their transfer-switch terminals within ten seconds. A picture showing transfer at fifteen seconds does not prove Type 10 compliance, even though the UPS can carry its own load through that interval. At the 480Y/277-volt UPS output, the Y denotes a shared-neutral connection: 480 volts between phases and 277 from a phase to neutral. The inverter normally holds 60 hertz (Hz) there, but bypass operation, exhausted batteries and downstream faults remain possible.

What the outage sketch does not certify

If Type 10 is specified for a load, test its actual generator-start and transfer sequence against the ten-second limit. A transfer from a dead utility source cannot be a make-before-break transfer; a controlled transfer back after utility recovery is a different operation. Confirm battery runtime at its specified loading and end-of-life condition rather than assuming every five-minute label guarantees five minutes in service. See APX-power-1 §12.

Order the long-lead chain before opening the hall

Under this design-build project, the owner purchases major items as owner-furnished equipment (OFE) and the builder installs them. Factory places must be reserved while utility requirements and room details are being settled. These 2025 to 2026 lead times are indicative queues, not a promised opening date.

Takeaway: The utility controls its connection and energization; the owner controls delivery of its purchased equipment; the builder must make it all work together. Ratings fixed for a factory order also affect the breaker-coordination study. Full-campus growth repeats hall equipment but adds substation capacity before the surviving transformers reach their limit (APX-power-1 §6).

Put each hand-off in the contract

Name who witnesses factory tests, accepts OFE deliveries, preserves a transformer if its pad is late, holds the engine air permit and obtains battery-room approval from the authority having jurisdiction (AHJ). Then test a real utility loss and planned removal of each maintainable component or path. Two 30 MVA transformers are a Phase 1 starting point, not a finished-campus N+1 claim.

Costs and what the lead-time ranges mean

The first 10 MW phase costs about $180–230 million including shared site and shell work; the completed campus costs about $400–500 million excluding customer IT. Annual operating expense is about $65 million, including about $34 million for electricity. The reference project plans 128 weeks from release to manufacture for its 30 MVA transformers; obtain dated factory quotations for the specified units. Reserve physical space and electrical connections for later upstream units so expansion does not require replacing Phase 1 equipment (APX-power-1 §5; APX-power-1 §13).

Which two-feed arrangement can survive the loss of one substation, and is it required for Tier III?

Feeders from two substations can survive one substation's loss. Two utility feeds are not a Tier III requirement.

Transformer B can keep both bus sections powered. Bus A remains energized, so its loads need another route before that bus section can be isolated for work.
