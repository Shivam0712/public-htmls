Heat and airflow identities

IT kW in equals heat kW out. The conversions that anchor all sizing: 1 kW = 3,412 BTU/h = 0.284 tons of refrigeration (a ton is 12,000 BTU/h). A 15 kW rack rejects 51,180 BTU/h, about 4.26 tons; Phase 1's 10.7 MW is about 3,040 tons (§12).

Airflow. At sea level ΔT(°F) = 3.1 × W ÷ CFM, so CFM = W ÷ (0.318 × ΔT°F) (Upsite Technologies). Hence the field rules: a 20 °F (11 K) rise needs about 157 CFM per kW, 25 °F about 125, 30 °F (17 K) about 105. A 15 kW rack: 2,355, 1,875 or 1,575 CFM. Widening the rise from 20 to 30 °F cuts airflow by a third, the whole economic case for containment, since a wide rise is only possible when supply and return cannot mix. A best-in-class raised-floor tile delivers about 1,900 CFM (ASHRAE 2021), so one tile cannot feed a 15 kW rack at 20 °F, one reason dense racks abandon the raised floor.

Cube law. Fan power is proportional to speed cubed; 80% speed is about half the power (SubZero Engineering, fan affinity laws). Big slow fan walls therefore beat small fast CRAH fans, and every degree of allowed inlet temperature that lets fans slow is worth money.

Bypass and recirculation. Static pressure should just overcome the airflow path. Over-pressurising drives bypass, cold supply short-circuiting to the return without passing a server, wasting capacity. Starving drives recirculation, hot exhaust re-entering intakes through gaps, missing blanking panels or over rack tops, pushing inlets above class. Containment exists to kill both; both return the moment blanking discipline lapses (Upsite; SubZero).

ASHRAE Thermal Guidelines, 5th edition, and Phoenix against the envelope

The current release is the 5th edition of Thermal Guidelines for Data Processing Environments, ASHRAE TC 9.9, March 2021. Air classes A1–A4 share a recommended envelope of 18–27 °C at the inlet; allowable ranges widen by class: A1 15–32, A2 10–35, A3 5–40, A4 5–45 °C. The edition added class H1 for high-density air-cooled gear, allowable 15–25 °C and recommended 18–22 °C, meant for a segregated zone. The recommended band has not moved; the classes changed how far a vendor lets an operator stray, and H1 pulls dense air gear back toward a tight band.

W-classes renamed by their upper supply-water temperature, all with a 2 °C lower limit: W17 (was W1), W27 (W2), W32 (W3), W40 (new), W45 (W4), W+ above 45 °C (was W5) (ASHRAE, Emergence and Expansion of Liquid Cooling in Mainstream Data Centers, 2021). W17 and W27 need chillers; W32 runs chiller-free in most climates; W40, W45 and W+ reject on towers or dry coolers year-round, the energy prize. Compliance tightened: a W-class now requires full, unthrottled operation across the whole class range.

Humidity moved from relative humidity to dew point, because a fixed RH is a different absolute moisture at each temperature; dew point targets the real risks, electrostatic discharge when dry and hygroscopic dust and corrosion when wet. Recommended band about −9 to 15 °C dew point (ASHRAE 2016/2021).

Phoenix against the envelope. Summer dry bulb reaches about 45 °C, at the A4 ceiling, so direct free air is unavailable at peak; but wet bulb rarely exceeds the low-to-mid 20s °C, which keeps evaporative and dry-cooler rejection viable for a warm loop. One hot-arid modelling study gives about 3,057 h/yr of air-side and 1,077 h/yr of water-side economizer availability, about 40% of the year (ScienceDirect) (verify: a single study; the split depends on assumed setpoints).

Who writes the class. The thermal criteria enter the Basis of Design through the owner's design engineer (Topic 2). The class binds the tenant: an A1 or H1 hall commits the operator to a tighter band and more energy than A2 or A3, and colocation SLAs historically excluded water at the rack, a clause AI density is forcing open. The reference hall writes A2-class air at 24 °C supply for the air zones and W40 water for the pods (§10).

Why the fan wall won. It scales with 5–10 MW halls, uses low-static horizontal airflow, is redundant at the fan rather than the unit, and, with EC fans at about 100% penetration in new Tier III and IV builds since 2024, reaches the fan energy a PUE of 1.3 needs; LONGWELL reports 38% less fan energy than CRAHs (verify: vendor figures). The air slice still matters: even when liquid takes the largest share, absolute air load grows with density (LG/IEA), so a good liquid loop cannot rescue an undersized air side.

Containment is the default. Hot-aisle containment is preferred over cold-aisle for new builds: Schneider Electric (Dunlap, Niemann) found the choice saves 43% of annual cooling energy cost, a 15% cut in annualized PUE, with the uncontained room held at 24 °C. Gains are higher supply temperatures, cube-law fan savings, roughly doubled effective capacity and no hot spots; SubZero measured top-to-bottom rack ΔT falling from over 10 °F to 1 °F. California Title 24 codifies it: economizers for 100% of load, variable-speed fans, and containment in large high-density air-cooled rooms, 2025 standards in force 1 January 2026. Arizona is outside Title 24; the reference BOD adopts the same rules because a PUE of 1.4 needs them.

Delivery. Fan walls and CRAHs are normally contractor-furnished, though hyperscale owners increasingly buy large air handlers as OFE to lock slots. The containment fit-out boundary with the tenant must say who supplies doors, roof panels and blanking panels; blanking discipline is a commissioning and operations hand-off (Topic 11), the price of turning the room into a plenum.

Economization modes for Phoenix, and what near-zero-water costs

Modes by climate fit (research §2.3): air-side direct outside air, cheapest where air is clean and mild, needs filtration and smoke handling, poor at a 45 °C peak; indirect evaporative or adiabatic, a heat exchanger keeps outside air out of the hall, wins in dry climates, uses water; water-side economizer, a plate-and-frame exchanger from condenser water to chilled water when wet bulb allows; adiabatic dry coolers, dry most of the year, spray only on the hottest hours.

What near-zero-water costs. Near-zero-water means dry rejection. At 45 °C dry bulb a dry cooler or air-cooled chiller has a poor approach and the compressor works hardest exactly when the grid is stressed, so a chilled loop rejected dry in Phoenix runs a partial PUE of about 1.3–1.4. A design PUE of 1.4 is defensible only because the warm loop recovers it: cold-plate heat on 36–45 °C water rejects on dry coolers with near-zero compressor hours at about 1.05–1.10, pulling the blend to about 1.2–1.3 (§12). Reclaimed water for peak adiabatic assist is the safety valve; blowdown goes to sewer (Topic 3).

An honest check on "dry most hours". A dry cooler needs its leaving water to sit several kelvin above the entering air. With FWS supply at 36 °C, dry operation is comfortable to an ambient in the high 20s °C; hotter afternoons need the pads, which pull entering air toward the wet bulb in the low-to-mid 20s. Phoenix has many summer hours above 30 °C, so the chapter's "peak days" means the hot hours of the hot season; the water budget is checked in §11 and holds. If hours prove long, the loop can float toward W40's 40 °C ceiling before any compressor runs (verify: needs the site weather file; the research gives economizer hours, not dry-cooler hours).

Supply temperatures and what they buy

The shift is from 7 °C chilled water to 18–30 °C for air loops and 30–45 °C facility water for liquid (research §3.2). Every degree warmer buys economizer hours and lets the chiller trip off; on W40 or W45 a Phoenix site runs compressor hours near zero for the liquid load. The reference design fixes 20 °C chilled supply and a W40 warm loop at 36 °C.

The trade-off (ASHRAE 2021): warmer coolant carries less heat per kelvin of headroom, so dense racks need more flow, bigger pumps and exchangers, or fewer blades; and rising chip power with falling case temperatures may regress the supportable class toward W32 or W27. The owner's efficiency and reuse interest wants the loop warm; the tenant's silicon headroom wants it cool.

GPU inlet floors. GB200 NVL72 (~120 kW) is cited in third-party guides at 18–25 °C facility supply, 40–50 °C return, cold plates rated to 1,200 W at 30–45 °C inlet; GB300 NVL72 (132–142 kW) carries a 45 °C design point, Lenovo listing facility water 2–50 °C (W45) with about 177 LPM per rack at 45 °C (verify: temperatures conflict by source and generation; §9). A colo cannot know its tenant's hardware, so the W40 loop carries a valved cross-tie from the chilled plant that can pull the FWS toward W32 for a pod that needs it, at the cost of compressor hours (§10).

Reuse grade. Liquid loops return 40–65 °C water, heat-pump grade; air halls exhaust 35–45 °C air, too low-grade without heat pumps (§11). The chapter's scale marks 55 °C as the top of the liquid-return range, the grade Odense lifts to district heat.

Plant sizing and ride-through

Heat to reject = IT + in-hall electrical losses (UPS, PDU, lighting, 6–8% of IT) + fan and pump heat inside the envelope. N+1 chillers and pumps per 10 MW hall and at the campus; headers and isolation valves arranged for concurrent maintainability (Tier III). Pipe velocity 1.5–3 m/s bounds pressure drop and erosion; a 10 MW loop at 10 K moves about 240 kg/s and needs roughly 14-inch mains (research §6.1). Phase 1's chilled loop carries 6.2 MW, for which 12-inch would do; the course keeps 14-inch mains and casts stubs and valved taps for 40 MW on day one, because pipe is cheap and a second trench through a live hall is not.

Parasitics and 90.4. Pumps, compressors, dry-cooler fans and fan walls are the mechanical load PUE and ASHRAE 90.4's annualized Mechanical Load Component measure; 90.4-2022 uses part-load-aware MLC and ELC ratios against climate-zone maxima, and addendum g credits recovered heat. Who owns the 90.4 calculation is named in the BOD, normally the mechanical engineer of record (research §3.4).

Ride-through. Generators take load within about 15 s (Topic 5), but chillers need about 5–15 minutes to restart conventionally (USPTO transient-cooling patents), about 3 minutes for the magnetic-bearing units claimed above; the window with no active cooling is 10–45 s (Bendel) (verify: secondary). At 120 kW per rack thermal time constants are seconds, so inlets rise measurably within a minute. A stratified chilled-water tank is the flywheel: cold water under warm across a thermocline, supply drawn from the bottom, return admitted at the top, sized in minutes for generator start plus chiller restart, 5–15; the course fixes 10 minutes on the chilled side only, since the warm loop has nothing to restart once dry-cooler fans and CDU pumps are back on generator.

Tank arithmetic (indicative). 6.2 MW for 600 s is 3,720 MJ; at a usable 10 K and 4.18 kJ/kg·K that is about 89,000 kg, roughly 90 m³ (about 24,000 US gallons); if the thermocline erodes the usable difference to 6 K, about 150 m³. Whether CDU pumps and fan walls ride the UPS through the 10–45 s gap is not in the research (verify); the course assumes they do, the conservative reading of "time constants are seconds".

Rear-door exchangers hang a coil on the rack's rear, passive or with EC fans: nVent RDHX Pro 68 kW at 14 °C water and 44 kW at 24 °C; Motivair ChilledDoor to 75 kW at 100% removal. Direct-to-chip single-phase (water-glycol) is the 2026 mainstream, 70–80% to liquid with 20–30% residual air; at 120 kW that is 24–36 kW per rack (the research's "per pod" means per rack), the load that keeps fan walls in the AI hall. Two-phase cold plates boil a dielectric for about 80% capture. Single-phase immersion takes about 100% at PUE 1.03–1.10 past 100 kW (Vertiv cites potential to ~900 kW per rack); two-phase immersion reaches 1.02–1.05.

The PFAS story. On 20 December 2022 3M announced its exit from PFAS manufacturing by end 2025, including all fluorinated fluids; the last Novec order date was 31 March 2025. EPA's hazardous-substance designation and EU restrictions leave two-phase methods at "immediate risk of obsolescence" (Motivair via DCD). Hydrocarbon two-phase fluids are not commercially qualified as of mid-2026; TSCA PFAS reporting for operators is due 31 January 2027. Single-phase DTC is now the dominant method at about 55% share (verify: market report). One chemistry decision pushed the industry back to the more complex single-phase water loop.

Loops and the CDU

The loops in order (research §4.2): chip to cold plate (Topic 8, up to the quick disconnect); rack manifold to blind-mate quick disconnects (GB200 uses Blindmate UQD04); the CDU, in-rack, in-row or facility-scale, a heat exchanger that hydraulically isolates the Technology Cooling System (TCS), the high-purity server loop, from the Facility Water System (FWS), with variable-speed pumps, filtration and dew-point-aware control (Vertiv; Stulz; Consulting-Specifying Engineer); then facility water to dry cooler or chiller.

Ratings. From 22 kW liquid-to-air (Eaton 10U) to 1 MW (LiquidStack, about 1.25 LPM per kW at a 4 °C approach), 2 MW at 3 °C (Eaton ROL4000) and 5 MW facility distribution units (DCX FDU, about 4 °C, N+1 four-pump); HPE ships a 1.3 MW in-row CDU for up to 8 racks. Commercial approach is 3–4 °C; the flow rule is 1.15–1.5 LPM per kW of liquid-captured heat (LiquidStack). The reference hall runs one 1 MW-class CDU per pod of about eight racks, in Topic 4's CDU room, piped overhead to the pod manifold, seven for six pods (6 + 1).

Fluids and quality. 25–30% propylene glycol (PG25) on the TCS; a wetted-materials list, leak detection with automated isolation valves and a clean flush at commissioning are mandatory (ASHRAE Liquid Cooling Guidelines; OCP Advanced Cooling Solutions). NVIDIA specifies ≤ 25 µS/cm conductivity and < 50 µm particulate. These are contract terms as much as engineering: the BOD says who owns the CDU, who proves water quality at hand-over, and who owns a leak on each side of the quick disconnect (research §4.5).

NVIDIA racks, indicative. GB200 NVL72: about 120 kW (1,200 W × 72), about 2 L/s (120 LPM) into the cabinet (NVIDIA via Fibermall), 18–25 °C supply and 40–50 °C return in third-party guides, cold plates to 1,200 W at 30–45 °C inlet. GB300 NVL72 (Blackwell Ultra, GTC March 2025): 132–142 kW (about 1,400 W per GPU), facility water 2–50 °C (W45) per Lenovo, NVIDIA design point 45 °C, about 177 LPM at 45 °C, Microsoft Azure citing about 136 kW per rack, about 90% to liquid (verify: the GB200 figure is third-party, the GB300 figure NVIDIA's; the course designs for W40 and hedges with the cross-tie).

Delivery. CDUs are variously owner-, contractor- or IT-vendor-furnished; the reference hall makes them OFE, ordered at land close. The TCS loop is commissioned jointly by the mechanical contractor, the CDU vendor and the rack integrator (parties only; levels in Topic 11). Overhead pipe competes with Topic 9's pathways; Topic 4 fixed the stack as busway highest, fiber raceway and copper tray next, liquid manifolds lowest.

The reference pod path, reconciled

The research's numbers do not close. Research §4.4 puts a TCS at 32 °C supply and 40 °C return above an FWS at 36–45 °C through a 3–4 °C approach. An exchanger cannot deliver 32 °C TCS water from 36 °C facility water; the TCS supply must sit above the FWS supply by the approach. Either the FWS is cooler than stated, making it a W32 loop that needs a chiller in Phoenix summers, or the TCS is warmer.

The course's ladder takes the second reading as its stated assumption: FWS 36 °C supply, about 46 °C return (W40, 10 K); TCS about 40 °C supply, about 48 °C return (8 K); cold-end approach 40 − 36 = 4 K, hot-end 48 − 46 = 2 K, consistent with a commercial unit. A 40 °C TCS supply sits inside GB200's 30–45 °C cold-plate rating and under GB300's 45 °C design point with about 5 K margin. FWS flow is about 80% of TCS flow because the temperature differences differ.

Per-rack flow, checked. The liquid share of a 120 kW rack is 70–80%, about 90 kW. At 8 K on water: 90 ÷ (4.18 × 8) ≈ 2.7 kg/s, about 161 LPM; PG25's lower specific heat lifts it toward 170. The research's 150–180 LPM and 1.25–1.5 LPM per kW therefore describe the liquid share, not all 120 kW: 150 LPM at 8 K carries about 84 kW, 180 LPM about 100 kW. A pod of eight racks moves about 0.72 MW and 1,300–1,400 LPM through its CDU, inside a 1 MW-class unit with reserve for GB300.

The residual-air correction. Research §6.1 splits Phase 1 as "6 MW liquid, 4 MW air"; only 70–80% of the pods' 6 MW reaches liquid, and 1.2–1.8 MW leaves as air. Corrected: about 4.5 MW on the warm loop and 5.5 MW of IT on chilled water (4 MW air and rear-door zones plus about 1.5 MW pod residual), before losses (§12). The pod also holds about one air-cooled 20–30 kW network rack per two GPU racks (Topic 9), outside the 1,000-rack count, cooled by the same fan-wall air; the fan walls' capacity includes it.

The cross-tie. Because the owner cannot know whether a tenant lands GB200-class racks specified at 18–25 °C or GB300-class racks designed for 45 °C, the FWS carries a valved cross-tie from the chilled plant, a plate exchanger or blended-return connection that can pull a pod's loop toward W32. Normally closed, it costs compressor hours only when used, and it is one reason the chilled plant's 2 + 1 headroom matters.

Water and heat reuse

WUE, defined once. Water Usage Effectiveness (The Green Grid, WP#35, 2011) = annual site water (L) ÷ IT energy (kWh). Site WUE counts water used at the facility; source WUE adds the water embedded in generating the electricity. Consumptive use is evaporated or lost; non-consumptive is withdrawn and returned. WUE gives no credit for reclaimed water or blowdown reuse (DCD), so the purple pipe does not lower the number; only running dry does.

Typical values, and where sources differ. LBNL's 2024 United States Data Center Energy Usage Report finds average site WUE just over 0.36 L/kWh through 2023. The widely quoted 1.8–1.9 L/kWh "industry average" traces to 2025 secondary guides (Data Center Knowledge; EESI), not LBNL; Topic 3 carried the 1.8 figure and is being corrected to cite both (verify: the two may measure different populations, a fleet-weighted estimate against evaporative-cooled sites). Evaporative cooling runs to 2.5 L/kWh; dry rejection approaches 0. Equinix's 2025 Sustainability Report gives 0.91 L/kWh portfolio-wide, 1.41 at evaporative sites, beside a 1.37 PUE. The OPR's ≤ 0.2 L/kWh, less than a cup per kWh, is achievable only with dry or near-zero-water rejection.

The reference budget, checked (course arithmetic). 10 MW of IT for a year is about 87.6 GWh; at 0.2 L/kWh the budget is about 17,500 m³. A bounding case in which the whole 4.5 MW warm-loop load were removed by evaporation (2.45 MJ/kg) uses about 1.8 kg/s, roughly 6,600 L/h, about 0.66 L/kWh while spraying; even so the budget covers about 2,600 spray hours a year, and real adiabatic pre-cooling evaporates far less because the coils still work dry. The budget holds if the pads are controlled on wet bulb rather than left on for the season.

Zero-water designs. Microsoft's next-generation design (announced 9 December 2024, applied to all designs from August 2024) is closed-loop chip-level cooling that "consumes zero water for cooling", filled once at construction; Steve Solomon: "this design will avoid the need for more than 125 million liters of water per year per datacenter" (Microsoft Cloud Blog). Microsoft's WUE fell from 0.49 (2021) to 0.30 L/kWh (2024); pilots in Phoenix, AZ and Mt. Pleasant, WI come online from late 2027 (verify: announced dates). The cost is PUE and capital: dropping evaporation raises energy, partly offset by warmer, more efficient chillers, and adds exchanger and dry-cooler capital. Vantage's Phoenix campus advertises near-zero WUE via closed-loop air-cooled chillers with air-side economization.

Heat reuse. Plausibility is set by return temperature: liquid returns 40–65 °C water, air halls 35–45 °C air. Meta's Odense data center feeds Fjernvarme Fyn's district network through ammonia heat pumps lifting about 40 °C water to 70–75 °C, a roughly 45 MW system by Ramboll, about 165,000 MWh/yr for about 11,000 households (earlier 100,000 MWh, 6,900 homes). LRZ SuperMUC-NG runs 40–45 °C water into adsorption chillers at a partial cooling PUE below 0.07 (ASHRAE 2021). The EU Energy Efficiency Directive (2023/1791, Art. 12) makes centers of 500 kW and above report energy, WUE, ERF and waste heat annually, and centers above 1 MW assess and implement waste-heat recovery unless infeasible; Germany's EnEfG (November 2023) sets Energy Reuse Factor quotas of 10% from 1 July 2026, 15% from 2027, 20% from 2028, plus PUE ≤ 1.2 for new builds. Phoenix has no heat customer: a hot arid metro has no district-heating demand and no sink for 45–65 °C water, and heat does not travel far. The reference hall builds no reuse; its loop temperatures leave the option open.

Delivery. WUE and reuse commitments are OPR terms and often permit conditions; missing them is permit and contractual liability. Sourcing, rights, NPDES and sewer are Topic 3.

PUE build-up (Phoenix year). Fence power = IT 10.0 + electrical losses 0.7 + warm-loop mechanical about 0.3 (dry-cooler fans, FWS and CDU pumps) + chilled-loop mechanical about 1.5 (compressors, pumps, fan walls) ≈ 12.5 MW, PUE ≈ 1.25. By path: the warm side runs about 1.05–1.10 (0.3 on 4.5 MW); the chilled side about 1.3–1.4 once its share of electrical losses is included (1.5 plus about 0.4 of the 0.7, on 5.5 MW). The chilled loop carries a bit over half the heat, 6.2 of 10.7 MW, and about 80% of the cooling electricity, 1.5 of 1.8 MW; 1.5 MW on 1,760 tons is about 0.85 kW per ton all-in, plausible for an air-cooled plant in high ambient. In the monsoon week, wet bulb up, pads spraying, chillers at full lift, the blend rises to 1.35–1.45; the OPR's 1.4 is at risk only that week, which is why the dry coolers get reclaimed-water assist. Every rack moved from the chilled to the warm loop lowers PUE, so the campus at 40 MW, later halls skewed to the pods, reads better than Hall 1.

Growth to 40 MW. Repeat the module per hall; campus N+1 across four halls with one optional 2N suite (Topic 2); concurrently maintainable headers on the stubs cast in Phase 1.

Scope split. OFE: chillers, CDUs, dry coolers. CFE: piping, headers, valves, fan walls, containment. Tenant or IT vendor: rack manifolds and quick disconnects in the wholesale hall, the operator in retail, pinned in the BOD. The TCS loop is commissioned jointly by the mechanical contractor, the CDU vendor and the rack integrator (Topic 11). The "rack that arrives before its CDU" is the signature failure: GPU racks on short lead times landing before the 26–52 week CDU is set, piped, charged and commissioned. Mitigation: order CDUs at land close, reserve slots, stage commissioning.

Verdicts on the course thresholds. Topic 2's density scale is broadly right and lightly dated: containment realistically reaches ~30 kW with disciplined airflow, so 20 kW is the comfort limit, not the wall; direct-to-chip is effectively mandatory above ~50–80 kW, with air and rear doors overlapping DTC at 40–80 and DTC alone above 80. Corrected bands: open air ≤ 10; containment 10–30; rear-door and in-row 20–80; DTC 50–200+ (GB300 at 142); immersion 100–250 (Vertiv: potential to ~900); marks at 20, 45, 80, 132, plus 142. On PUE by regime: Phoenix room air on dry rejection can exceed 1.5 on peak days, so 1.4–1.6; rear doors capture 80–100% of sensible heat, not 50–80%, though 1.2–1.35 is fair; DTC on W40/W45 dry-cooled reaches 1.1–1.2 annualized, immersion 1.03–1.10; switch points stay at about 20, 45 and 80 kW, and regime PUE depends more on rejection strategy and climate than on density.
