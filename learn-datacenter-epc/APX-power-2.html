Appendix: Power Systems II, Distribution and Redundancy

The detail behind the Topic 6 chapter: the chain from the UPS output bus to the dual-corded server, what leaves with the PDU transformer, the static transfer switch, busway against cable, the voltage argument, the redundancy models and their arithmetic, 48 V and ±400 V DC in AI racks, grounding and protection, the emergency power off button, and the reference hall's distribution worked zone by zone. The chapter cites sections by number (APX-power-2 §n). Sources are in parentheses as the research gave them; "(verify)" marks figures flagged as single-vendor, dated, inconsistent or moving fast. Upstream of the UPS output bus is Topic 5; the rack is Topic 8; suppression is Topic 10. All numbers are indicative, 2025–26.

The chain, and why a transformer sat in the PDU

The links. UPS output bus, 480/277 V or 415/240 V three-phase, in 2–3 MW blocks. Floor PDU, 300–1,000 kVA, with an input breaker, voltage-changing equipment where needed and branch panels, 3,000–5,000 lb (Buildermuse, 2026). Optional STS, 250–4,000 A (LayerZero, 2025). Busway or cable-and-RPP. The whip or tap-off. The rack PDU, a 0U strip at 208 V or 415/240 V, commonly 30–60 A three-phase, with units to ~130 kW (Raritan PX4; Introl, 2025). The dual-corded load (Schneider Electric, Vertiv, Raritan white papers; Data Center Strategy, 2025). The chapter omits the optional STS from its main path (§2).

Why the transformer was there. A legacy PDU isolation transformer changes voltage and creates a separately derived system, with a new neutral and ground-fault reference. The North American legacy stepped 480 V down to 208/120 V for servers that took 120 V (US Patent 6,967,283).

What the reference hall removes. The hall omits that legacy 480-to-208 V isolation transformer, not every voltage-changing device. Topic 5 fixes the reference UPS output at 480Y/277 V. A designed 480-to-415 V autotransformer arrangement must therefore supply 415/240 V, including a suitable neutral, bonding and ground-fault protection arrangement. An autotransformer does not itself create a separately derived system. Silverback DC Solutions describes this device as 90% smaller and less expensive than a 120/208 V PDU isolation transformer; that is a vendor comparison, not a hall cost estimate. Only the separate case of a UPS natively supplying 415/240 V can feed suitable busway without an intervening voltage-change transformer, the ‘no intermediate transformation’ case (Server Technology, 415 VAC Power Distribution, 2014).

Removing legacy isolation impedance can raise available fault current. One vendor compares ~17,576 A with a 480-to-208 V transformer and ~56,144 A in a different 415 V scheme without it (Vertiv/Liebert, Proper Application of 415V Systems, R1010). Those are example schemes, not a calculation for the reference autotransformer (verify: vendor-specific comparison). Short-circuit, coordination and arc-flash studies must calculate the actual installation (§10); §12 distinguishes them from Topic 5’s ~50 kA upstream figure.

The static transfer switch

An STS uses thyristors to transfer a load between two sources. About 4 ms is an indicative sub-cycle transfer time, conditional on the equipment, both sources being suitable and the circumstances of transfer, not a guaranteed interruption time for every load (Schneider Electric WP62; LayerZero). It can keep single-corded gear supplied from A or B. An ATS transfers electromechanically, generally more slowly and with simpler switching hardware.

Failure modes and placement. Phase angle, SCR state, breaker state and both source voltages must be monitored, giving an STS more failure modes than a simple ATS (Schneider WP62). A large centralized STS can itself become a failure point for multiple racks (TRG Datacenters, 2025). The reference design puts a rack-local ATS or STS only where a single-corded device needs both sources. Dual-corded racks take A and B directly; distributed 3M2 does not require a bank of central transfer switches.

Shared-bus propagation. A load fault can affect every load on a shared bus if protection fails to clear it at the branch before the bus collapses (Vertiv/Emerson technical note TN-00011). Selective coordination is intended to contain a downstream branch fault, while a fault within the common bus itself can interrupt that entire bus run (§3, §10). Dual-cording lets the other independent path carry the load if it remains healthy. Any quantity of local switches must be derived from the actual single-corded equipment schedule, not assumed as a Phase 1 central STS count (§12).

Busway against cable-and-RPP

A remote power panel is a breaker panel near racks fed by fixed cable home-runs from the PDU. It can cost less initially when loads stay stable; adding a run later means installation work in an operating hall, subject to site permits and procedures (aidatacenterguide.com, 2025). Busway is prefabricated overhead conductor track with tap-off points: higher initial material cost, lower installation labor in the cited comparison, about 4–5 prefabricated pieces per 40 ft, and easier rearrangement (Semianalysis; DCD, 2025). Whether a tap-off can be inserted on an energized run depends on the listed busway and plug, site procedure and applicable electrical-safety rules. A live-insertion feature is not blanket permission for energized work. Overhead routing also frees floor space. Busway fits dense, changing rows; cable fits small, stable rooms (Starline/Legrand; Vertiv).

Ratings, cost and supply. Starline Track Busway spans 40–1,250 A at IP2X–IP54, with feeders to 6,300 A (Starline/Legrand). In a vendor model of a 28-rack row with 400 A A/B redundancy, a new plug box cost about $350 and the owner installed it, versus contractor mobilization for another whip (Starline, Cost Analysis, 2019) (verify: dated vendor scenario, not a current installed price). The reference design keeps cable-and-RPP for 300 stable 8 kW racks (§12). With about 94% of scheduled rack MW on busway, its high-ampacity supply and copper pricing are schedule risks alongside switchgear and UPS (research, open questions).

A downstream rack-branch fault should clear at that branch if protection coordinates. A fault in the common busway conductor is upstream of its taps and can interrupt the run; a fault in a common panel can similarly interrupt that panel. Larger shared equipment also demands its own fault and arc-flash assessment (§10).

Voltage: what 415/240 V buys and what it costs

At fixed power, higher voltage lowers current, and resistive loss in a given conductor falls with the square of current. Moving from 208 V to 415 V nearly doubles three-phase power at the same current (asbeam.com, 2025). One equipment-specific server-PSU comparison reports ~85.5% efficiency at 240 V, 82% at 120 V and ~84% at 208 V (Mirus International, HMRP WP001). Eaton gives ~89% chain efficiency for a particular native EU 400/230 V comparison. Avoiding a legacy PDU isolation transformer can avoid its cited 1–2% loss, but these percentages are not guaranteed for the hall’s equipment.

The reference voltage step. Standard IT supplies rated to 240 V cannot simply take 277 V line-to-neutral from 480Y/277 V; direct use would require suitable equipment (CSE). Topic 5 fixes this hall’s UPS output at 480Y/277 V. Its floor distribution retains a designed 480-to-415 V autotransformer and neutral arrangement to supply 415/240 V. Only a native 415/240 V UPS output is the separate direct-feed, no-voltage-change case (Server Technology, 415 VAC Power Distribution, 2014; Silverback DC Solutions). Available fault current for either design requires its own calculation. The ~17.6-to-56 kA comparison describes two vendor example schemes, not this autotransformer (§1, §10).

The 60 A calculation. For this chapter’s illustrative 80% continuous-loading assumption, usable three-phase power is √3 × voltage × 60 A × 0.8: about 17.3 kW at 208 V and 34.5 kW at 415 V, or about 2.0×. This 80% assumption is not a universal rule for every listed circuit. asbeam.com (2025) instead pairs 17.3 kW with 39.9 kW at 415 V and calls it a 130% increase (verify: the latter omits the 80% factor). The same illustrative calculation at 400 V gives about 33 kW.

A 120 kW rack. One 415 V, 60 A circuit supplies about 34.5 kW on that assumption. Four equivalents are needed on each independently survivable side, eight single-input 60 A rack PDUs if each takes one circuit. Alternatively, two suitably protected approximately 125 A rack-PDU inputs per side can be supplied from a roughly 250 A side tap-off, four higher-current strips total. A 160 A tap-off supplies only about 92 kW at 80% and cannot carry the rack alone. Final connectors, protection and rack-PDU ratings need equipment-specific design (Server Technology math; Introl: at 480 V three-phase, 100 kW requires only 120 A per circuit).

A/B sizing, single-corded gear, metering, and who delivers what

Size for survival, measure normal sharing. Each independent side of a dual-corded rack must be able to carry its full load when the other fails: do not size both sides on an assumed permanent 50/50 split unless policy and equipment allow it (calcpanel.com, 2025). Normal sharing of roughly half the rack load on each route is an operating expectation to check by metering, not something wiring alone enforces. For a 120 kW rack, four 415 V, 60 A equivalents supply about 138 kW usable per side at the chapter’s illustrative 80%. Four separate 60 A inputs on each side imply eight single-input strips total. A different arrangement uses a roughly 250 A tap-off on each side, about 144 kW usable, feeding two suitably protected approximately 125 A rack-PDU inputs per side, four higher-current strips total. A 160 A side tap-off has only about 92 kW usable on the same assumption and is insufficient. Listings, conductor sizing, protection and phase balance determine the build (§4).

Single-corded gear and metering. A local rack ATS or STS can select A or B for a single-corded appliance; its failure then affects that appliance (TRG Datacenters). The AI pods’ network racks, about one air-cooled 20–30 kW rack per two GPU racks (Topic 9), take the pod A/B pair where equipment is dual-corded. Branch-circuit and per-outlet metering help prove source mapping and detect a server drawing on only one supply before a path fails (Obelinf, 2026). Topic 11 covers the monitoring stack.

Delivery boundaries. PDUs, STS where needed and busway are often owner-furnished on design-build jobs, with the contractor installing and terminating them. Wholesale demarcation may be the PDU output breaker, with tenants supplying racks and contracting for whips and cable plant; some providers instead offer rack-ready suites (Sentinel Data Centers). Retail delivery commonly extends to busway or RPP and often the whip, while wholesale tenants more often fit out the white space (Obelinf; DataCenterKnowledge, 2025). The reference hall writes each wholesale breaker or tap-off boundary, and each retail rack-ready scope, into the lease. A future tenant or IT-vendor sidecar has a separately agreed busbar interface (§9). The lease also assigns review and funding of studies affected by tenant changes (§10).

Redundancy models and who uses them

Module capacity is not a path or a Tier. N installs just enough capacity. N+1 adds one module beyond demand and can cover one module failure or its maintenance, without necessarily making a distribution route maintainable. 2N provides two systems each sized for the whole load; 2N+1 adds spare module capacity to duplicated systems. Distributed 3M2 or 4M3 shares demand among three or four systems so one can be lost: 3M2 uses at most about 66.7% of installed capacity; 4M3, about 75% (CSE/Caterpillar). A block-redundant catcher has active blocks and an unloaded reserve reached through transfer equipment, allowing higher normal loading of active UPS blocks (Socomec; STACK). These labels describe equipment arrangements, not facility-wide path maintainability or an awarded Tier.

The 3M2 seating plan. Connect rows to A/B, B/C and A/C in turn. On losing B, the first row moves wholly to A, the second wholly to C, and the third remains shared. Each survivor must be able to take its reassigned load without a central STS in the dual-corded rack path. For the same 10 MW usable Phase 1 baseline, two three-block groups of 2.5 MW require six blocks, 15 MW installed. Full 2N instead needs two complete 10 MW routes, eight 2.5 MW blocks and 20 MW installed (§12).

Use and indicative figures. Enterprise sites may use N+1; banks, 2N or 2N+1; colocation and cloud, mixes of 2N and 3M2/4M3 (CSE, Data center co-location electrical system design). CoreSite VA3 combines 2N, four-to-make-three and a catcher UPS (CoreSite). The following legacy availability figures are illustrative industry estimates, not Uptime certification or predictions for this hall (§7). Downtime, where quoted, equals (1 − availability) × 8,760 h.

Failure math, Tiers, and the AI relaxation

Conditional calculations. For a repairable element, availability A = MTBF / (MTBF + MTTR). Series availabilities multiply; independent parallel-path unavailabilities multiply (IEEE 493, the Gold Book, now Inactive-Reserved, with guidance in the IEEE 3006 series). If one path has 99.9% availability, its illustrative downtime is 8.76 h/year. If two such paths fail independently, their sources and transfer arrangements function as designed, and the repair model applies, 0.001 × 0.001 = 0.000001 unavailability, about 31.5 s/year (rule sourced; arithmetic illustrative). The approximation pair MTBF ≈ MTBF² / (2 × MTTR) gives ~4.5 million hours for two units each at 3,000 h MTBF and 1 h MTTR, versus ~187,500 h at 24 h MTTR (InventiveHQ). Shared faults, human action and failed transfers can invalidate the independence assumption. Spares and repair response matter to the assumed MTTR.

Tiers are not uptime percentages. Uptime Institute ‘removed all references to expected downtime per year’ in 2009 and ‘does not assign availability predictions to Tier levels’ (Uptime Institute Journal). Tier III requires concurrent maintainability across the assessed facility; Tier IV requires facility-wide fault tolerance and physical separation against a single event (Uptime Institute). Neither means a planned maintenance event plus an unrelated second fault is automatically harmless. TIA-942 uses Rated-1 through Rated-4, with Rated-3 concurrently maintainable; BICSI 002 is a companion ‘intended to complement and exceed the minimum requirements in TIA-942’ (TechTarget; TIA). Equipment counts cannot certify any of these designs.

Workload-specific AI relaxation, dated. Some operators accept N or N+1 compute blocks while retaining greater protection for networks, storage and controls, where checkpoint-restart makes interruption tolerable to those operators (ModulEdge citing Uptime Institute, 2025). AWS reports a best-case GPU-node MTTR of ~12 minutes using fail-in-place N+1 (AWS, 2025). NVIDIA Kyber and OCP sidecar proposals contemplate single-corded GPU racks (verify: secondary citations). That is not a general permission to relax a multi-tenant colo: the reference hall keeps dual-corded AI pods and its 3M2 facility design.

Changing the model mid-design

The owner sets the model in the OPR, the engineer renders it in the BOD, and the provider describes the delivered redundancy and availability commitment in the SLA (Topic 2).

A dated procurement decision. Changing 2N to 3M2, or the reverse, alters UPS blocks, PDUs and their pairings, any switches justified by single-corded loads, upstream switchgear, busway routes and electrical-room space. The chapter’s six-block 3M2 and eight-block 2N counts are alternatives for the same 10 MW usable baseline, not two entitlements bought together (§12). Topic 3’s planning sequence reserves factory slots around T+3–6 and releases manufacture near T+8, after a 60% one-line. As dated inputs, Buildermuse (2026) quotes 40–60 weeks for MV switchgear, while Topics 3 and 5 carry a wider 44–84-week range. Neither is a delivery promise; late topology changes may move the schedule (Buildermuse, 2026).

Freeze the baseline; price the option. The reference OPR fixes 3M2 on 2.5 MW blocks before long-lead release. A tenant’s optional carved-out 2N suite needs a separate scope, cost and capacity allocation. For example, a 2.5 MW usable 2N suite needs its own 5 MW installed block-pair capacity and duplicated downstream routes; it is not free capacity within the six blocks already assigned to 10 MW of 3M2 service. Adding it without reducing the baseline requires additional capacity. Taking its load from the baseline instead requires redesigning and explicitly reducing or reallocating baseline capacity. Its additional cost needs pricing rather than being presumed covered by the six-block budget (verify: project-specific price).

The course’s first-phase budget is about $180 million–$230 million because it includes shared site and shell work for the four-hall build; the full campus is about $400 million–$500 million excluding IT, with about $65 million annual operations including about $34 million electricity (chapter course decisions). These are not distribution-only costs or four identical phase invoices. Sidecar space and pathways may be reserved while the DC equipment and interface remain a later decision (§9); an actual DC conversion still requires engineering and procurement review.

48 V, ±400 V DC and the copper wall

48 V and ORv3. The OCP Open Rack v3 shelf rectifies facility AC to a shared 48 V rack busbar, reducing per-server conversion stages. Google attributes about a 30% efficiency improvement to its earlier 12-to-48 V transition, begun in 2010 and contributed to OCP in 2016–2017. That is a comparison of Google designs, not a guaranteed efficiency gain for this hall (DataCenterKnowledge; Google Cloud Blog). ORv3 shelves reach 33 kW at near 98% in an equipment example (Advanced Energy).

A scaling constraint, not a prohibition at 100 kW. Delivering 120 kW at an ideal 48 V draws 2,500 A. A 0.1 mΩ increase at one connector at that current dissipates about 625 W. NVIDIA’s example of a 1 MW rack at 54 V exceeds 18,000 A and cites busbars over 200 kg and up to 64 U of power shelves (The Register; NVIDIA Technical Blog / Converge Digest, 2025). These figures explain why copper, connections and shelf space become difficult as density rises; they do not establish an absolute 100 kW limit for every 48 V design.

High-voltage DC proposals. OCP Mt Diablo, or Diablo 400, co-authored by Microsoft, Meta and Google, specifies a sidecar using ±400 V DC rails, 800 V rail to rail, for the 100 kW–1 MW design range; its authors chose 400 V partly to use the EV supply chain (OCP, 2025; v0.7.0 spec). NVIDIA targets 1 MW racks starting in 2027 alongside Kyber, including an ~600 kW NVL576 example. Its May 2025 blog, updated July 2025, claims up to 5% end-to-end efficiency improvement and 45% less copper compared with 415 VAC distribution, with production targeted to coincide with Kyber in 2027 (NVIDIA Technical Blog, 20 May 2025, updated 31 July 2025; ModulEdge, 2026). Two published conductor comparisons differ: 85% more transmitted power through the same conductor size in the Technical Blog, and 157% more with the same wire gauge in NVIDIA’s Building the 800 VDC Ecosystem (verify: comparison bases do not reconcile). None is a measured saving for the reference hall.

Status and interface. Reports date Vertiv’s portfolio to H2 2026, Eaton’s reference architecture to October 2025 and initial small-volume shipments to Q3 2026 (szsanyi.com; BigGo, 2026) (verify: trade-press dates and targets). AMD had not committed to a proprietary path (Drybulb, 2026). Listings, adopted codes and DC-arc protection still need project-specific review; a DC arc lacks AC’s periodic current zero (szsanyi.com). Reserve structural capacity, floor bays and pathways, while recognizing the risk that AC assets could be stranded (aidatacenterguide.com). The illustrated future sidecar cannot connect directly to an existing 48 V busbar: suitable conversion and a specified interface are required. Nor is existing AC busway assumed listed or compatible for 800 V DC. The sidecar is a potential tenant or IT-vendor item with a newly negotiated electrical demarcation (research, §3.3–3.4; §12).

Grounding, harmonics, arc-flash and coordination

Grounding. Data-center grounding may go beyond life-safety code minima (eypmcfinc.com). A common bonding network can include a signal reference grid; the report describes example copper grids on 0.6–3 m centres using at least #6 AWG, citing IEEE 1100 and TIA-942 Appendix G. TIA-607-C defines a bonding-busbar hierarchy; example cited conductors are 1/0 AWG to a busbar and at least 6 AWG to each cabinet, without serial cabinet bonding (ecmag.com; pducables.com). These are not substitute specifications for the site’s adopted standards, fault levels and equipment. The signal-grid noise case is weaker with active-PFC equipment, though grids may remain useful or be required by a vendor (Schneider SRG white paper). TN-S separates neutral and protective earth; the reference autotransformer’s neutral and bonding arrangement must be designed, not inferred from the legacy isolation transformer (§1).

Harmonics. Modern active-PFC server supplies can draw near-sinusoidal current with under 5% THD in the cited examples (EmergEdge; 3dfs WP55). K-13 for mixed loads and the view that K-20 is often unnecessary are equipment-dependent observations (green-data blog). Concentrated identical AI supplies warrant checking whether a different harmonic rating or mitigation is needed (QT&E, 2025). Do not prescribe K-13 for every reference autotransformer or K-20 for every AI pod without load and transformer data.

Arc-flash. Energized electrical work requires assessment and, where applicable, an NFPA 70E 130.2(B) permit; continuity needs do not themselves authorize it (nfpa70e.net). Equipment labels and arc-flash boundaries follow the applicable study and NFPA 70E requirements; 1.2 cal/cm² is the cited boundary criterion (US Made Supply; payapress). Available fault current and protective clearing time affect incident energy. PPE selection and any high-energy work decision require the applicable risk assessment: NFPA 70E does not categorically ban all energized work above 40 cal/cm². Remote racking, maintenance switches that shorten clearing time, arc-resistant gear and IR windows are possible engineering controls (US Made Supply; Bowtie).

Fault current and coordination. Vertiv/Liebert, Proper Application of 415V Systems, R1010, compares about 17.6 kA behind a 480-to-208 V isolation transformer with about 56 kA in a different 415 V scheme without it. These are two vendor example schemes, not the reference autotransformer’s calculated available fault current. A downstream rack fault should clear at its branch, rather than at a PDU or UPS, where the installed protective devices can be selectively coordinated. A fault in the shared bus or panel is a different event (§3). Interrupting ratings and short-circuit current ratings must suit the calculated location; a cited Bussmann CUBEFuse has a 300 kA interrupting rating at 600 V, which does not by itself establish system coordination (asbeam.com).

Grounding, short-circuit, coordination and incident-energy studies are site-specific stamped PE deliverables (Bowtie Engineering). The cited review interval is at least every five years or after relevant upstream changes (payapress); actual updates also depend on adopted rules and changes to sources, protection or tenant equipment. The reference lease assigns review before affected tenant equipment is energized and funding through fit-out (§5).

EPO: the code, the opt-out and the accident record

The code trade. Under the cited NEC 2020 edition, Article 645.10 specifies a disconnecting means for IT power and dedicated HVAC and closure of fire and smoke dampers in qualifying IT equipment rooms. Article 645 also offers special wiring allowances, such as certain raised-floor whips (CSE; thebuildingcodeforum). The actual requirements depend on the adopted edition and the authority having jurisdiction, or AHJ.

A conditional ordinary-wiring route. Designing to ordinary Chapter 3 wiring methods, with applicable low-voltage cabling requirements, can avoid Article 645’s EPO requirement if the adopted code and AHJ permit that route. It does not waive other fire, shutdown or occupancy requirements (CSE; thebuildingcodeforum). The route dates to at least the 2005 NEC; editions from 2011 also provided zoned shutdown and critical-operations alternatives subject to their conditions, including approved procedures, qualified staff, fire protection and AHJ approval. A reported 2026 draft would move conditions from 645.4 to 645.5 and update Article 722 references, not establish the locally adopted rule (thebuildingcodeforum, 2025). NFPA 75 8.4.2.1 addresses disconnection on gaseous total-flooding activation unless a Chapter 4 risk analysis warrants continued power; NFPA 76 is reported not to impose an EPO requirement. The fire design and adopted requirements must still be checked.

The accident record, with its limits. Practitioners say some owners find Article 645’s wiring benefits not worth its prerequisites (mikeholt practitioners). For NYC Transit Rail Control Center on 29 August 2021, the UPS rode through a ConEd voltage dip, with both systems reported to have worked as intended. The investigation identified manual activation of an uncovered EPO as the outage’s most likely cause, not a proven general failure mechanism (WSP USA report, 8 September 2021, via governor.ny.gov). Georgia Tech CCB247 reported a roughly 96-minute outage after accidental EPO activation during power work (Georgia Tech TSO notice). A 2010 Ponemon survey reports ‘accidental EPO/human error (51 percent)’ as a combined root-cause category, not 51% EPO incidents. Separate, older vendor accounts cite 20 EPO incidents, or 13% of UPS failures, in 2002–03 and EPO in 26% of human-error failures (pducables ‘Ken’s Korner’; DataCenterKnowledge, 2007) (verify: dated samples). Uptime describes accidental EPO as recurring and advises against installation unless locally required.

The reference decision. The Uptime Institute Annual Outage Analysis 2025 reports nearly 40% of surveyed organizations experienced a major human-error outage in three years, 85% of those involving procedure failure or flawed procedure; these are not EPO incident rates (Uptime Institute press release, 6 May 2025). The hall proposes Chapter 3 wiring and no Article 645 button, conditional on early AHJ and fire-marshal approval and an approved emergency response. Topic 10’s no-gas white-space choice removes the cited gaseous-system trigger, not every possible fire requirement. A tenant suite requiring an EPO needs an approved, guarded and zoned design. An EPO installed for an approved design must not later be disabled or bypassed by operations.

Reference hall: distribution by zone, worked

Loads and block count. The rack-mix sum is 300 × 8 + 300 × 15 + 200 × 40 + 200 × 120 kW = 38.9 MW. About 40 MW is the rounded IT planning load; at PUE 1.4, about 56 MW is rounded total site demand, not another rack-circuit schedule (chapter course decisions). Topic 5’s five 2.5 MW N+1 blocks for 10 MW describe capacity spare, not necessarily maintainable distribution paths. The reference Phase 1 instead uses two 3M2 groups: each has three 2.5 MW blocks, 7.5 MW installed and 5 MW usable; loss of a block leaves two carrying 2.5 MW each. Thus six blocks, 15 MW installed, serve 10 MW usable. Alternative full 2N service of the same 10 MW needs eight blocks, 20 MW installed. A catcher scheme needs transfer design in its active paths; ordinary N+1 alone does not establish Tier III. Four repetitions of the six-block baseline would give 24 blocks and 60 MW installed for about 40 MW usable. Neither count certifies a Tier (§6, §7).
