Appendix: Power Systems II, Distribution and Redundancy

Sources and fine detail behind the chapter. Sections are numbered so the chapter can point at them.

The detail behind the Topic 6 chapter: the chain from the UPS output bus to the dual-corded server, what leaves with the PDU transformer, the static transfer switch, busway against cable, the voltage argument, the redundancy models and their arithmetic, 48 V and ±400 V DC in AI racks, grounding and protection, the emergency power off button, and the reference hall's distribution worked zone by zone. The chapter cites sections by number (APX-power-2 §n). Sources are in parentheses as the research gave them; "(verify)" marks figures flagged as single-vendor, dated, inconsistent or moving fast. Upstream of the UPS output bus is Topic 5; the rack is Topic 8; suppression is Topic 10. All numbers are indicative, 2025–26.

The chain, and why a transformer sat in the PDU

The links. UPS output bus, 480/277 V or 415/240 V three-phase, in 2–3 MW blocks. Floor PDU, 300–1,000 kVA, with an input breaker, an optional transformer and two branch panels, 3,000–5,000 lb (Buildermuse, 2026). Optional STS, 250–4,000 A (LayerZero, 2025). Busway or cable-and-RPP. The whip or tap-off. The rack PDU, a 0U strip at 208 V or 415/240 V, 30–60 A three-phase common, units to ~130 kW (Raritan PX4; Introl, 2025). The dual-corded load (Schneider Electric, Vertiv, Raritan white papers; Data Center Strategy, 2025). The chapter draws six boxes because the reference hall keeps the STS out of the main path (§2).

Why the transformer was there. A PDU transformer changes voltage and creates a separately derived system, a new neutral and ground-fault reference. The North American legacy stepped 480 V down to 208/120 V for servers that took 120 V (US Patent 6,967,283).

When it leaves, and the bill. At 415/240 V the UPS feeds the busway directly and the server takes 240 V with "no intermediate transformation" (Server Technology, 415 VAC Power Distribution, 2014). Where the UPS output is 480Y/277 V, as Topic 5 fixed it, a 480/277 → 415/240 V autotransformer makes the small step, "90% smaller and less expensive than a 120/208 V PDU isolation transformer" (Silverback DC Solutions); it creates no separately derived system, which is acceptable because the UPS output is already a referenced source. Removing the isolation transformer removes its impedance from the fault path: one vendor's figures show available fault current at the PDU rising from ~17,576 A with a 480 → 208 V transformer to ~56,144 A at 415 V without it (Vertiv/Liebert, Proper Application of 415V Systems, R1010) (verify: one vendor's figures for one example system). Short-circuit, coordination and arc-flash studies become day-one deliverables (§10); §12 reconciles this with Topic 5's ~50 kA.

The static transfer switch

An STS transfers a load between two sources using thyristors in about 4 ms, sub-cycle, with no moving parts (Schneider Electric WP62; LayerZero). It keeps single-corded gear alive in a dual-path world; an ATS does the same job electromechanically, slower and simpler.

Failure modes. Monitoring phase angle, SCR state, breaker state and voltage on both sides makes an STS far more complex than an ATS, with more ways to fail (Schneider WP62). The systemic risk is placement: "big centralized STS units are a bad idea because they create single points of failure that can take down multiple racks" (TRG Datacenters, 2025). Best practice pushes transfer switches to the rack, where a failure takes one device.

Shared-bus propagation. A load fault on a shared bus can propagate to every load on that bus, single- and dual-corded, if the bus collapses before the branch breaker clears (Vertiv/Emerson technical note TN-00011). Dual-cording protects against loss of a source, not against a fault that drags the bus down; selective coordination is the defence (§10). The reference hall has no central STS in the main paths, rack ATS or STS only where single-corded gear demands it, and 6–10 STS at 800–1,600 A in Phase 1 for single-corded and reserve duty (§12).

Busway against cable-and-RPP

A remote power panel is a breaker panel near the racks fed by cable home-runs from the PDU; every circuit is a fixed run, cheaper on day one and best when load is stable; growth means a new home-run, a live-hall conduit pull and a hot-work permit (aidatacenterguide.com, 2025). Busway is prefabricated overhead conductor track into which tap-off boxes twist-lock without de-energizing the bus: higher capex, far lower install labour (about 4–5 prefabricated pieces per 40 ft), reconfigurable under load, overhead so it frees the floor, the hyperscale and AI default (Semianalysis; DCD, 2025). Two rows were left off the chapter's compare for width: best fit (busway for dense, changing layouts; cable for small, stable rooms) and the fault note (higher single-path ampacity means larger bus-fault energy) (Starline/Legrand; Vertiv).

Ratings, cost model, supply. Starline Track Busway spans 40–1,250 A at IP2X–IP54, feeders to 6,300 A (Starline/Legrand). In Starline's model of a 28-rack row with 400 A A/B redundancy, a new bus plug box costs about $350 and the owner installs it, against a contractor mobilisation per new whip (Starline, Cost Analysis, 2019) (verify: a 2019 model still cited in 2025 materials). Busway is a premium the day it is hung and a saving at the first change; in an AI hall that is the first tenant. Cable keeps Zone 1, 300 racks at 8 kW that will not change (§12). High-ampacity busway has become a long-lead item priced off copper (research, open questions); with 94% of the hall's megawatts on busway, that is a schedule risk beside switchgear and UPS.

Voltage: what 415/240 V buys and what it costs

At fixed power, raising voltage lowers current in proportion and resistive loss falls with the square of current; 208 V to 415 V nearly doubles what a conductor and breaker carry (asbeam.com, 2025). At the server, 240 V input runs the PSU at ~85.5% against 82% at 120 V and ~84% at 208 V (Mirus International, HMRP WP001); the EU's native 400/230 V gives ~89% chain efficiency with off-the-shelf gear (Eaton); and the chain keeps the 1–2% a PDU transformer would lose.

Why not 480 V to the server. 277 V line-to-neutral exceeds the 240 V rating of standard IT power supplies, so 480/277 V needs custom servers and stays a distribution voltage (CSE). Hence the autotransformer in the reference hall's PDUs.

The 60 A arithmetic, and two numbers that disagree. Usable power per 415 V three-phase feed is √3 × 415 × I × 0.8, the 0.8 being the NEC continuous-load derate; a 60 A feed gives ≈ 34.5 kW. asbeam.com (2025) quotes 17.3 kW at 208 V against 39.9 kW at 415 V, "a 130% increase" (verify: inconsistent figures; 17.3 kW is derated and 39.9 kW is not). The chapter uses the derated pair, 17.3 and 34.5 kW, an honest 2.0×; the EU figure, ~33 kW, is the same formula at 400 V.

How a 120 kW rack is fed. About four 60 A circuits per side at ~34.5 kW each, doubled for A and B, as two rack PDUs per side from A and B busway through 160–250 A tap-offs (Server Technology math; Introl: "at 480 V three-phase, 100 kW requires only 120 A per circuit"). At 208 V the same rack needs twice the circuits, which is why AI racks left it.

A/B sizing, single-corded gear, metering, and who delivers what

The cardinal rule. Each of a dual-corded rack's feeds must carry the full load if the other is offline: "do not split kW 50/50 for sizing unless policy and OEM allow it" (calcpanel.com, 2025). In operation each side runs at or below 50% so the survivor takes 100% without tripping. The operator, not the design, enforces this.

Single-corded gear and metering. Legacy appliances and some network kit get two sources through a rack ATS or STS, the single point of failure accepted for that one device (TRG Datacenters). The AI pods' network racks, about one air-cooled 20–30 kW rack per two GPU racks (Topic 9), are dual-corded from the pod's A/B pair; only a single-corded appliance inside one needs a transfer switch. Branch-circuit and per-outlet metering are names here; per-outlet monitoring is how you prove the A/B mapping and catch a rack running on one PSU before a path fails (Obelinf, 2026). Topic 11 has the stack.

Delivery boundaries. PDUs, STS and busway are frequently owner-furnished on design-build jobs; the contractor installs and terminates. In wholesale colocation the demarcation is often "the output breaker of the PDU," the tenant supplying racks and contracting for whips and cable plant, though many providers now deliver rack-ready suites (Sentinel Data Centers). In retail the owner owns to the busway or RPP and often the whip; in wholesale the tenant fits out the white space (Obelinf; DataCenterKnowledge, 2025). The reference hall is both: wholesale demarcation at the PDU output breaker or busway tap-off; retail rack-ready with the cost in $/kW. A DC sidecar is tenant or IT-vendor furnished and its busbar is a new demarcation (§9); the study a tenant's gear change invalidates (§10) has no natural owner, so the lease names one.

Redundancy models and who uses them

Definitions. N: exactly enough. N+1: one module beyond need; survives one failure or one unit in maintenance, not both. 2N: two independent systems each rated for 100%, each at or below 50%. 2N+1: 2N with a spare in each system. Distributed redundant, 3M2 or 4M3: three or four independent systems share the load so any one can be lost; in 3M2 each runs at or below 66.7% (CSE/Caterpillar). Block redundant, catcher or reserve bus: N active blocks plus one unloaded reserve reached via STS, so the active UPS run at 75% or more (Socomec; STACK).

Distributed redundancy as a seating plan. Row 1 takes A and B, row 2 B and C, row 3 A and C. When B fails, row 1 goes wholly to A and row 2 wholly to C while row 3 still splits, so A and C each carry one and a half rows; a row can therefore be at most two thirds of a system, which is where 66.7% comes from. No STS is in the path.

Who uses what. Enterprise N+1; banks 2N or 2N+1; colocation and cloud a mix of 2N and 3M2/4M3 (CSE, Data center co-location electrical system design); CoreSite's VA3 runs 2N plus four-to-make-three with a catcher UPS (CoreSite).

Failure math, Tiers, and the AI relaxation

The method. A = MTBF / (MTBF + MTTR). Series elements multiply availabilities; parallel elements multiply unavailabilities (IEEE 493, the Gold Book, now Inactive-Reserved with guidance in the IEEE 3006 series). One path at 99.9% is down about 8.76 h a year; two in parallel give 0.001 × 0.001 = 0.000001, about 31.5 s a year (rule sourced; arithmetic illustrative). Pair MTBF ≈ MTBF² / (2 × MTTR): two units at 3,000 h MTBF and 1 h MTTR give ~4.5 million hours; at 24 h MTTR, ~187,500 h (InventiveHQ). Repair time is a divisor: spares on site and a vendor response time are redundancy by other means.

The caveat. Uptime Institute "removed all references to 'expected downtime per year'… in 2009" and "does not assign availability predictions to Tier levels" (Uptime Institute Journal). The 99.982% in every Tier III sales deck is a number Uptime disowns.

Tiers and the other standards. Tier III is concurrently maintainable: redundant components and multiple distribution paths, one active at a time. Tier IV is fault tolerant: multiple active, physically isolated paths, surviving any single event including operator error (Uptime Institute). TIA-942 uses Rated-1 to Rated-4, Rated-3 being concurrently maintainable; BICSI 002 is a companion "intended to complement and exceed the minimum requirements in TIA-942" (TechTarget; TIA). 2N, 2N+1, 3M2, 4M3 and block redundant can all be built to Tier III; N and N+1 cannot.

The AI relaxation, dated. "Many operators now run N or N+1 on the compute blocks and reserve 2N for the network, storage, and control plane," because training tolerates checkpoint-restart (ModulEdge citing Uptime Institute, 2025). AWS reports a best-case GPU-node MTTR of ~12 minutes using fail-in-place N+1 (AWS, 2025). NVIDIA's Kyber and the OCP sidecar contemplate single-corded GPU racks (verify: secondary citations). The reference hall does not relax: a colocation facility does not control its tenants' workloads, so its AI pods are dual-corded and 3M2 like the floor.

Changing the model mid-design

The owner sets the model in the OPR; the engineer renders it in the BOD; the colocation provider writes it into the SLA as an availability commitment and a redundancy descriptor (Topic 2 covers OPR and BOD).

The ripple. A change from 2N to 3M2 or back moves the UPS block count (8 against 6 for 10 MW, §12), the PDU count and pairing, the STS population, the upstream switchgear sections, the busway pairing and the electrical-room area, each a long-lead line with a factory slot reserved at the 60% one-line (Topic 3: slots at T+3–6, release to manufacture near T+8). The research quotes MV switchgear at 40–60 weeks (Buildermuse, 2026); Topics 3 and 5 carry 44–84 weeks from wider sources. The course reconciles to Topic 5's range: 40–60 is the optimistic end of a market whose slower quotes pass 80 weeks. "A late redundancy change can move the whole schedule" (Buildermuse, 2026).

The reference hall's discipline. The model is frozen in the OPR before the 60% one-line: 3M2 on 2.5 MW blocks, one optional 2N suite on a carved-out block pair. The one decision left open is DC in the AI pods (§9), because the pathways, bays and busway class are already drawn, so exercising it later changes rack-side hardware, not gear counts.

48 V, ±400 V DC and the copper wall

48 V and ORv3. The OCP Open Rack v3 power shelf rectifies facility AC to a 48 V busbar shared down the rack, removing per-server PSUs. Google's 12 V to 48 V move, begun in 2010 and contributed to OCP in 2016–2017, "resulted in a 30 percent improvement in energy efficiency," per Urs Hölzle (DataCenterKnowledge; Google Cloud Blog). ORv3 shelves reach 33 kW at near 98% (Advanced Energy).

The wall. Past ~100 kW the current at 48 V becomes brutal: at ~2.5 kA a 0.1 mΩ rise in one connector dissipates ~625 W. Per NVIDIA, a one-megawatt rack at 54 V needs more than 18,000 A, busbars over 200 kg and up to 64 U of power shelves (The Register; NVIDIA Technical Blog / Converge Digest, 2025).

±400 V / 800 V DC. OCP Mt Diablo (Diablo 400), co-authored by Microsoft, Meta and Google, defines a disaggregated sidecar power rack at ±400 VDC, 800 V rail to rail, for racks of 100 kW to 1 MW; 400 V was chosen to piggyback the EV supply chain (OCP, 2025; v0.7.0 spec). NVIDIA's 800 VDC architecture targets 1 MW racks "starting in 2027" with Kyber (NVL576, ~600 kW) and claims "up to 5% improvement in end-to-end power efficiency" and "reducing copper requirements by 45% compared to 415 VAC distribution," with production to "coincide with NVIDIA Kyber rack-scale systems in 2027" (NVIDIA Technical Blog, 20 May 2025, updated 31 July 2025; ModulEdge, 2026). Its conductor figures conflict and are quoted verbatim: "800 VDC enables 85% more power to be transmitted through the same conductor size" (NVIDIA Technical Blog) and "the same wire gauge can carry 157% more power than 415 VAC" (NVIDIA, "Building the 800 VDC Ecosystem") (verify: nearly a factor of two apart; neither states its basis).

Status, codes, pre-provisioning. Vertiv ships 800 VDC in H2 2026; Eaton released a reference architecture in October 2025; first small-volume shipments by Q3 2026 (szsanyi.com; BigGo, 2026) (verify: trade-press dates). AMD has deliberately not committed to a proprietary power path (Drybulb, 2026). NEC, UL and IEC treatment lags the hardware; a DC arc has no zero crossing and does not self-extinguish (szsanyi.com). Pre-provision floor space, structure and pathway headroom for sidecar racks; the sidecar is IT-vendor or hyperscaler furnished with the interface at the busbar, a new demarcation (research, §3.3–3.4). The stranded-asset risk is real: "the ratings you pour into the slab cap the rack power you can ever feed" (aidatacenterguide.com). The reference hall names it and does not hedge (§12).

Grounding, harmonics, arc-flash and coordination

Grounding. Data center grounding exceeds code minimum, which targets life safety (eypmcfinc.com). The structure is a common bonding network with a signal reference grid: copper on 0.6–3 m (2–10 ft) centres, ≥ #6 AWG, per IEEE 1100 (Emerald Book) and TIA-942 Appendix G. TIA-607-C defines the busbar hierarchy, 1/0 AWG to the busbar and ≥ 6 AWG to each cabinet, never serially bonded (ecmag.com; pducables.com). The SRG's noise case is weaker for active-PFC gear but it remains standard practice and often a warranty condition (Schneider SRG white paper). Isolated grounds are deprecated for the equipotential mesh; TN-S is the preferred earthing arrangement.

Harmonics. Modern server PSUs use active power factor correction, drawing near-sinusoidal current under 5% THD, "the single most impactful harmonic mitigation development of the past decade" (EmergEdge; 3dfs WP55). K-13 suits mixed loads and K-20 is "typically overkill" (green-data blog), but thousands of identical, synchronised supplies in an AI hall can push toward K-20 (QT&E, 2025). The reference hall specifies K-13 for the PDU autotransformers and checks K-20 in the AI pods.

Arc-flash. Data centers work energised, so an energised electrical work permit under NFPA 70E 130.2(B) is "frequently required, not the exception" (nfpa70e.net). 130.5(H) requires labels on essentially all distribution gear; the arc-flash boundary is where incident energy equals 1.2 cal/cm²; PPE Categories 1–4 map to incident energy; energised work above 40 cal/cm² is not permitted (US Made Supply; payapress). Engineering controls sit above PPE: remote racking, maintenance switches, arc-resistant switchgear and IR windows can move a worker from Category 4 to outside the boundary (US Made Supply; Bowtie).

Selective coordination and studies. Downstream of the UPS a rack fault must trip only its branch device, never the PDU or the UPS; time-current curves must nest. At the rack PDU the fuse-versus-breaker question turns on interrupting capacity: a Bussmann CUBEFuse carries 300 kA at 600 V, comfortable against transformerless 415 V fault current (asbeam.com). Because the transformer's removal raises that current (§1), coordination and SCCR must be re-verified. The incident-energy and grounding studies are stamped PE deliverables (Bowtie Engineering), reviewed at least every five years or after any upstream change (payapress). A tenant's gear change invalidates the study and the labels; the reference hall's lease names the owner, funded by a fit-out fee, with tenant gear changes reviewed before energisation (§5).

EPO: the code, the opt-out and the accident record

The trade. NEC Article 645 governs IT equipment rooms; 645.10 requires a disconnecting means that drops IT power and dedicated HVAC and closes fire and smoke dampers (NEC 2020). In exchange, 645 relaxes wiring, such as loose whips under a raised floor (CSE; thebuildingcodeforum).

The opt-out. Article 645 is optional. Built to ordinary Chapter 3 wiring methods, with Article 722 for low-voltage cabling, 645 and its EPO do not apply; the path has existed since at least the 2005 edition. The 2011 and later editions added zoned EPO and the "critical operations data systems" exception (645.10(B)), allowing omission with an approved shutdown procedure, qualified staff, fire protection and AHJ approval. In the 2026 draft 645.4 disappears and its six conditions move to 645.5 with references to Article 722 (thebuildingcodeforum, 2025). NFPA 75 8.4.2.1 requires power to be disconnected on activation of a gaseous total-flooding system unless a Chapter 4 risk analysis warrants continuous power, which ties the EPO to Topic 10's suppression choice. NFPA 76 has no EPO requirement.

The accident record. Owners opt out because "the benefits to be reaped are not worth the prerequisites" (mikeholt practitioners). NYC Transit Rail Control Center, 29 August 2021: the UPS rode through a ConEd dip ("both systems worked exactly as intended"); the outage's "most likely cause… was the 'Emergency Power Off' button being manually activated," a button that "did not have a protective cover at the time" (WSP USA report, 8 September 2021, via governor.ny.gov). Georgia Tech CCB247: ~96 minutes after "the EPO system was triggered accidentally" during power work (Georgia Tech TSO notice). Ponemon Institute, National Survey on Data Center Outages (Emerson Network Power, 2010): "accidental EPO/human error (51 percent)" among root causes; one UPS vendor logged 20 EPO incidents, 13% of all UPS failures, in 2002–03; another found 26% of human-error failures EPO-caused, often by vendors, delivery staff or cleaners (pducables "Ken's Korner"; DataCenterKnowledge, 2007) (verify: dated surveys). Uptime calls accidental EPO "a recurring cause of downtime" and does not recommend installation unless local code compels it.

Practice, and the trap. Hyperscalers and large colos build to Chapter 3 and omit the EPO, or zone and cover it. Uptime's Annual Outage Analysis 2025: "nearly 40% of organizations have suffered a major outage caused by human error over the past three years," 85% of them procedural (Uptime Institute press release, 6 May 2025). The AHJ and fire marshal decide before schematic design, because the choice dictates wiring methods. The trap is an EPO designed in and later disabled by operations: a non-compliant risk nobody owns. The reference hall never installs the button; Topic 10 chose no gas in the white space, so NFPA 75 8.4.2.1 does not reopen it. A 2N-suite tenant who insists gets one zoned to that suite, covered, as that tenant's risk.

Reference hall: distribution by zone, worked

The block count. Topic 5 sized five 2.5 MW blocks N+1 for 10 MW; the Tier III candidates were block redundant (five, but an STS in every path, §2), 2N (eight) and 3M2 (six). One 3M2 group is three blocks, 7.5 MW installed, each at most 66.7% loaded: usable = 3 × 2.5 × 0.667 = 5 MW, and on loss of one block the other two carry 2.5 MW each. Phase 1's 10 MW needs two groups: 6 blocks, 15 MW installed, 10 MW usable, 67%, 5 MW idle. Under 2N, four pairs: 8 blocks, 20 MW, 50%, 10 MW idle. Block redundant or N+1 on five blocks: 12.5 MW, 80%, but N+1 is not concurrently maintainable and the catcher puts an STS before every PDU. 4M3 would use 75%; 2N+1, five blocks for two blocks of load, 40%. Four halls of six give 24 blocks, 60 MW installed for 40 usable.

PDUs and fault current. About 20 PDUs at 750 kVA–1 MVA, each with a 480 → 415/240 V autotransformer (§1), paired so no row's two feeds share a block: 15–20 MVA for 10 MW, the 3M2 ratio again. Topic 5 quotes ~50 kA at the unit-substation secondary; the research's vendor example gives ~56 kA at a transformerless PDU. Both are indicative and from different systems; the autotransformer adds little impedance, so the hall assumes 50–56 kA at the PDU and lets the stamped study set final numbers before busway and rack-PDU orders release.

Gear and availability. The chapter's compare carries the Phase 1 gear count; the optional 2N suite is one carved-out block pair, 5 MW installed for 2.5 MW usable, with duplicated PDUs, STS and busway. With §7's figures, one path at 99.9% is down ~8.76 h a year; a rack on two paths from different blocks, 0.001 × 0.001, ~31.5 s; and pair MTBF ≈ MTBF² / (2 × MTTR) makes 1 h against 24 h MTTR a factor of 24. None of it is certified.

Growth and hand-offs. Phases 2–4 repeat the 10 MW pattern; later halls skew to 120 kW DC-ready pods, with 15–20% of space held for a 40 kW pod becoming 120. The DC decision is re-taken at each hall's 60% one-line; Hall 1's gate near T+8 falls before ±400 V hardware ships (Q3 2026 to 2027), so Hall 1 gets AC busway and sidecar bays, knowingly.
