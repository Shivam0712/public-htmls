Demarcation. The interconnection agreement must fix the exact ownership and operating boundary, meter location and ownership, high-side-device ownership, construction and maintenance duties, switching authority, and each party’s energization scope. A boundary may be near the incoming structure or high-side breaker, but do not assume the line-side terminals, revenue-metering CTs and PTs, or first protective device always have the same owner. Typically the utility owns the incoming line and controls energization of its side, while the customer owns downstream transformers and MV switchgear. Under design-build with owner-furnished equipment, the design-builder engineers and builds assigned customer-side work; utility-side work may be utility-built or customer-built and deeded back under the agreement. Substation delivery, including utility scope, can run 2–4 years; utility studies and transformer procurement are separate schedule risks (§6).

Verdict on the explorer's second rule. Its under-15 MW distribution, 15–80 MW at 69–138 kV, and over-80 MW at 230 kV and up bands suggest a pattern, not utility thresholds. The overlapping ranges better show that some 60–80 MW sites may use 230 kV, while this 56 MW course campus plans for 138 kV. The utility study and agreement govern the actual connection.

Dual feeds as a spectrum of independence

“Dual feed” describes three possible levels of independence, not a guarantee about two named circuits. In ascending potential outage benefit and cost: two feeders from one substation bus may survive a feeder fault but share that bus; feeders from different substations may survive loss of one substation if their upstream paths do not share the failed element; and feeds from different transmission paths or injection points may survive some specified network contingencies (research synthesis). None of these pairs eliminates wider grid events. Trace both feeds through their buses, substations and transmission paths, and study shared exposures, before calling them physically independent.

Potential outage benefit is not Tier credit. The Tier Standard: Topology states that “the only reliable source of power for a data center is the engine-generator plant” and that “the number of utility feeds, substations, and power grids… neither predicts nor influences” the Tier (Uptime Institute, Tier Standard: Topology). More independent utility supply may reduce outage frequency, engine runtime, fuel use, wear and permitted operating hours, but earns no Tier credit. The reference campus plans two 138 kV lines for their possible operating benefit. The utility facilities study must establish their routes and shared elements; the course does not assume separate transmission paths.

Substation layout, protection and campus voltage

Bus arrangements generally rise in resilience and cost from radial to main-tie-main (double-ended), ring bus and breaker-and-a-half. Main-tie-main is the course's maintainability starting point, not proof that every load has an alternate path. To maintain an isolated transformer or incomer, open and isolate its input. A tie rated for the combined transferred load, supplied by a surviving transformer with adequate site-rated capacity, can then keep both bus sections live. Specify tie continuous and fault-duty ratings, and verify protective coordination in that switching state. For bus-section maintenance, de-energize and isolate the section being worked on. Its downstream loads need an additional routed feed or suitable transfer arrangement; closing the tie alone leaves that section energized. Check source and feeder capacity, load routes, interrupting duty, relay settings and permitted switching sequences for normal, one-input-out and bus-section-out states. The pattern recurs at unit substations and downstream distribution in Topic 6.

Protection. Selective coordination aims to clear a fault at the nearest upstream protective device and limit the affected zone. The study covers relay curves, interrupting ratings and arc-flash. Vacuum breakers dominate at MV, with microprocessor relays implementing IEEE device functions including 50/51 overcurrent, 87 differential and 27/59 under- and over-voltage, alongside bus differential and breaker-failure protection. Study ownership is in §7. Check coordination again for alternate tie and transfer states, not just the normal one-line.

Transformer populations, fluids and impedance

MW and MVA before unit counts. MW measures real power; MVA measures apparent power. Power factor is MW divided by MVA, so a 2.5 MVA transformer supplies at most 2.25 MW at 0.9 power factor, before losses and derating. A 2.5 MVA hall unit cannot be assumed to feed a 2.5 MW UPS at full output, after UPS input losses, while also serving mechanical load. At illustrative 0.96 UPS efficiency and 0.9 input power factor, full UPS output alone requires 2.5 ÷ (0.96 × 0.9) ≈ 2.89 MVA, before recharge or other loads. Confirm the actual UPS input specification, site ratings and feeder allocations.

Two populations. Large outdoor, oil-filled power transformers step 138 kV down to 34.5 kV and are typically 30–100 MVA. The course starts with two 30 MVA units for Phase 1 and reserves positions for further units. At completed-site demand, three 30 MVA units leave 60 MVA with one out: approximately 57 MW at 0.95 power factor before losses and margin, or only 54 MW at 0.9 (§6). Four 30 MVA units or appropriately larger units are expansion options subject to study. Indoor MV/LV unit-substation transformers step 34.5 kV down to 480Y/277 V for UPS blocks and other plant. The rewritten chapter proposes eight 2.5 MVA dry-type hall units. With one unavailable, their aggregate nameplate is 17.5 MVA, but aggregate capacity does not establish that each load has a suitable alternate feeder or that full-output UPS and mechanical loads fit. Distribution-transformer efficiency requirements may apply to selected units (§5).

Oil, dry and ester. Oil-filled equipment wins on efficiency, cooling and power density and is standard outdoors, but indoor installations raise NEC Article 450 vault, containment and fire-separation questions. Dry-type is the standard indoor choice for UPS-feeding unit substations: no flammable-liquid sump, with visual and thermal inspections rather than the oil-sampling regime. Natural and synthetic esters, including Cargill FR3 and Eaton Envirotemp, offer a less-flammable liquid option (research synthesis).

Impedance. The research cites IEEE C57.12.00 and Federal Pacific's catalogue for a nominal 5.75% impedance at both 2,500 and 3,000 kVA. Available transformer-limited fault kVA is approximately transformer kVA divided by per-unit impedance. For a 2.5 MVA, 480 V candidate unit, full-load current is 2,500,000 ÷ (1.732 × 480) ≈ 3,007 A. A simplified bolted secondary fault is about 50.1 kA at 6% impedance or 52.3 kA at 5.75%. Applying the research's −7.5% manufacturing tolerance to 5.75% gives about 56.6 kA, before other contributions and circuit impedance are assessed. If a 3 MVA, 480 V alternative is selected, full-load current is approximately 3,608 A and the corresponding simplified faults are 60.1 kA at 6%, 62.7 kA at 5.75%, and approximately 67.8 kA after that tolerance. Do not carry a 65 kA breaker selection from one unit size to the other. Specify interrupting and withstand ratings only after studying selected impedance, source and motor contributions, and switching configurations. Lower impedance raises fault current but improves voltage regulation. Topic 6 repeats downstream fault-duty checks, including where an isolation transformer is removed. Queue mitigations remain standard designs, possible dual-rated 34.5/13.8 kV units and reservation slots before design freeze (§6).

Losses, efficiency and the DOE rule

Two kinds of loss. No-load core loss persists whenever a transformer is energized, potentially 8,760 hours a year. Load copper loss scales approximately with current squared: at half rated current it is a quarter of full-load copper loss. Energy estimates therefore need the energized equipment count and actual load profile, not just hall nameplate.

Check the live rule at order (verify). The research identifies 10 CFR 431, the April 2013 final rule with 2016 compliance levels, the 22 April 2024 final rule with a 23 April 2029 compliance date, and DOE's action at 90 FR 43371 (9 September 2025) under the Congressional Review Act, Pub. L. 119-8. It describes the last action as removing certain December 2024 Part 431 amendments. That does not establish that the April 2024 distribution-transformer rule was wholly reversed. Recheck the live 10 CFR 431 product definitions, tables and compliance dates and the precise amendments removed against official text. Put the applicable product class and required efficiency level in the purchase specification rather than relying only on “DOE compliant.”

Efficiency levels. The research reports medium-voltage dry-type efficiency tested at 50% load, with 2016 levels of about 98.9% for a 500 kVA high-BIL unit and 99.3–99.5% for 1,500–2,500 kVA units. It reports 99.49% for a 2,500 kVA liquid-immersed unit (10 CFR 431 tables, as summarised in the research). These figures do not establish the applicable table entry or losses for every selected hall unit; obtain its certified values. The research supplies no rated loss figure for the campus power transformers.

What losses cost, and what is not yet known. The research's illustrative 2,500 kVA dry-type unit has 4 kW no-load and 27 kW full-load copper loss. At a constant 50% rated current and $0.10/kWh, its annual cost is (4 × 8,760 + 27 × 0.25 × 8,760) × $0.10 ≈ $9,400. At the chapter's illustrative $0.07/kWh, the same assumed loading costs about $6,600. A 100 W continuous no-load reduction saves about $88 a year at $0.10/kWh. Neither single-unit example establishes a campus total. Model the selected hall units, energized counts and loading; large-transformer core and copper losses; UPS losses; and switchgear and cable losses separately, using operating profiles and the applicable tariff.

Only approximately 10 MW of Hall 1's 14 MW is designated UPS-protected computing load; the other approximately 4 MW includes cooling, electrical losses and other plant. Do not apply a 94% UPS-efficiency assumption to all 14 MW or add losses already included in that total a second time. For illustration only, 10 MW of UPS output at 94% efficiency requires about 10.64 MW input, a roughly 0.64 MW UPS loss at that operating point; at 97%, the corresponding loss is about 0.31 MW. Neither value is an annual estimate without measured loading, operating hours and the selected UPS efficiency curve (§9).

Medium-voltage switchgear

AIS versus GIS. Air-insulated switchgear has a larger footprint, lower cost and easier access for maintenance; it is the usual indoor MV lineup. Gas-insulated switchgear is compact and sealed, useful where space is tight, the environment harsh or voltage higher, and historically used SF6 insulation (research synthesis).

The SF6 problem. SF6 has a roughly 23,500 100-year global-warming potential on the cited IPCC AR5-era figure (arXiv 2308.13667); AR6 values appear in the GHG Protocol's August 2024 GWP table. The research describes Regulation (EU) 2024/573 as restricting new equipment up to 24 kV from 1 January 2026, with a later restriction up to 52 kV by 2030; it also reports California and New York restrictions beginning in 2025 and tightening EPA reporting. The European 2026 up-to-24 kV restriction does not directly cover this 34.5 kV US campus. Check the applicable voltage class, jurisdiction, effective date and exemptions at specification time.

SF6-free technologies include vacuum interruption with dry or clean air, including Siemens “blue” GIS and ABB offerings, and fluoronitrile mixtures, C4F7N or Novec 4710, marketed as g3 by GE Vernova and Hitachi Energy. Some product lines first launched at IEC 50 Hz rather than locally rated 60 Hz (EPA assessment cited in the research). Confirm the offered equipment's 34.5 kV service and applicable 38 kV class, US 60 Hz availability and rating, interrupting and arc ratings, approvals and delivery. The reported 12–18% premium (Dataintelo) is (verify: single market-research source). The course proposes SF6-free gear, not a claim that every marketed alternative fits.

Three separate design checks. Arc resistance: IEEE C37.20.7 tests enclosures for internal arcing and defined personnel-accessibility locations: Type 1, front; Type 2, front, sides and rear; Type 2B, Type 2 with control-compartment doors open; and Type 2C, between compartments. The course proposes Type 2B for personnel exposure during specified energized operations. It does not make energized work generally safe. Selective fault clearing: protective-device settings must clear a fault in the smallest feasible zone while keeping unaffected circuits supplied; an arc-rated enclosure does not replace coordination. Maintainable bus paths: isolate the equipment to be worked on and prove an adequately rated route for its loads. Neither arc resistance nor selective clearing creates a replacement route for an isolated bus section (§3).

Lead time and the coordination study. Data-center-spec MV switchgear is reported at 52–84+ weeks versus a roughly 44-week all-class average; the relevant 38 kV class can take 96–120 weeks. The selective coordination study is an explicit design-build deliverable, usually assigned to the engineer of record or specialist. It needs purchased-device ratings, source and fault data, and every permitted tie and alternate-source state. Early factory-slot reservations, the Topic 3 60% one-line rating freeze (T+3–8) and final protection settings therefore form a dependency loop. Topic 6 continues the study to PDUs and arc-flash labels.

UPS

Classes and topologies. IEC 62040-3 distinguishes VFI, voltage and frequency independent double-conversion; VI, voltage independent line-interactive; and VFD, voltage and frequency dependent offline operation. The research reports static double-conversion efficiency of 94–97%, with newer designs reported at 96.8–99.3%, plus a moderate footprint and typical 5–10 minute battery ride-through. Line-interactive is reported at 97.5–98.5%, with smaller equipment and roughly 35% lower cost per kW for smaller applications. Rotary DRUPS from Piller, Hitec and Euro-Diesel combines flywheel and engine, with reported efficiency up to about 97%, 13–40 seconds of ride-through, units to approximately 2,500 kVA paralleled to 30 MVA or more, and a claimed 12–18% lower 15-year total cost; its rotating plant and different maintenance burden matter (research §5.1).

Distinct operating paths. In normal VFI double-conversion, the rectifier feeds a direct-current link and the continuously operating inverter supplies the alternating-current output. Battery takeover supports that link when input fails; it is not a required rack transfer. A static bypass instead routes around the inverter in specified fault or overload conditions and cannot be assumed to retain inverter conditioning. A bypass-based high-efficiency mode may deliberately use a less-conditioned path in normal service, raising reported efficiency from 94–97% to 98–99%, but changing how upstream disturbances reach the load. Schneider's Neil Rasmussen estimates about a 2.3% saving, approximately $15,000 annually for a 1 MW site at half load and $0.10/kWh. Assess surge protection and the selected product's disturbance response. Some operators decline that mode, while Schneider's eConversion is a Galaxy V default. Silicon-carbide VFI designs report efficiency approaching 99% without conventional eco-mode. The course chooses normal VFI operation with eco-mode off; it does not promise an always-conditioned output under bypass. Actual losses depend on selected equipment and load (§5).

Modules, blocks and paths. Hot-swappable modules of 15–500 kW can provide module-level N+1 within a frame (Schneider Galaxy VXL 500–1,250 kW; Vertiv; Eaton). That does not protect against losing an entire 2.5 MW block or its feeder. Four 2.5 MW blocks exactly supply 10 MW. Five blocks are bare N+1 at full rating: after one loss, each of four survivors must carry 2.5 MW. The course installs six and plans for five survivors to carry 2 MW each, leaving 0.5 MW of nameplate per surviving block. That headroom is not proven transient capacity. Require an actual load-redistribution, feeder and alternate-route check for the one-block-out state. Topic 6 must prove the proposed distributed arrangement and independently routed downstream A/B feeds. An optional 2N tenant suite needs two separately routed paths, each capable of the suite's full load, from appropriate sources through UPS and downstream distribution. Extra frames alone do not make 2N.

A 2.5 MW UPS output is not necessarily compatible with a 2.5 MVA input transformer. At illustrative 96% efficiency and 0.9 input power factor, full output requires approximately 2.89 MVA input before battery recharge. Verify actual input power factor, loss curve, recharge limits, transformer site rating and feeder assignment against the proposed hall units (§4). The 2–3 MW UPS block is an economic and failure-domain planning range, not a promise that one UPS, one generator and one transformer have matching usable ratings.

Output. The course output is 480Y/277 V under specified UPS operating conditions. Topic 6 uses a 480-to-415/240 V autotransformer at each PDU; choosing a 415 V UPS output would alter that downstream design.

Delivery. Reported 2026 lead times are 26–52 weeks for 500 kVA–1.5 MW frames, with 26–40-week IGBT and SiC component queues beneath them. Name the factory witness test as a hand-off and confirm the separate battery-cabinet delivery (§11).

The AI load at the UPS

GPU clusters can ramp quickly when jobs start, checkpoint or stop. Synchronized computation can produce repeated load swings, but their magnitude and frequency depend on workload, rack controls, onboard capacitance and how pods operate together. The research offers tens of MW in seconds, brief peaks of 150% of steady-state, and buffering aimed at swings up to ~30 Hz as possible workload-specific scenarios, not a measured profile for this campus or universal UPS requirements (research §5.5). A sufficiently large ramp could exceed inverter or battery limits and, on generator power, challenge governor and voltage-regulator response.

Before assigning UPS transient headroom or prescribing a buffer, request measured or vendor-guaranteed rack, pod and hall ramp magnitudes, durations, repetition rates, power-factor behaviour and power-cap settings. Conduct a transient study of UPS inverter and direct-current-link limits, battery or supercapacitor response, generator step-load and recharge response, and protection settings together, in grid- and engine-supplied states. Candidate mitigations include more suitable UPS capacity, supercapacitors or additional battery, a BESS, staged workload ramps and NVIDIA-SMI power limits; Tesla and Fluence target buffering applications, while EPRI DCFlex addresses grid-side coordination (research §10). Select against the delivered workload profile rather than a headline peak percentage.

Hand-off to Topic 8. Topic 8's GB300 research describes a rack-side 0.2–3 Hz swing scenario and 65 J per GPU of onboard capacitance trimming peaks seen by the facility by about 30%. Those workload-specific figures do not establish what every rack sends to the UPS or prove a 30 Hz buffer is needed here. The course has 200 planned 120 kW AI racks, about 61% of the stated 38.9 MW rack mix. Keep a possible buffering position on the one-line while the rack vendor and operator establish what reaches each power block; rack-level design belongs to Topic 8.

With fast-start standby generation, short-duration batteries bridge transfer and allow for retries, rather than running the hall for hours. For this course, 10 MW delivered at the UPS output for five minutes equals 10 × 5 ÷ 60 ≈ 0.83 MWh delivered. Installed battery energy must be greater to cover conversion losses, discharge limits, ageing, temperature and reserve. The other approximately 4 MW of hall demand is not automatically battery-backed.

Market share, with unlike measures. VRLA is reported as roughly 61.76% of installed capacity or market in one 2025 estimate (Mordor Intelligence, 2025), while “lithium-ion now captures approximately 40% of data center backup UPS installations across all facility types, rising to 55% at hyperscale” (Vertiv, December 2025, via Introl and Axis Intelligence). Installed base and new installations can differ. Reported lithium economics include about a 35% initial premium, 39% lower ten-year total cost and 60% smaller footprint (verify: vendor-derived).

Three distinct approval questions. UL 9540A is a fire-propagation test method, producing data for review; it is not a system listing or an installation approval. UL 9540 is a system listing, whose applicability to the selected UPS battery must be checked. NFPA 855 is an installation standard where the locally adopted rules make its relevant provisions applicable. Ask the AHJ which edition, UPS-specific scope and exceptions, listings, test reports, unit limits, spacing, ventilation, deflagration control, sprinkler provisions under NFPA 13 and separations govern this installation (verify with the AHJ and adopted official text). The research identifies NFPA 855 editions 2020, 2023 and 2026, with IFC 2021 pointing to 855-2020 and IFC 2024 to 855-2023. Its general statement about a 20 kWh lithium threshold does not establish one approval route for every lithium UPS battery. UL 9540A results may support alternative spacing or unit arrangements where adopted provisions permit them. Do not impose a separate battery room or one explosion-control layout as a universal requirement.

The course chooses dedicated, ventilated, fire-rated LFP battery rooms; approval of that project-specific layout remains a gate. Clean-agent suppression alone does not stop self-sustaining thermal runaway; the complete fire strategy belongs to Topic 10. Cabinet purchasing and installation may be owner- or UPS-vendor-furnished, but warranty and end-of-life recycling, especially for VRLA replacements, must be assigned. UL 9540A historically lacked fixed pass/fail targets, leaving AHJ interpretation and potentially “a protracted period” of case-by-case review (Data Center Frontier). Allow review time rather than treating the chosen layout as approved.

BESS beyond the UPS (research §6.4). A separate BESS can serve peak shaving, flexible interconnection, grid services or load smoothing, but does not replace specified UPS ride-through. A reported 5 MW/10 MWh commercial system saved over $500,000 annually on demand charges after a 35% reduction (SunLith, 2025); a flat, 24/7 data-center load may have less shifting opportunity. A Duke University study (February 2025) estimated approximately 100 GW of new load could connect with minimal grid impact if flexible, motivating further interconnection analysis, not guaranteeing an earlier date for this campus. The research reports a roughly five-year median interconnection wait and some PJM cases above eight years. A grid-services BESS might connect at MV, while transient smoothing may be nearer UPS or LV equipment (Fluence; Tesla Megapack). The course reserves a 34.5 kV bus position and does not buy a BESS in Phase 1.

NFPA 110 Type 10 is a separate test. It concerns backup power at designated transfer-switch load terminals within ten seconds. Engine rated speed near ten seconds does not prove it, and the illustration's approximately 15-second hall transfer does not establish Type 10 compliance. Identify designated Type 10 loads and verify their actual transfer arrangements and witnessed timing. Do not call a transfer from a dead utility source closed-transition. Controlled retransfer after utility recovery is a different switching operation. The course's five-minute battery specification provides a planned window for the transition and retries at stated load and battery condition, not immunity to repeated start failure (§11). Topic 12 develops operating procedures.

What “clean” means at the UPS output. In normal VFI operation, the specified 480Y/277 V output is supplied by the inverter rather than raw mains. Indicative specified operating-performance targets from the research are approximately ±1% steady-state voltage, regulated 60 Hz and typically under 2–3% voltage THD, subject to the selected UPS specification and transient conditions. Battery support can preserve inverter output through the illustrated upstream loss and generator transfer. Those figures are not guarantees while an unconditioned bypass carries load, after battery exhaustion or during an unisolated downstream fault. Topic 6 therefore inherits a specified protected output under defined operating and fault conditions, not proof that the bus is always energized or that downstream A/B independence and selectivity have already been established.
