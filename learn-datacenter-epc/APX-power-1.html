Demarcation. The interconnection agreement names the ownership and operating boundary, often near the incoming structure or high-side breaker. Do not assume that the line-side terminals, revenue-metering CTs and PTs, or first high-side protective device always have the same owner. The agreement must assign metering, high-side-device ownership, construction, maintenance and switching authority. Typically the utility owns the incoming line and controls energization of its side, while the customer owns downstream transformers and MV switchgear. Under design-build with owner-furnished equipment, the design-builder engineers and builds the assigned customer-side work; utility-side work may be utility-built or customer-built and deeded back under the agreement. Substation delivery, including utility scope, can run 2–4 years; the utility studies and transformer procurement are separate schedule risks (§6).

Verdict on the explorer's second rule. Its under-15 MW distribution, 15–80 MW at 69–138 kV, and over-80 MW at 230 kV and up bands suggest a pattern, not utility thresholds. The overlapping ranges above better show that some 60–80 MW sites may use 230 kV, while this 56 MW course campus chooses 138 kV. The interconnection study governs. The course decision is recorded in the plan, not made here.

Dual feeds as a spectrum of independence

“Dual feed” describes three possible levels of independence, not a guarantee about two named circuits. In ascending potential availability benefit and cost: two feeders from one substation bus can survive a feeder fault but share that bus; feeders from different substations can survive loss of one substation if their upstream paths do not share the failed element; and feeds from different transmission paths or injection points can survive some wider network contingencies (research synthesis). Even the last arrangement does not eliminate regional events. Trace each feed through its buses, substations and transmission paths before calling it physically independent.

Availability benefit is not Tier credit. The Tier Standard: Topology states that “the only reliable source of power for a data center is the engine-generator plant” and that “the number of utility feeds, substations, and power grids… neither predicts nor influences” the Tier (Uptime Institute, Tier Standard: Topology). A more independent utility supply may reduce outage frequency, engine runtime, fuel use, wear and permitted operating hours, but earns no Tier credit. The reference campus takes two 138 kV lines for their possible operating benefit. The utility facilities study must establish where they run and what they share; the course does not assume two lines provide separate transmission paths.

Substation layout, protection and campus voltage

Bus arrangements generally rise in resilience and cost from radial to main-tie-main (double-ended), ring bus and breaker-and-a-half. Main-tie-main is the course's maintainability starting point, not proof by itself that every load has an alternate path. For transformer-input maintenance, isolate one input and, if permitted, close the tie so the healthy input feeds both bus halves. For bus-section maintenance, de-energize and isolate the section being worked on; its protected loads must receive power by another routed feed. An energized tie does not make work on that bus section safe. Check remaining source and feeder capacity, load routes, interrupting duty, relay settings and the permitted switching sequence for each normal, one-input-out and bus-section-out state. The pattern recurs at unit substations and downstream distribution in Topic 6.

Protection. Selective coordination aims to clear a fault at the nearest upstream protective device and limit the affected zone. The study covers relay curves, interrupting ratings and arc-flash. Vacuum breakers dominate at MV, with microprocessor relays implementing IEEE device functions including 50/51 overcurrent, 87 differential and 27/59 under- and over-voltage, alongside bus differential and breaker-failure protection. Study ownership is in §7. Coordination must be checked again for alternate switching states, not just the normal one-line.

Transformer populations, fluids and impedance

Two populations. Large power transformers step 138 kV down to 34.5 kV. They are typically 30–100 MVA, oil-filled and outdoors. This course starts with two 30 MVA units and plans four eventually: three leave only 60 MVA with one out, below the approximately 62 MVA needed for 56 MW at 0.9 power factor before margin (§6). Indoor MV/LV unit-substation transformers step 34.5 kV down to 480Y/277 V for UPS blocks and other plant. Hall 1 proposes eight 3 MVA dry-type units. Their combined nameplate with one unavailable is 21 MVA, but aggregate capacity does not establish that each load has a suitable alternate feeder. Distribution-transformer efficiency requirements may apply to selected units (§5).

Oil, dry and ester. Oil-filled equipment wins on efficiency, cooling and power density and is standard outdoors, but indoor installations raise NEC Article 450 vault, containment and fire-separation questions. Dry-type is the standard indoor choice for UPS-feeding unit substations: no flammable-liquid sump, with visual and thermal inspections rather than the oil-sampling regime. Natural and synthetic esters, including Cargill FR3 and Eaton Envirotemp, offer a less-flammable liquid option (research synthesis).

Impedance. The research cites IEEE C57.12.00 and Federal Pacific's catalogue for a nominal 5.75% impedance at both 2,500 and 3,000 kVA. Available transformer-limited fault kVA is approximately transformer kVA divided by per-unit impedance. For the proposed 3 MVA, 480 V unit, full-load current is 3,000,000 ÷ (1.732 × 480) ≈ 3,608 A. A simplified bolted secondary fault is about 60.1 kA at 6% impedance or 62.7 kA at 5.75%. Applying the research's −7.5% manufacturing tolerance to 5.75% gives about 67.8 kA before other contributions and circuit impedance are assessed. Consequently, the old 2.5 MVA calculation and an automatic 65 kA breaker selection cannot be carried over. Specify interrupting and withstand ratings only after the selected unit's impedance, source, motor contributions and switching configurations have been studied. Lower impedance raises fault current but improves voltage regulation. Topic 6 repeats fault-duty checks downstream, including where an isolation transformer is removed. Queue mitigations remain standard designs, possible dual-rated 34.5/13.8 kV units and reservation slots before design freeze (§6).

A 2.5 MVA transformer does not automatically support a 2.5 MW UPS at full output: at 0.96 UPS efficiency and 0.9 input power factor, the illustrative input is 2.5 ÷ (0.96 × 0.9) ≈ 2.89 MVA, before recharge or other loads. A 3 MVA proposal provides little allowance on those assumptions; confirm the actual UPS input specification, site rating and feeder allocation.

Losses, efficiency and the DOE rule

Two kinds of loss. No-load core loss persists whenever a transformer is energized, potentially 8,760 hours a year. Load copper loss scales approximately with current squared: at half rated current it is a quarter of full-load copper loss. Energy estimates therefore need the energized equipment count and actual load profile, not just hall nameplate.

The rule and the reversal (verify: check current official text at order). The research identifies 10 CFR 431, the April 2013 final rule with 2016 compliance levels, the 22 April 2024 final rule with a 23 April 2029 compliance date, and DOE's action at 90 FR 43371 (9 September 2025) under the Congressional Review Act, Pub. L. 119-8. It describes the last action as removing certain December 2024 Part 431 amendments. That description alone does not establish that the April 2024 distribution-transformer rule was reversed. Verify the live 10 CFR 431 product definitions, tables and compliance dates and the specific amendments removed by the 2025 action against official text before claiming a current or future floor. Write the required efficiency level and applicable product class into the purchase specification rather than relying only on “DOE compliant.”

Efficiency levels. The research reports medium-voltage dry-type efficiency tested at 50% load, with 2016 levels of about 98.9% for a 500 kVA high-BIL unit and 99.3–99.5% for 1,500–2,500 kVA units. It reports 99.49% for a 2,500 kVA liquid-immersed unit (10 CFR 431 tables, as summarised in the research). These figures do not establish the applicable table entry or losses for this course's 3 MVA dry-type unit; obtain its certified values. The research supplies no rated loss figure for the campus power transformers.

What losses cost, and what is not yet known. The research's illustrative 2,500 kVA dry-type unit has 4 kW no-load and 27 kW full-load copper loss. At a constant 50% rated current and $0.10/kWh, its annual cost is (4 × 8,760 + 27 × 0.25 × 8,760) × $0.10 ≈ $9,400. A 100 W continuous no-load reduction saves about $88 a year at that tariff. This example cannot be multiplied into a defensible campus total for the selected 3 MVA units without their loss data and loading. Even twenty units at the example's loading would cost about $188,000 a year, not the previously claimed low single-digit millions. Add selected-unit core and load losses, large-transformer losses and UPS losses separately, using each device's operating profile and tariff, before making a campus-wide annual claim.

Only approximately 10 MW of Hall 1's 14 MW is designated UPS-protected computing load; the other approximately 4 MW includes cooling, electrical losses and other plant. Do not apply a 94% UPS-efficiency assumption to all 14 MW. For illustration only, 10 MW of UPS output at 94% efficiency requires about 10.64 MW input, a roughly 0.64 MW UPS loss at that operating point; at 97%, the corresponding loss is about 0.31 MW. Neither value is an annual estimate without measured loading, operating hours and the selected UPS efficiency curve (§9). Switchgear and cable losses need their own design data.

Medium-voltage switchgear

AIS versus GIS. Air-insulated switchgear has a larger footprint, lower cost and easier access for maintenance; it is the usual indoor MV lineup. Gas-insulated switchgear is compact and sealed, useful where space is tight, the environment harsh or voltage higher, and historically used SF6 insulation (research synthesis).

The SF6 problem. SF6 has a roughly 23,500 100-year global-warming potential on the cited IPCC AR5-era figure (arXiv 2308.13667); AR6 values appear in the GHG Protocol's August 2024 GWP table. The research describes Regulation (EU) 2024/573 as restricting new switchgear up to 24 kV from 1 January 2026 and up to 52 kV by 2030; it also reports California and New York restrictions beginning in 2025 and tightening EPA reporting. Verify the current jurisdictional restriction, its effective date, exemptions and applicable voltage class at specification time. An EU rule is not automatically the rule at this US campus.

SF6-free technologies include vacuum interruption with dry or clean air, including Siemens “blue” GIS and ABB offerings, and fluoronitrile mixtures, C4F7N or Novec 4710, marketed as g3 by GE Vernova and Hitachi Energy. Some product lines first launched at IEC 50 Hz rather than locally rated 60 Hz (EPA assessment cited in the research). Confirm the offered equipment's 34.5 kV service and applicable 38 kV class, 60 Hz rating, interrupting and arc ratings, approvals and delivery before selecting it. The reported 12–18% premium (Dataintelo) is (verify: single market-research source). The course proposes SF6-free gear, not a claim that every marketed alternative fits.

Arc resistance and selectivity solve different problems. IEEE C37.20.7 tests enclosures for internal arcing and defined personnel-accessibility locations: Type 1, front; Type 2, front, sides and rear; Type 2B, Type 2 with control-compartment doors open; and Type 2C, between compartments. The course proposes Type 2B for personnel exposure during specified energized operations. It does not make energized work generally safe or establish concurrent maintainability on its own. Selective coordination uses protective-device settings to clear a fault in the smallest feasible zone, keeping unaffected circuits supplied. Arc-resistant construction does not replace that study, and coordination does not replace an arc-rated enclosure or work procedures.

Lead time and the coordination study. Data-center-spec MV switchgear is reported at 52–84+ weeks versus a roughly 44-week all-class average; the relevant 38 kV class can take 96–120 weeks. The selective coordination study is an explicit design-build deliverable, usually assigned to the engineer of record or specialist. It needs purchased-device ratings, source and fault data, and every permitted tie and alternate-source state. Early factory-slot reservations, the Topic 3 60% one-line rating freeze (T+3–8) and final protection settings therefore form a dependency loop. Topic 6 continues the study to PDUs and arc-flash labels.

Fuel and testing. Uptime states a 12-hour minimum starting point at N load while meeting the facility topology objective: “The Uptime Institute Owners Advisory Committee defined 12 hours of minimum fuel storage as a starting point… at 'N' load while meeting the facility's stated topology objective” (Uptime Institute Journal, “Data center fuel system design and reliability”). The course's 48-hour on-site storage target is an owner choice above that minimum, not itself proof of compliance or guaranteed autonomy. The owner-supplier must assign delivery and preservation responsibilities, while the air-permit applicant, usually the owner with an environmental consultant, must secure permissions before applicable construction, testing and operation (research §4.5).

UPS

Classes and topologies. IEC 62040-3 distinguishes VFI, voltage and frequency independent double-conversion; VI, voltage independent line-interactive; and VFD, voltage and frequency dependent offline operation. The research reports static double-conversion efficiency of 94–97%, with newer designs reported at 96.8–99.3%, plus a moderate footprint and typical 5–10 minute battery ride-through. Line-interactive is reported at 97.5–98.5%, with smaller equipment and roughly 35% lower cost per kW for smaller applications. Rotary DRUPS from Piller, Hitec and Euro-Diesel combines flywheel and engine, with reported efficiency up to about 97%, 13–40 seconds of ride-through, units to approximately 2,500 kVA paralleled to 30 MVA or more, and a claimed 12–18% lower 15-year total cost; its rotating plant and different maintenance burden matter (research §5.1).

Eco-mode. A bypass-based high-efficiency mode can raise reported efficiency from 94–97% to 98–99%, but changes how upstream disturbances reach the output. Schneider's Neil Rasmussen estimates about a 2.3% saving, approximately $15,000 annually for a 1 MW site at half load and $0.10/kWh. Surge protection and the selected product's disturbance response need evaluation; some operators decline the mode, while Schneider's eConversion is a Galaxy V default. Silicon-carbide VFI designs report efficiency approaching 99% without conventional eco-mode. The course chooses VFI operation with eco-mode off; actual losses depend on selected equipment and load (§5).

Modules, blocks and paths. Hot-swappable modules of 15–500 kW can provide module-level N+1 within a frame (Schneider Galaxy VXL 500–1,250 kW; Vertiv; Eaton). That does not protect against losing an entire 2.5 MW block or its feeder. Four 2.5 MW blocks exactly supply 10 MW; five meet the simple capacity test with one block out; six are this course's installation. Its proposed distributed arrangement and independently routed downstream A/B feeds must be proved in Topic 6. An optional 2N tenant suite needs two separately routed paths, each capable of the suite's full load, from appropriate sources through UPS and downstream distribution. Extra frames alone do not make 2N.

A 2.5 MW UPS output is not necessarily compatible with a 2.5 MVA input transformer. At illustrative 96% efficiency and 0.9 input power factor, full output requires approximately 2.89 MVA input before battery recharge. Verify actual input power factor, loss curve, recharge limits, transformer site rating and feeder assignment against the course's proposed 3 MVA hall units (§4). The 2–3 MW UPS block is an economic and failure-domain planning range, not a promise that one UPS, one generator and one transformer have matching usable ratings.

Output and bypass. In VFI operation the rectifier feeds a direct-current link and the already operating inverter continuously supplies the alternating-current output. When input fails, the battery supports that link; there is no required 20 ms rack transfer. A static bypass routes around the inverter in specified conditions and cannot be assumed to retain the inverter's voltage, frequency or waveform conditioning. The course output is 480Y/277 V. Topic 6 uses a 480-to-415/240 V autotransformer at each PDU; choosing a 415 V UPS output would alter that downstream design.

Delivery. Reported 2026 lead times are 26–52 weeks for 500 kVA–1.5 MW frames, with 26–40-week IGBT and SiC component queues beneath them. Name the factory witness test as a hand-off and confirm the separate battery-cabinet delivery (§11).

The AI load at the UPS

GPU clusters can ramp quickly when jobs start, checkpoint or stop. Synchronized computation can produce repeated load swings, but their magnitude and frequency depend on the workload, rack controls, onboard capacitance and how pods operate together. The research offers tens of MW in seconds, a brief 150% of steady-state load and buffering targeted at swings up to ~30 Hz as scenarios, not universal equipment requirements or guaranteed measurements (research §5.5). A sufficiently large ramp can exceed inverter or battery limits and, on generator power, challenge governor and voltage-regulator response.

Request measured or vendor-guaranteed rack, pod and hall ramp magnitudes, durations, repetition rates, power-factor behaviour and power-cap settings. Model the UPS inverter and direct-current-link limits, battery or supercapacitor buffering, generator step-load and recharge response, and protection settings together, in grid and engine-supplied states. Candidate mitigations include UPS capacity, supercapacitors or additional battery, a suitable BESS, staged workload ramps and NVIDIA-SMI power limits; Tesla and Fluence target buffering applications, while EPRI DCFlex addresses grid-side coordination (research §10). Select a mitigation against the delivered load profile rather than a single headline percentage.

Hand-off to Topic 8. Topic 8's GB300 research describes a rack-side 0.2–3 Hz swing scenario and 65 J per GPU of on-board capacitance trimming peaks seen by the facility by about 30%. Those workload-specific figures do not establish what every rack sends to the UPS or prove that a 30 Hz buffer is needed here. The course has 200 planned 120 kW AI racks, about 61% of the stated 38.9 MW rack mix. Keep a possible buffering position on the one-line while the rack vendor and operator establish what reaches each power block; rack-level design belongs to Topic 8.

Installed battery energy must be greater to cover conversion losses, discharge limits, ageing, temperature and reserve. The other approximately 4 MW of hall demand is not automatically battery-backed.

Market share, with unlike measures. VRLA is reported as roughly 61.76% of installed capacity or market in one 2025 estimate (Mordor Intelligence, 2025), while “lithium-ion now captures approximately 40% of data center backup UPS installations across all facility types, rising to 55% at hyperscale” (Vertiv, December 2025, via Introl and Axis Intelligence). Installed base and new installations can differ. Reported lithium economics include about a 35% initial premium, 39% lower ten-year total cost and 60% smaller footprint (verify: vendor-derived).

Codes and this UPS battery. UL 9540A is a fire-propagation test method; UL 9540 is a system listing. They are not interchangeable approvals. NFPA 855 is an installation standard, but first establish whether and how its provisions apply to this particular UPS battery under the locally adopted edition and code, including any UPS-specific scope or exceptions (verify with the AHJ and adopted official text). The research identifies 2020, 2023 and 2026 editions, with IFC 2021 pointing to 855-2020 and IFC 2024 to 855-2023. Its general statement about a 20 kWh lithium threshold is not a safe universal rule for all UPS batteries. Ask the AHJ which listing, test reports, unit limits, spacing, ventilation, deflagration control, sprinkler provisions under NFPA 13 and separations apply. UL 9540A results may support alternative spacing or unit arrangements where the adopted provisions permit them. Do not assert that every UPS needs the same room or explosion-control layout.

The course chooses dedicated, ventilated, fire-rated LFP battery rooms; approval of that design remains a project gate. Clean-agent suppression alone does not stop self-sustaining thermal runaway; the complete fire strategy belongs to Topic 10. Cabinet purchasing and installation may be owner- or UPS-vendor-furnished, but warranty and end-of-life recycling, especially for VRLA replacements, must be assigned. UL 9540A historically lacked fixed pass/fail targets, leaving AHJ interpretation and potentially “a protracted period” of case-by-case review (Data Center Frontier). Allow review time rather than treating the chosen layout as already approved.

BESS beyond the UPS (research §6.4). A separate BESS can serve peak shaving, flexible interconnection, grid services or load smoothing, but does not replace the specified UPS ride-through. A reported 5 MW/10 MWh commercial system saved over $500,000 annually on demand charges after a 35% reduction (SunLith, 2025); a flat, 24/7 data-center load may have less shifting opportunity. A Duke University study (February 2025) estimated approximately 100 GW of new load could connect with minimal grid impact if flexible, motivating further interconnection analysis, not guaranteeing an earlier date for this campus. The research reports a roughly five-year median interconnection wait and some PJM cases above eight years. A grid-services BESS might connect at MV, while transient smoothing may be nearer UPS or LV equipment (Fluence; Tesla Megapack). The course reserves a 34.5 kV bus position and does not buy a BESS in Phase 1.

NFPA 110 Type 10 is a different test. It concerns backup power at designated transfer-switch load terminals within ten seconds. An engine reaching speed around ten seconds does not prove that requirement, and this illustration's approximately 15-second hall transfer is not itself Type 10 compliance. Identify any designated Type 10 loads and verify their actual transfer arrangements and witnessed timing. The course's five-minute battery specification gives a planned window for this transition and retries at stated load and battery condition, not immunity to repeated start failure (§11). Topic 12 develops operating procedures.

What “clean” means at the UPS output. In normal VFI operation, the specified 480Y/277 V output is supplied by the inverter rather than raw mains. Indicative performance targets from the research are approximately ±1% steady-state voltage, regulated 60 Hz and typically under 2–3% voltage THD, subject to the selected UPS specifications and transients. Battery support can preserve that output through the illustrated upstream loss and generator transfer. It cannot guarantee an energized, in-tolerance bus after batteries are exhausted, while an unconditioned bypass is carrying the load, or after an unisolated downstream fault. The hand-off to Topic 6 is therefore a specified protected output under defined operating and fault conditions, not an assertion that the bus is always energized or that downstream A/B independence and selectivity have already been proved.
