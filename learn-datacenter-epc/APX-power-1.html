Appendix: Power Systems I, Utility to UPS

Sources and fine detail behind the chapter. Sections are numbered so the chapter can point at them. The research report behind both.

The detail behind the Topic 5 chapter: the power chain from the utility demarcation to the UPS output bus as it stands in 2025–26. Service voltage and demarcation; what "two feeds" buys; substation layout; transformers, impedance, losses and the DOE rule's uncertain state; the Phase 1 sizing arithmetic with the lead-time table and the owner-furnished risks; MV switchgear and the SF6 transition; generators, fuel and the air permit; UPS topologies, eco-mode and the AI load; batteries and their codes; and the utility-failure sequence that defines "clean" where Topic 6 begins. The chapter cites sections by number (APX-power-1 §n). Non-obvious claims carry their source in parentheses; "(verify)" marks figures the research flagged as soft, dated or single-sourced. All worked numbers are indicative. Interconnection and procurement models are Topic 3's appendix; everything below the UPS output bus is Topic 6's.

LandGate puts colocation at 5–50 MW on 69 or 115 kV and hyperscale at 115–138 kV. For the reference campus at ~56 MW the defensible 2026 choice is a dedicated customer substation at 115 or 138 kV; Topic 3 fixed 138 kV with two incoming lines.

Demarcation. The boundary is the demarcation point, usually the line-side terminals of the incoming structure or the high-side breaker. The utility owns the line, the revenue metering CTs and PTs and typically the first high-side protective device, and it owns the energization decision; the customer owns everything downstream. Under design-build with owner-furnished equipment the design-builder engineers and builds the customer side while the utility builds its own (or the customer builds and deeds it back) under the interconnection agreement. Substation construction runs 2–4 years when the utility's scope is included; the two schedule risks are the study timeline (Topic 3) and the LPT lead time (§6).

Verdict on the explorer's second rule. The landing page's bands (under 15 MW distribution; 15–80 MW at 69–138 kV; over 80 MW at 230 kV and up) are roughly right at the edges, but the middle band is too wide and the top threshold too high for 2026; the table above is the corrected pattern. The 56 MW campus lands at 115–138 kV either way, but many 60–80 MW sites now connect at 230 kV. Every utility sets its own thresholds; the interconnection study governs. The code change is recorded in the plan, not made here.

Dual feeds as a spectrum of independence

"Dual feed" is a spectrum, and buyers routinely pay for the label without the substance. In ascending independence and cost: two feeders from the same substation bus, which survive a feeder fault and little else; two feeders from different substations, which survive a single substation loss; and feeds from different transmission paths, which survive a transmission contingency, the strongest, rarest and most expensive (research synthesis).

Uptime requires nothing of the utility side. The Tier Standard: Topology states that "the only reliable source of power for a data center is the engine-generator plant" and that "the number of utility feeds, substations, and power grids… neither predicts nor influences" the Tier (Uptime Institute, Tier Standard: Topology). Dual feeds reduce how often the engines run, which is worth real money in fuel, wear and permit hours, but earn no Tier credit. The reference campus takes two 138 kV lines for that reason; which arrangement the utility can offer is a facilities-study question, and the answer is often the first.

Substation layout, protection and campus voltage

Bus arrangements, ascending in resilience and cost: radial; main-tie-main (double-ended); ring bus; breaker-and-a-half. For Tier III main-tie-main is the workhorse: either transformer or bus section comes out with the tie closed and no load dropped. Open a main, feed both sections through the tie, maintain the dead side. The pattern repeats at the unit substations and, in Topic 6, at the distribution.

Protection. A fault must clear at the nearest upstream breaker only. That requires a selective coordination study covering relay curves, interrupting ratings and arc-flash. Vacuum breakers dominate at MV, driven by microprocessor relays implementing the IEEE device functions: 50/51 overcurrent, 87 differential, 27/59 under- and over-voltage, plus bus differential and breaker-failure. Ownership of the study is in §7.

Footprint (verify). The research gives only "on the order of several acres" including pads, firewalls, control building and clearances; no source quantifies it further.

13.8 kV versus 34.5 kV. 34.5 kV "has become the workhorse distribution voltage for large campuses because it moves more power with less copper and fewer feeders" (ATK Energy, 2026); 13.8 kV is simpler and cheaper for smaller sites. For a 56 MW campus with distributed 10 MW halls, 34.5 kV is the defensible choice, each hall stepping down at its own unit substations. The cost is that 38 kV-class switchgear carries the longest lead times in the market (§7).

Transformer populations, fluids and impedance

Two populations. The large power transformer (LPT) steps HV to MV, here 138 kV to 34.5 kV, typically 30–100 MVA, oil-filled, outdoors; the campus needs two to three (N+1) for 56 MW. The MV/LV unit-substation transformer steps 34.5 kV to 480Y/277 V at 1,000–3,000 kVA, feeding UPS blocks and mechanical plant; dozens across the campus, and the units the DOE rules bite on (§5).

Oil, dry and ester. Oil-filled wins on efficiency, cooling and power density and is standard outdoors, but indoors it triggers NEC Article 450's vault, containment and fire-separation requirements. Dry-type is the standard indoor choice for unit substations feeding UPS: no flammable liquid, no sump, inspection on a cycle of years against oil sampling on a cycle of months. Natural and synthetic esters (Cargill FR3, Eaton Envirotemp) are a less-flammable middle path (research synthesis).

Impedance. Per IEEE C57.12.00 the nominal impedance of a 2,500 kVA unit is 5.75% (Federal Pacific's catalogue confirms 5.75% at 2,500 and 3,000 kVA). Available fault kVA ≈ transformer kVA ÷ %Z as a per-unit value. For one reference unit at 480 V: full-load current 2,500,000 ÷ (1.732 × 480) = 3,007 A; bolted secondary fault at 6% Z ≈ 3,007 ÷ 0.06 ≈ 50,100 A (~50 kA), ≈ 52 kA at the 5.75% nominal; breakers rated 65 kA. Lower %Z means higher fault current and a bigger breaker but better voltage regulation. Engineers size interrupting ratings against the −7.5% manufacturing tolerance on impedance. Topic 6 picks this up at the PDU, where removing the isolation transformer raises fault current again. Mitigations on the transformer queue: standard designs, dual-rated units (34.5/13.8 kV) and reservation slots booked before the design freezes (§6).

Losses, efficiency and the DOE rule

Two kinds of loss. No-load (core) loss is constant whenever the unit is energized, 8,760 hours a year. Load (copper) loss scales with the square of current: at 50% load it is a quarter of its full-load value.

The rule and the reversal (verify: confirm the live CFR text at order). The standard in force in 10 CFR 431 was set by the April 2013 final rule with 2016 compliance. DOE's final rule of 22 April 2024 set mandatory compliance for 23 April 2029, tightening medium-voltage dry-type (MVDT) losses by roughly 10%. Then, under the Congressional Review Act (Pub. L. 119-8), at 90 FR 43371 (9 September 2025) DOE removed certain December 2024 amendments to Part 431. The research's reading: the 2016 floors apply to units installed before 2029, the 2029 floors remain the forward target, and the exact forward floor is genuinely uncertain. A buyer writes the efficiency level into the purchase specification rather than relying on "DOE compliant".

Efficiency levels. MVDT efficiency is tested at 50% load. The 2016 levels run from about 98.9% for a 500 kVA high-BIL unit to 99.3–99.5% for 1,500–2,500 kVA; a 2,500 kVA liquid-immersed unit is 99.49% (10 CFR 431 tables, as summarised in the research). The research gives no figure for the LPT; the chapter's breakdown labels its ~0.5% indicative.

What losses cost. Against a ~$34M annual electricity bill (Topic 1), one 2,500 kVA dry-type unit at ~4 kW no-load and ~27 kW full-load copper loss, 50% load factor, $0.10/kWh: (4 × 8,760 + 27 × 0.25 × 8,760) kWh × $0.10 ≈ $9,400 a year per transformer. Across twenty-plus unit substations at full build plus the LPTs, transformer losses run into the low single-digit millions a year; a 100 W no-load reduction saves ~$88 a year per unit. The UPS loses an order of magnitude more (§9), which is why the chapter's breakdown puts 3–6% there and half a point at each transformer stage; switchgear and cable losses are small and unsourced.

The LPT rating question. Two 30 MVA units hold N+1 only to about 27 MVA, roughly Phase 1 and part of Phase 2. The alternative, 2 × 45–50 MVA on day one, holds N+1 through 40 MW with no third order at the price of more copper up front. Either way the 138 kV substation is sized for ~56 MW from day one, and each later ~10 MW hall repeats the Phase 1 set off the shared 34.5 kV bus; Phase 1 equipment is never resized, only joined (research §8).

Verdict on the explorer's first rule. "N+1 blocks = ceil(IT ÷ 2.5) + 1" is right for UPS, where 2–3 MW is the 2025–26 sweet spot, and wrong for generators: data center diesels cluster at 2.25–3.25 MW (Cummins DQKAN 2.5, Cat C175-16 3.0, Rehlko KD3250 3.25), roughly one per UPS block, but the count follows IT × PUE. Corrected: gensets = ceil(IT × PUE ÷ ~2.75) + 1; 3M2 adds one more UPS block. Recorded in the plan, not changed here.

Every source says the same thing: reserve factory slots before the design freezes. OEM expansions (Siemens' Georgia plant, ABB's vacuum-interrupter line) are not expected to bite until 2027 or later (research §10).

Owner-furnished risk. Furnishing the LPTs, switchgear, gensets and UPS, the owner takes on the design freeze (ratings lock early to hold the slot, and the coordination study waits on them, §7); storage, since units often arrive before the pad and someone must insure them and keep the preservation heaters energized; warranty administration; and the factory acceptance test as a named hand-off (Topic 11). The classic dispute is ship date against pad-ready date: if the pad is late the owner-as-supplier eats demurrage and storage, a cost that in design-bid-build sits with a contractor. The contract names late owner-furnished equipment as an owner delay and says who stores, insures and heats early gear (research §2.6, 4.5, 5.7). Two further gates sit with the owner: the air permit that gates generator testing (§8) and the AHJ's approval of lithium rooms (§11).

Medium-voltage switchgear

AIS versus GIS. Air-insulated switchgear is larger, cheaper and easiest to maintain, the default indoor MV lineup. Gas-insulated switchgear is compact and sealed, used where space is tight, the environment harsh or the voltage higher, and was historically insulated with SF6 (research synthesis).

The SF6 problem. SF6's 100-year GWP is about 23,500 on the IPCC AR5-era figure (arXiv 2308.13667); AR6 values are in the GHG Protocol's August 2024 GWP table. Regulation (EU) 2024/573 bans SF6 in new MV switchgear up to 24 kV from 1 January 2026 and up to 52 kV by 2030; California and New York ban new SF6 gear from 2025; EPA reporting is tightening. Many operators specify SF6-free voluntarily under SBTi commitments.

SF6-free technologies: vacuum interruption with dry or clean air (Siemens "blue" GIS; ABB), and fluoronitrile mixtures, C4F7N or Novec 4710, marketed as g3 (GE Vernova; Hitachi Energy). Two US caveats. Some SF6-free lines launched 50 Hz IEC-only and are not yet 60 Hz rated (EPA assessment cited in the research) (verify: confirm 60 Hz availability of the specific line at order). SF6-free MV gear commands a 12–18% premium (Dataintelo) (verify: single market-research source). The reference campus specifies SF6-free at 34.5 kV and confirms the 60 Hz rating in the purchase order.

Arc-resistant gear, IEEE C37.20.7. The standard tests switchgear for internal arcing faults and rates where a person can safely stand: Type 1, front only; Type 2, front, sides and rear; Type 2B, Type 2 with control-compartment doors open; Type 2C, between compartments. Data centers commonly specify Type 2B for energized work, which is what concurrent maintainability means in practice; it carries a cost and footprint premium but is near-universal in mission-critical lineups.

Lead time and the coordination study. Data-center-spec MV switchgear runs 52–84+ weeks against a ~44-week all-class average, and 96–120 weeks for the 38 kV class. The selective coordination study is a deliverable someone must explicitly own, usually the engineer of record. It cannot finish until owner-furnished ratings are frozen, so study, freeze and slot form a loop; the campus freezes ratings at the 60% one-line (T+3–8, Topic 3) and names the study's owner in the design-build agreement. Topic 6 carries it to the PDU and the arc-flash labels.

Amazon's Manassas, Virginia site has 93 generators at 2.5 MW each (Latitude Media, "The data center boom is a diesel generator boom"); the reference campus at full build will have about two dozen.

Tier 2 versus Tier 4 Final. EPA Tier 2 is allowed for true emergency standby only. The moment a set runs for demand response, peak shaving or bridge power, federal rules push it to Tier 4 Final with SCR and often DPF, a real capex and footprint adder; this happened in Virginia in June 2025 when Tier 2 diesels ran for demand response in a heatwave. The air permit sets allowable run hours, monthly test caps and reporting, and in non-attainment areas can gate the project. The reference hall's Tier 2, emergency-only, HVO-capable choice is the cheapest permit and set; if Topic 3's gas bridge or a demand-response programme becomes real, it moves to Tier 4F and the permit is rewritten.

Gas as prime power (verify: dated; see Topic 3). INNIO's 21 October 2025 release describes "the largest order by power delivery in its history: a groundbreaking 2.3 GW power infrastructure project featuring 92 power packs, each delivering 25 MW," with VoltaGrid for Oracle Cloud Infrastructure (POWER Magazine, 15 October 2025). ERCOT's large-load queue was ~226 GW in November 2025, ~77% data centers, and "more than 233 GW" by 9 December (ERCOT board via Latitude Media and Utility Dive); ~50 GW of behind-the-meter capacity was announced in 2025. Bridge or permanent architecture is the open question; Topic 3 priced the bridge and did not buy it.

Sizing and architecture. Generators are rated in kW and kVA; at 0.8 pf, 700 kW needs 875 kVA, and assuming 0.9 pf when the truth is 0.75 undersizes the plant by over 15%. A UPS-fed load presents a hard transient: on transfer the rectifiers draw a large step plus recharge current, which drives oversizing relative to steady load and governor and AVR specification to an ISO 8528-5 transient class. Five 2.75 MW sets give 13.75 MW against a 14 MW nameplate hall load; the diversified design load (Topic 2) covers the gap, indicatively. Paralleling switchgear synchronizes several sets onto a common bus for economical N+1; isolated-redundant dedicates a standby set. The research is silent on the generator bus voltage; the plan assumes MV paralleling onto the 34.5 kV bus per hall (verify). NFPA 110 Type 10 means power at the transfer switch load terminals within 10 seconds.

Fuel and testing. Uptime requires a 12-hour minimum for all Tiers: "The Uptime Institute Owners Advisory Committee defined 12 hours of minimum fuel storage as a starting point… at 'N' load while meeting the facility's stated topology objective" (Uptime Institute Journal, "Data center fuel system design and reliability"). Specifications commonly target 24–72 h, NFPA 110 Level 1 practice references 96 h for some systems, and "72 hours" is an owner convention. Sub-base ("belly") tanks give immediate supply; bulk storage and day tanks provide the autonomy target, with fuel polishing against microbial growth and water, and resupply contracts. The reference hall carries 48 h with a 24 h resupply contract. Testing: monthly light-load exercise and an annual load-bank test to full rating, inside the permit's run-hour caps.

Delivery and risk. Gensets and paralleling gear are owner-furnished long-lead items (50–110 weeks). The air permit is a gating item: construction can be halted for emissions non-compliance. Delay risk sits with the owner as supplier; permit risk with whoever holds the application, usually the owner and its environmental consultant.

UPS

Classes and topologies. IEC 62040-3 classifies output as VFI (voltage and frequency independent, double-conversion), VI (voltage independent, line-interactive) and VFD (voltage and frequency dependent, offline). Static double-conversion runs at 94–97%, the newest at 96.8–99.3%, with the best transient response because the load never sees raw mains, a moderate footprint and 5–10 minutes of battery: the data center default. Line-interactive runs at 97.5–98.5%, smaller and about 35% cheaper per kW, for edge and small colo. Rotary DRUPS (Piller, Hitec, Euro-Diesel) reaches ~97%, couples a flywheel to a diesel for 13–40 s of ride-through with no battery, in units to ~2,500 kVA paralleled to 30 MVA and more; it removes battery rooms at the price of a large rotating machine with an engine attached, claims a 12–18% lower 15-year TCO, and is rarer in US colocation (research §5.1).

Eco-mode. Feeding the load through the bypass and conditioning only on a disturbance lifts efficiency from 94–97% to 98–99%. Schneider's Neil Rasmussen puts the saving at ~2.3%, about $15,000 a year for a 1 MW site at 50% load and $0.10/kWh. The load sees raw mains in normal operation, so surge protection becomes mandatory and some operators refuse the mode; adoption is higher in Europe. Schneider's eConversion is now the Galaxy V default. Silicon-carbide VFI designs reach ~99% without eco-mode, which may make the debate moot. The reference hall runs VFI with eco-mode off, accepting 3–6 points for a load that never sees the grid.

Modular frames and the block. Modular UPS build from hot-swappable 15–500 kW modules with N+1 inside the frame (Schneider Galaxy VXL 500–1,250 kW; Vertiv; Eaton). The block sweet spot is 2–3 MW: large enough for economy of scale, small enough to be a granular failure domain and to match one genset and one unit substation. Each reference block is frames paralleled to 2.5 MW with N+1 modules inside, so a module failure is invisible before Topic 6's block-level redundancy is counted.

Output voltage. 480/277 V is the traditional North American output; 415/240 V is increasingly common because it delivers 240 V to the server supply without another transformer. The reference hall's output is 480Y/277 V; Topic 6 places a 480-to-415/240 V autotransformer in each PDU and notes that a 415 V UPS output would remove it.

Delivery. 26–52 weeks for 500 kVA–1.5 MW frames in 2026, with IGBT and SiC component lead times of 26–40 weeks underneath. The factory witness test is the named hand-off. Battery cabinets are a second queue inside the order (§11).

The AI load at the UPS

A GPU cluster ramps tens of MW in seconds as training jobs start, checkpoint and stop; synchronized all-reduce steps create periodic second-scale swings, and clusters can briefly hit 150% of steady state (research §5.5). The transient can exceed the UPS's ability to source and condition power, pushing it onto stored energy, and can destabilise gensets when the hall is on engine power. There is no settled fix. Four compete: oversizing the UPS; energy buffering with supercapacitors, extra battery or Megapack-class BESS (Tesla and Fluence target swings up to ~30 Hz); software smoothing through power capping, dummy workloads and staged ramps (NVIDIA-SMI limits); and grid-side coordination through EPRI DCFlex (research §10).

Hand-off to Topic 8. Topic 8's research on the GB300 generation gives the rack side: racks swing load at 0.2–3 Hz, and 65 J per GPU of on-board capacitance trims the peaks the facility sees by about 30%. The figures are compatible: tens of hertz is the target of grid-side buffering; sub-3 Hz is what a current rack presents after its own capacitors have worked. What reaches the UPS input is a negotiation between rack vendor, buffering and block oversizing, and the AI pods (200 racks at 120 kW, 61% of load) are where it happens. Topic 5 sizes the pods' blocks with the transient in view and keeps a buffering position on the one-line; the rack side is Topic 8.

With gensets starting in ≤ 10 s, operators size batteries to cover the transfer plus retries, not hours; lithium's selling point is footprint and cycle life, not runtime.

Market share (the sources conflict). VRLA still leads the installed base at ~61.76% (Mordor Intelligence, 2025), while "lithium-ion now captures approximately 40% of data center backup UPS installations across all facility types, rising to 55% at hyperscale" (Vertiv, December 2025, via Introl and Axis Intelligence). One measures the installed base, the other new installations; both can be true. Lithium carries a ~35% premium against a ~39% lower ten-year TCO and a 60% smaller footprint (verify: vendor-derived).

Codes. UL 9540A is the fire-propagation test method; UL 9540 the system listing; NFPA 855 the installation standard, which requires any lithium system over 20 kWh to be UL 9540 listed and UL 9540A tested. Editions 2020, 2023 and 2026 (released September 2025); the binding one is whatever the AHJ adopted (IFC 2021 points to 855-2020, IFC 2024 to 855-2023). NFPA 855 sets spacing (about 3 ft), per-unit kWh caps, ventilation, deflagration control, sprinklers per NFPA 13 and separation, but UL 9540A test data can justify closer spacing and larger units; the 2026 edition shifts toward analysis and testing (research §10). These codes moved lithium out of the UPS room into dedicated, ventilated, fire-rated battery rooms with explosion control; the chapter's cutaways draw that wall. Clean-agent suppression does not stop thermal runaway because the reaction supplies its own oxygen; fire protection is Topic 10.

Approval and delivery risk. The owner or the UPS vendor buys and installs the cabinets; warranty and end-of-life recycling, especially for retired VRLA, must be assigned. The sharpest risk is an AHJ new to lithium: UL 9540A historically had no fixed pass/fail targets, so AHJs interpret results locally, producing "a protracted period" of case-by-case review (Data Center Frontier). The campus budgets schedule for AHJ education.

BESS beyond the UPS (research §6.4). Peak shaving: a 5 MW/10 MWh C&I system cut demand charges 35% and saved over $500k a year (SunLith, 2025), but a 24/7 data center has no off-peak trough. Flexible interconnection: a Duke University study (February 2025) found ~100 GW of new load could connect with minimal grid impact if flexible, and BESS makes that feasible without curtailing uptime, the strongest 2026 case because it can shorten the interconnection wait (median ~5 years, some PJM cases over 8). Position: the MV bus for grid services, the UPS or LV level for transient smoothing (Fluence, Tesla Megapack). Verdict: marginal for peak shaving alone, increasingly yes for interconnection acceleration plus smoothing plus demand response. The reference campus reserves a BESS position at the 34.5 kV bus and does not buy it in Phase 1.

The utility-failure sequence and the clean bus

Second by second, Hall 1, indicative (research §5.6):

TimeEvent
t = 0Utility drops; MV relays detect loss of voltage or frequency
0–20 msUPS carries critical load on battery through the inverter, no break (VFI); the output bus stays clean
1–3 sGenerators receive the start signal, crank, come up to speed
≤ 10 sSets reach rated voltage and frequency (NFPA 110 Type 10); paralleling gear synchronizes them onto the 34.5 kV bus
10–15 sClosed-transition transfer moves mechanical load and UPS rectifiers onto generator power; batteries recharge
Utility returnsControls verify stable utility for a set interval, then retransfer, usually closed-transition; sets cool down and stop

The load sees nothing at any step. The battery covers steps two to five, about 10–15 s of the five minutes it holds; the margin is for start failure and retries. Topic 12 extends the sequence into operating procedures.

What "clean" means at the UPS output bus. Voltage regulated at 480Y/277 V, ±1% steady state with tight transient bounds. Frequency 60 Hz, tightly held; a VFI output is frequency-independent of its input. Waveform a low-distortion sine, typically under 2–3% THDv, regardless of upstream disturbance. Availability continuous through utility loss and genset transfer, which is the whole point of the chain. Topic 5 ends at the UPS output bus, where clean power is available to distribute; Topic 6 starts at that same bus, inherits one that is always energized, always in tolerance and coordinated, and must maintain A/B independence and selectivity from there to the rack.
