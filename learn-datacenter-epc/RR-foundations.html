Topic 1 of 14 — The Vocabulary and Mental Model Every Later Topic Assumes

TL;DR - A data center is a building engineered to convert electricity into computation and heat reliably; for a 40 MW IT-load colocation campus in a hot US metro (Phoenix/Dallas), expect indicative all-in construction cost of roughly $400–500 million ($10–12.5M per MW of IT load, excluding tenant IT gear), annual facility operating cost of roughly $40–60 million (electricity dominant and largely passed through to tenants), and a 15-year total cost of ownership on the order of $1.2–1.4 billion. - In 2025–26 such an owner would most likely deliver the facility as a design-build / turnkey contract with owner-furnished long-lead equipment (transformers, switchgear, generators, chillers ordered early against an owner-standard reference design), because power interconnection and equipment lead times — not construction labor — are now the binding schedule constraint, and design-build is the fastest, most schedule-reliable model with a single point of responsibility. - The market context is a demand supercycle: US data centers reached 176 TWh (4.4% of US electricity) in 2023 per LBNL; hyperscalers now hold 48% of global capacity (Synergy, Q4 2025); North American colocation vacancy hit a record-low 1.4% at year-end 2025 (CBRE), and AI/liquid-cooled builds now carry a construction premium and far higher cost-per-MW.

1. What a Data Center Is, and the Types That Exist

1.1 A plain definition and the physical framing

A data center is a purpose-built facility that houses computing, storage, and networking equipment and supplies it with conditioned power, cooling, connectivity, and physical security so it can run continuously. The most useful mental model for a newcomer is thermodynamic: a data center is a machine that converts electricity into computation and an almost equal quantity of heat. Essentially all electrical energy drawn by IT equipment is dissipated as heat that must be removed continuously; the building exists to deliver clean power to the chips and to move that heat back out.

Quantifying where the electricity goes: in a typical facility, IT equipment consumes roughly 60–65% of total power, cooling roughly 30–40%, and power-distribution/conversion losses (UPS, transformers, PDUs) plus lighting and ancillary loads the small remainder. One industry teardown of a 100 MW facility running at PUE 1.54 apportioned it as roughly 65% to IT compute, with UPS/distribution conversion losses around 4 MW and lighting/security/BMS/fire around 4 MW, the rest to cooling (DC Atlas, 2025). These proportions shift with efficiency: the global average PUE of respondents was 1.54–1.56 in Uptime Institute's 2024 and 2025 surveys, while best-in-class hyperscale runs far lower — Google's 2025 Environmental Report reports it is "operating with a 2025 fleet-wide average power usage effectiveness (PUE) of 1.09 and using 83% less overhead energy than the industry average."

1.2 Facility types and who owns what

The industry sorts data centers two ways: by who owns and uses the facility, and by scale and location. Definitions vary between sources, so confirm against the specific operator.

  • Enterprise: Private facility owned and operated by a single organization for its own IT. Historically the dominant model; Synergy reports on-premise/enterprise fell from 56% of global capacity in 2018 to about 32% at end-2025, and forecasts 19% by 2031.
  • Colocation (multi-tenant): A provider owns and operates the building and rents space, power, and cooling to multiple tenants who install their own IT gear. Three sub-models:
    • Retail colocation — smaller footprints, roughly 100 kW to ~1 MW per customer, priced per rack/cabinet with bundled power, 1–3 year terms (e.g., Equinix, Digital Realty, CoreSite).
    • Wholesale colocation — larger dedicated halls or whole buildings to a single tenant, roughly 250 kW to tens of MW, priced per kW with 5–15 year leases.
    • Powered shell — the provider delivers the building, land, and utility power capacity, and the tenant fits out the electrical/mechanical interior itself (often a triple-net lease). Powered land/shell is roughly 15–20% of a finished facility's cost.
  • Hyperscale: Very large standardized facilities built and run by cloud/platform operators (AWS, Microsoft, Google, Meta), commonly ~100 MW and up, with campuses in the hundreds of MW and gigawatt-scale sites planned. Hyperscalers both self-build (Synergy: ~60% of hyperscale capacity is owned, own-built at end-2025) and lease wholesale from colocation developers (~40%).
  • Edge and micro: Small facilities (roughly 1–10 MW for edge, down to single-rack "micro") placed close to users or data sources to cut latency.
  • Modular / prefabricated: A construction method rather than a use-case — factory-built power/cooling skids or whole data-hall modules trucked to site for fast assembly (e.g., AWS's factory-built "Project Houdini" white-space skids). Modular can cut schedule and, per some modeled estimates, 20–30% of capex versus a comparable traditional build.
  • "AI factory" / GPU cloud (emerging): Single-workload, single-tenant campuses purpose-built for GPU training/inference clusters at very high rack density (often 30–130 kW per rack) requiring liquid cooling. Operators are frequently "neoclouds" (e.g., CoreWeave, Nscale) leasing land and powered shells on net terms. Synergy forecasts the neocloud market approaching $400B by 2031.

Boundaries are blurring. Hyperscalers increasingly lease wholesale rather than self-build to move faster; colocation developers increasingly build entire single-tenant campuses to a hyperscaler's spec ("build-to-suit"); and master-planned wholesale campuses now target 100–500 MW total with modular, phased data halls (datacenters.com, 2025).

1.3 Market structure in 2025–26 (a few well-sourced numbers)

  • US energy footprint: US data centers consumed 176 TWh in 2023, or 4.4% of total US electricity, up from 58 TWh in 2014; LBNL projects 325–580 TWh by 2028 (6.7–12.0% of US electricity) — 2024 LBNL/DOE United States Data Center Energy Usage Report, published December 2024.
  • Global electricity: The IEA estimated data centers used about 415 TWh globally in 2024, roughly 1.5% of world electricity.
  • Ownership concentration: Per Synergy Research Group, hyperscale operators ran 1,360 large data centers at the end of Q4 2025 and "now account for 48% of the worldwide capacity of all data centers," with "almost 60% of that hyperscale capacity … in own-built, owned data centers"; the three largest (Amazon, Microsoft, Google) together hold 58% of hyperscale capacity. The US accounts for about 55% of worldwide hyperscale capacity.
  • Growth and AI share: LBNL found data-center power demand more than doubled between 2017 and 2023, driven chiefly by AI/accelerated servers; the compound growth rate rose from ~7% (2014–2018) to ~18% (2018–2023). Preleasing shows where new demand comes from: CBRE's H1 2025 report (published Sept 8, 2025) found "74.3% of all under-construction capacity already committed," and JLL's 2026 Global Data Center Outlook independently cites "77% of the construction pipeline pre-committed to tenants" — primarily cloud and AI occupiers.
  • Vacancy: Per CBRE Research (North America Data Center Trends H2 2025), "the overall vacancy rate in primary markets fell to a record low 1.4% at year-end," with record net absorption of 2,497.6 MW (Northern Virginia led at 1,102.0 MW) — power access, not construction, is now the binding constraint. Dallas-Fort Worth surpassed 1 GW of total supply with 2.4% vacancy and absorbed 470.8 MW; ~700 MW under construction was 94.5% preleased (CBRE, H2 2025).

1.4 Useful units and conventions

  • IT load — the power drawn by the computing equipment itself (servers, storage, network). The primary way a data center's size is quoted. Our reference facility is "40 MW IT."
  • Facility load (total load) — IT load plus cooling, losses, and ancillary loads; always larger than IT load. At PUE 1.4, a 40 MW IT facility draws ~56 MW total.
  • Critical load — the portion of load supported by protected, redundant power (UPS/generator-backed); in practice close to the IT load plus critical cooling.
  • MW as the unit of a building — capacity, cost ($/MW), leases ($/kW), and grid interconnection are all denominated in power, not square feet. Power, not floor area, is the scarce resource.
  • Racks and rack units (U) — a rack is the standard equipment cabinet; a rack unit (1U = 1.75 in) measures device height. A standard rack has ~42U.
  • kW per rack — the density measure. Traditional enterprise/colo ran 3–10 kW/rack; AI/GPU deployments run 20–130 kW/rack, forcing liquid cooling.
  • White space vs gray space — white space is the IT floor (racks, servers); gray space is the support area housing UPS, switchgear, generators, and cooling plant. A rough planning ratio is ~1:1, but it rises with redundancy and density.
  • PUE (defined once): Power Usage Effectiveness = total facility power ÷ IT power. A PUE of 1.0 is a theoretical ideal (all power to IT); 1.5 means 50% overhead for cooling and losses. Industry average was ~1.54–1.56 (Uptime Institute, 2024–25); hyperscale best-in-class ~1.09 (Google, 2025). A later topic owns efficiency metrics in detail.

2. The Facility Lifecycle

2.1 Stages from idea to end of life (with 2025–26 durations for a 40 MW-class facility)

A 40 MW facility from decision to operation typically runs 3–6 years today, with the "power clock" now dominating. Indicative stage durations:

  1. Business case & site strategy (3–9 months) — demand thesis, target market, budget, return targets, delivery-model decision.
  2. Site & power acquisition (6–24+ months, often the longest and most variable) — land, zoning/entitlement, and above all grid interconnection. Interconnection now routinely exceeds four years in primary markets; PJM projects have averaged over three years to reach an interconnection service agreement and another ~four years to come online (Data Center Knowledge, 2026). This has stretched dramatically.
  3. Design (6–12 months, overlapping) — progressing through Concept → Schematic Design (SD) → Design Development (DD) → Construction Documents (CD). (US convention; local equivalents differ.)
  4. Procurement (overlaps design; long-lead items ordered 12–36 months ahead) — substation transformer lead times ran ~140 weeks in 2023, ~150 in 2025, and exceed 160 weeks (~3 years) in 2026 (Data Center Knowledge). Generators, switchgear, and chillers commonly quote 12–24+ months.
  5. Construction (12–24 months for 40 MW; modular can compress to 6–12) — site, shell, MEP fit-out.
  6. Commissioning (2–6 months, overlapping construction turnover) — levels of testing culminating in integrated systems testing before handover. A later topic owns commissioning detail.
  7. Operations & refresh (facility life 15–20+ years; IT refreshed every 3–5 years) — day-to-day running plus mid-life MEP refresh.
  8. Decommissioning — asset retirement, equipment removal, site repurposing.

What has stretched vs compressed. Stretched: grid interconnection (now 4–7 years in constrained regions) and long-lead electrical equipment (transformers to ~3 years) — these are outside any contractor's control and now define the critical path. Per JLL's 2026 Global Data Center Outlook (published Jan 6, 2026), "despite developers preordering materials up to 24 months in advance, more than half of projects in 2025 experienced construction delays of three months or more. The average equipment lead time globally is now 33 weeks, a 50% increase from pre-2020 levels." Compressed: the build itself, via prefabrication/modular construction, repeatable reference designs, and parallel (rather than sequential) work.

2.2 What gets locked when — the influence/cost-of-change curve

The ability to influence outcomes is highest at the start and collapses as the project proceeds; the cost of change moves in the opposite direction. Decisions locked early — site and power capacity, redundancy topology (N+1 vs 2N), target rack density and cooling approach (air vs liquid), and the electrical one-line — are cheap to change on paper during concept/SD but extremely expensive later. The stakes are quantified by Uptime Institute's Annual Outage Analysis: power is the leading cause of outages (of 97 operators surveyed in 2024, "54 percent primarily attributed their woes to power," and power accounted for 45% of impactful outages in 2025), and 54% of operators said their most recent significant outage cost more than $100,000 with one in five exceeding $1 million — and many such failures trace to power/cooling/cabling decisions made during construction that are hard and costly to fix afterward. Industry reporting adds that skipping AI-readiness at design can cost $10–50 million in later retrofits (2026). The practical lesson for the reference facility: the density and cooling decision, and the amount of power secured, must be right at concept — they cascade into every downstream cost.

2.3 Who is in the room at each stage

  • Business case/site: owner/developer, capital partners, real-estate and power-acquisition teams, utility, local government/economic-development.
  • Design: owner's engineer, architect, MEP engineers, owner's representative, commissioning agent (ideally engaged pre-design to author acceptance criteria).
  • Procurement: owner's procurement team, contractor, equipment OEMs (Schneider, Vertiv, Eaton, Caterpillar/Cummins, etc.).
  • Construction: general contractor / design-build or EPC contractor, subcontractors, owner's rep, inspectors.
  • Commissioning/turnover: commissioning agent, contractor, OEM field engineers, the future operations team.

3. Who Actually Builds One: Parties and Contracting Models

3.1 The parties and their roles

  • Owner / developer — commissions, finances, and (usually) owns the facility; sets requirements and carries ultimate risk.
  • Owner's representative — the owner's project-management proxy; manages schedule, budget (construction, soft costs, owner-furnished equipment, contingency), and stakeholders.
  • Owner's engineer — technical advisor to the owner who reviews designs against the owner's standards.
  • Architect — building design, code compliance, envelope; in the AIA framework, also an administrative role certifying payment and interpreting the contract.
  • MEP engineers — mechanical, electrical, and plumbing design; in a data center this is the heart of the project (power and cooling are ~70%+ of cost).
  • General contractor (GC) — builds to a completed design under design-bid-build.
  • EPC / design-build contractor — a single entity carrying both design and construction under one contract (single point of responsibility).
  • Subcontractors — the trades (electrical, mechanical, controls, etc.) who perform most of the actual work.
  • Commissioning agent (CxA) — plans, witnesses, and signs off testing against the Owner's Project Requirements. Independence matters: the value of commissioning rests on the CxA being independent of the parties whose work is being checked; when the CxA reports to the GC rather than the owner, findings that threaten a milestone tend to get softened, defeating the purpose. A later topic owns commissioning.
  • Equipment OEMs — manufacturers of transformers, switchgear, UPS, generators, chillers/CDUs; increasingly contracted directly by the owner (owner-furnished equipment).
  • Utility — supplies grid power and executes the interconnection agreement; now often the pace-setter for the whole project.

3.2 The contracting models

Each model differs chiefly in how design and construction responsibility are packaged, and therefore in who carries which risk.

  • Design-Bid-Build (DBB): Owner contracts design (architect/MEP) and construction (GC) separately and sequentially. Owner warrants the completeness of drawings to the contractor, so the owner carries design risk; the GC carries construction cost/schedule risk against a fixed price. Cheapest unit price on paper but slowest (sequential) and most exposed to change orders and design-vs-build finger-pointing. Chosen when scope is well-defined, budget certainty matters more than speed, or public procurement requires low-bid.
  • Design-Build (DB): One entity designs and builds under a single contract. Design and construction risk sit with the design-builder, giving the owner a single point of responsibility and overlapping (fast-track) delivery. This is the workhorse of modern private data-center delivery. Chosen when speed and single-point accountability matter.
  • EPC / Turnkey: A design-build variant emphasizing a single fixed (lump-sum) price and a fully complete, "turn-the-key" facility, with the contractor absorbing most design, procurement, construction, and integration risk. The term comes from process industries — oil & gas and power generation — where the FIDIC "Silver Book" (Conditions of Contract for EPC/Turnkey Projects, 1st ed. 1999, 2nd ed. 2017) formalized owner-favorable, single-point turnkey risk transfer for privately financed plants. In data centers "EPC" is used more loosely — often meaning a design-build/turnkey package for the power/cooling plant — and rarely maps exactly to the oil-&-gas Silver Book model, because data-center owners retain more control (owner-standard designs, owner-furnished equipment) than a classic project-financed EPC would allow.
  • Construction-Manager-at-Risk (CMAR / CM-at-Risk, with GMP): The construction manager joins early to advise on design, then commits to a Guaranteed Maximum Price (GMP) — a ceiling above which the CM absorbs overruns, with savings often shared. Design risk stays with the owner's design team; cost risk above the GMP shifts to the CM. Chosen for complex, fast-track projects wanting early contractor input plus a cost ceiling. In US practice, GMP is documented via AIA A102/A133.
  • Integrated Project Delivery (IPD) / alliance contracting: Owner, designer, and builder share a single multi-party contract with pooled risk/reward. Rare in data centers but used by some sophisticated owners for highly collaborative, complex programs.
  • The hyperscaler pattern: Design-build or CMAR on owner-standard reference designs with owner-furnished long-lead equipment (owner directly procures transformers, generators, switchgear, cooling plant from preferred vendors). This hybrid lets the owner control specification, lock supply-chain slots early, and repeat a "kit of parts" across sites while pushing construction execution risk to the contractor.

3.3 Standard contract families and what they allocate

  • AIA (American Institute of Architects): The century-old US default for private building work. Core documents: A101 (owner-contractor, stipulated/lump sum), A201 (general conditions — the operative rulebook), A141 (owner-design-builder). Liquidated damages (LDs) are not pre-set in A201; the rate is drafted into A101 §4.5 (triggered by §3.3.3) and A201 §15.1.7 waives consequential damages. Contingency, escalation, and force majeure are drafted into the agreement/supplementary conditions.
  • ConsensusDocs: Launched 2007, endorsed by ~40 industry associations including AGC; drafted to be more balanced/collaborative than architect-centric AIA. Handles LDs and consequential-damages waivers with explicit fill-in provisions (e.g., ConsensusDocs 200 §6.6).
  • DBIA (Design-Build Institute of America): Standard forms purpose-built for design-build, allocating single-point design+build responsibility to the design-builder.
  • FIDIC: The international suite. Red Book (build to employer's design — owner carries design risk), Yellow Book (plant & design-build — contractor designs), Silver Book (EPC/turnkey — maximum risk transfer to contractor, fixed price/date). Widely used outside the US and on internationally financed projects.
  • NEC (New Engineering Contract): UK-origin, collaborative, option-based (e.g., target-cost Option C) contracts emphasizing proactive program/risk management; common in the UK and increasingly on international infrastructure.

Across all families, liquidated damages compensate the owner for late completion (critical when a tenant lease commitment rides on the date); force majeure excuses defined uncontrollable events; escalation clauses allocate material/equipment price inflation (heavily negotiated since 2020); and contingency (a cost buffer) sits in the GMP or contract sum. In a GMP/CMAR arrangement contingency is explicit and often shared; in lump-sum DBB/EPC it is buried in the contractor's price.

3.4 Evidence on delivery-method outcomes, and whether it transfers

The strongest quantitative evidence is the CII/Pankow "Revisiting Project Delivery Performance 1998–2018" study (Molenaar/Franz, published by DBIA, 2018), a 20-year update of the 1998 CII benchmark of 350+ projects. Its findings: - Cost: Design-build cost ~1.9% less per square foot than CMAR and ~0.3% less than DBB, with 2.4% less cost growth than CMAR and 3.8% less than DBB. - Schedule: Design-build was fastest — 3.9% less schedule growth than CMAR and 1.7% less than DBB; 13% faster construction than CMAR and 36% faster than DBB; 61% faster than CMAR and 102% faster than DBB from design through completion (the concurrency advantage).

Transferability caveat: These are cross-sector building/infrastructure studies, not data-center-specific, and the samples predate the current AI-driven supply-chain crunch. The direction — design-build/collaborative models deliver faster with tighter cost/schedule reliability — is highly relevant to speed-driven data centers, but the exact percentages should be treated as indicative, not literal, for a 2025–26 data center. Note also that an older CII sample found DBB sometimes edged DB on schedule growth, so the literature is not perfectly unanimous; the weight of evidence favors design-build for speed and reliability.

3.5 How delivery interacts with long-lead procurement (2025–26)

Because transformers (~3 years), switchgear, and generators now define the critical path, owners increasingly decouple procurement from the construction contract, buying long-lead gear directly as owner-furnished equipment (OFE) and assigning or novating it to the contractor for installation. This starts the equipment clock in parallel with (not after) design and interconnection, but it shifts coordination, delivery, storage, and integration-warranty risk back toward the owner — which the contract must explicitly allocate (schedule relief and compensation for late/defective OFE). A later topic owns procurement in depth.

4. What It Costs: Capex, Opex, $/MW, TCO

4.1 The $/MW convention — what's in and out

Cost is quoted per MW of IT (or "critical") load, and it almost always excludes the IT equipment (servers/GPUs) — those are the tenant's or the compute-owner's cost. Quotes differ because of what else is included: - Shell-and-core only (building envelope + basic infrastructure) — the narrowest number. - All-in / turnkey (shell + full electrical/mechanical fit-out ready for IT) — the broadest. - Some numbers exclude land and the powered shell (~15–20% of cost); some exclude soft costs. This is why headline figures range so widely. Always ask: per MW of what (IT load, critical, or gross)? Air-cooled or liquid? Shell-and-core or all-in? Land in or out?

4.2 Current benchmarks (2024–2026, with dates and sources)

  • Turner & Townsend Data Centre Construction Cost Index 2025–2026 (published late 2025; FX averaged Oct 2024–Oct 2025; benchmarked on an air-cooled, build-to-suit hyperscale facility of 30–50 MW IT load): cost per watt rose 5.5% in 2025 (down from 9.0% in 2024). Indicative US$/W to build: Silicon Valley $13.3, New Jersey $12.9, Portland $10.9, Atlanta $9.9, Phoenix $9.8, Columbus $9.8, Dallas among "heating" markets. Most expensive globally: Tokyo $15.2, Singapore $14.5, Zurich $14.2. Colocation/enterprise can shift the number by up to ±15%; US liquid-cooled carries a 7–10% premium over air-cooled of similar IT load.
  • JLL 2026 Global Data Center Outlook (published Jan 2026): average global construction cost rose from $7.7M/MW (2020) to $10.7M/MW (2025), forecast $11.3M/MW (2026) — a ~7% CAGR. Scope note: JLL's figures are shell-and-core only; tenant tech fit-out can add up to $25M/MW for AI.
  • Cushman & Wakefield 2026 Data Center Development Cost Guide (published Sep 2026): all-in greenfield US/Canada averages $17.6M/MW for the most modern facilities, excluding chips/GPUs, up ~21% per MW since the Q4 2024 edition. Powered land in primary US markets averaged $584,000/MW in 2026 YTD. The global pipeline is ~$2.3 trillion, of which $492 billion is under construction.
  • General 2025–26 rules of thumb (multiple trade sources): standard/air-cooled colocation and enterprise builds ~$8–12M/MW; AI-optimized/liquid-cooled $15–20M+/MW, and by some 2026 estimates $20–37M per deliverable MW all-in.

Which to trust: For a conventional air-cooled colocation build in a low-cost hot metro, the T&T Phoenix figure (~$9.8/W ≈ $9.8M/MW core) and JLL's $10.7M/MW (2025) are the most methodologically transparent and mutually corroborating; treat ~$10–12M/MW all-in as the working range. Cushman's $17.6M/MW is higher because it is all-in greenfield for the most modern (AI-capable) facilities — a different, denser product. The lesson is that the spread is mostly a scope/density difference, not a disagreement.

4.4 Cost drivers

Density (higher kW/rack → more power/cooling infrastructure per MW), redundancy level (2N roughly duplicates electrical gear vs N+1), climate (hot metros carry a cooling penalty but often cheaper power/land/tax), labor market (skilled MEP/commissioning labor is scarce), tariffs and equipment inflation (T&T survey: 47% of respondents saw 6–15% bid-price rises in the past year, 21% saw >15%), and speed premiums (compressing a schedule against fixed lead times raises crew density, adds shifts, and costs money).

4.5 Opex composition

Facility operating cost is dominated by electricity, then maintenance, then staffing, then land/lease, insurance, taxes, and water. Indicative benchmarks: - Facility cash opex ~$1.0–1.5M per MW per year (ITK Research, aggregating MCIM/industry benchmarks), of which electricity is the single largest line but "rarely above 40–45%" in temperate, well-managed sites; other models put total opex at $800–2,000/kW/year. - Electricity → dollars via PUE: annual IT energy = IT MW × 8,760 h; multiply by PUE for facility energy, then by $/kWh. Dropping PUE from 1.5 to 1.2 cuts cooling-attributable electricity ~60%, trimming the total electricity bill ~20% (ITK). - Maintenance ~ often modeled at ~3% of capex/year; insurance ~0.7% of capex/year; staffing roughly 1.5 FTE/MW for colocation down to ~0.2 FTE/MW for single-workload AI campuses (staffing benchmarks, 2026). - Water matters in hot climates using evaporative cooling (US data centers used ~17 billion gallons in 2023).

4.6 TCO as a discipline

Owners model 10–20 year total cost of ownership, discounting future opex to present value. Typical assumptions: facility depreciated/amortized over 15–30 years, electrical/mechanical major refresh at mid-life, IT refreshed every 3–5 years, and a discount rate around the owner's WACC (commonly ~8–10% for infrastructure). TCO discipline surfaces capex/opex trade-offs: e.g., a more efficient UPS costs more upfront (capex) but a 1-point efficiency gain at a 10 MW site saves ~$1.4M over 10 years (opex) (Data Center Knowledge/Vertiv); conversely, "value-engineering" cheaper cooling to save capex can raise PUE and cost far more in lifetime electricity. How tenants pay (the revenue side): colocation is priced per kW of committed power per month (wholesale) or per rack/cabinet with bundled power (retail), typically as a modified-gross lease with power passed through (metered or at a PUE-marked rate). Per CBRE's year-end 2025 data, "the national average lease rate rose by 6.5% year over year to $195.94 per kW per month, marking the fourth straight annual increase" for a 250–500 kW requirement; secondary markets like Dallas/Phoenix run lower (~$120–160/kW for larger blocks). This is why the operator's electricity is largely a pass-through, not a margin risk.

4.7 REQUIRED — Worked example: 1,000 racks, ~40 MW IT, colocation, hot US metro

Every number below is INDICATIVE, for illustration only, built from the sourced 2025–26 benchmarks above. Arithmetic is shown.

Setup / assumptions - IT load = 40 MW; 1,000 racks ⇒ average density = 40,000 kW ÷ 1,000 = 40 kW/rack (a modern, moderately dense colo/AI-capable design — note traditional colo would be 8–10 kW/rack over ~4,000 racks; the brief's 1,000-rack, 40 MW combination implies high density). - Conventional-plus, air-cooled, N+1, Tier III-class. Assume PUE = 1.4 (hot climate, air-cooled). - Location: Phoenix/Dallas — T&T Phoenix ≈ $9.8/W core; industrial power ≈ $0.07/kWh.

Step 1 — Capex (excludes tenant IT gear). - Core build: 40 MW × $9.8M/MW (T&T Phoenix) = $392M. - Colocation fit-out adjustment (+ up to ~15%) and soft costs/land → working all-in range ≈ $400–500M (≈ $10–12.5M/MW). Take midpoint $450M for the model. - (If this were a liquid-cooled AI build, add 7–10% on the T&T basis, or use the $15–20M/MW class → $600M–$800M+. We proceed with the air-cooled case.)

Step 2 — Annual electricity. - Facility power = 40 MW × 1.4 = 56 MW. - Annual energy = 56,000 kW × 8,760 h = 490,560,000 kWh ≈ 491 GWh/yr. - Electricity cost = 490,560,000 kWh × $0.07 = ≈ $34.3M/yr. (In colocation this is largely passed through to tenants.)

Step 3 — Non-power opex (indicative). - Maintenance ≈ 3% × $450M = $13.5M/yr. - Insurance ≈ 0.7% × $450M = $3.2M/yr. - Staffing ≈ 1.5 FTE/MW × 40 MW = 60 FTE × ~$130k loaded ≈ $7.8M/yr. - Property tax, water, connectivity, G&A ≈ $4–8M/yr (call it $6M). - Non-power subtotal ≈ 13.5 + 3.2 + 7.8 + 6 = ≈ $30.5M/yr (rounds within the ITK "$1.0–1.5M/MW ex-power" sanity band: 40 MW × ~$0.5–0.8M/MW ex-power). - Total facility opex ≈ $34.3M (power) + $30.5M (non-power) ≈ $65M/yr gross (≈ $1.6M/MW/yr including power — consistent with the upper ITK band for a hot, dense site).

Step 4 — 15-year TCO (undiscounted, then discounted). - Capex: $450M. - Opex: 15 × $65M = $975M. - Mid-life MEP refresh (~12% of capex, once): ≈ $54M. - Undiscounted 15-yr TCO ≈ 450 + 975 + 54 ≈ $1.48B. - If electricity is treated as a tenant pass-through (removed from operator cost): opex drops to ~$30.5M/yr → 15 × 30.5 = $458M; operator-borne 15-yr TCO ≈ 450 + 458 + 54 ≈ $0.96B. - Discounting: at an ~8% discount rate, the present value of a level $65M/yr for 15 years is ≈ $65M × 8.56 (annuity factor) ≈ $557M, so discounted 15-yr TCO ≈ 450 + 557 + (54 discounted ≈ 20) ≈ ~$1.03B (vs $1.48B undiscounted). Discounting roughly halves the weight of future opex — which is exactly why owners model it.

Step 5 — Revenue sanity check (why the build pencils). - At full lease, 40 MW × 1,000 kW × ~$160/kW/month (secondary-market wholesale) = $6.4M/month ≈ $77M/yr gross; at the primary-market ~$196/kW/month it approaches ~$94M/yr. Against ~$30–65M/yr opex, the spread services the ~$450M capex — illustrating the utility-like economics that draw REIT and infrastructure capital.

Delivery-model choice for this facility (2025–26): Design-build / turnkey with owner-furnished long-lead equipment, on a repeatable reference design. Rationale: with interconnection (4+ years) and transformers (~3 years) as the binding constraints, the owner must start the power and equipment clocks in parallel (favoring OFE) while transferring execution risk and getting a single point of responsibility and the fastest, most schedule-reliable delivery (favoring design-build, per the CII/Pankow evidence). A CMAR/GMP variant is the main alternative if the owner wants an explicit open-book cost ceiling with early contractor input.

5. The Vocabulary Later Topics Assume

  • IT load — power consumed by the computing equipment itself; the way size is quoted (our example: 40 MW).
  • Critical load — the load carried on protected, redundant, UPS/generator-backed power.
  • N — exactly enough capacity to carry the load, no spare.
  • N+1 — one spare unit beyond what the load needs (a component can fail/be serviced with cooling/power holding).
  • 2N — a fully duplicated, independent second system (no shared single point of failure).
  • White space — the IT floor where racks and servers live.
  • Gray space — the support area housing UPS, switchgear, generators, and cooling plant.
  • MEP — mechanical, electrical, and plumbing — the power and cooling systems that are ~75–80% of build cost.
  • OPR (Owner's Project Requirements) — the owner's written, measurable statement of what the facility must do; the yardstick for commissioning. (Named here; a later topic owns it.)
  • BOD (Basis of Design) — the design team's documented assumptions/decisions showing how the design meets the OPR. (Named here; owned later.)
  • Long-lead equipment — items with long manufacturing lead times (transformers ~3 yrs, switchgear, generators) that define the schedule and are often ordered early as owner-furnished equipment.
  • Commissioning — the structured process of testing and verifying systems meet the OPR before handover. (Owned by a later topic.)
  • PUE (Power Usage Effectiveness) — total facility power ÷ IT power; efficiency measure (1.0 ideal; ~1.5 industry average).
  • kW per rack — power density of a rack; the key measure separating traditional (3–10 kW) from AI (20–130 kW) deployments.

Glossary of Terms Introduced (one line each)

  • Data center — a building that reliably converts electricity into computation and heat.
  • Enterprise data center — privately owned and used by one organization for its own IT.
  • Colocation — multi-tenant facility renting space/power/cooling to customers who bring their own IT.
  • Retail vs wholesale colocation — small (per-rack, bundled power) vs large (per-kW, single-tenant halls).
  • Powered shell — building + land + utility power delivered; tenant fits out the interior.
  • Hyperscale — very large (~100 MW+) standardized cloud/platform facilities, self-built or leased.
  • Edge / micro — small facilities near users to cut latency.
  • Modular / prefabricated — factory-built components/modules assembled on site for speed.
  • AI factory / GPU cloud — dense, single-workload facilities for AI training/inference, usually liquid-cooled.
  • IT load / facility load / critical load — compute power / total power / protected redundant power.
  • MW / kW per rack — the power unit of a building / the density measure.
  • Rack / rack unit (U) — equipment cabinet / 1.75-inch height unit (42U standard).
  • White space / gray space — IT floor / support-infrastructure area.
  • PUE — total facility power ÷ IT power (efficiency ratio).
  • N / N+1 / 2N — exact capacity / one spare / fully duplicated system.
  • MEP — mechanical, electrical, plumbing systems.
  • Owner's representative / owner's engineer — the owner's PM proxy / technical advisor.
  • GC / EPC / design-builder — builds to a completed design / single-entity fixed-price turnkey / single-entity design+build.
  • DBB / DB / CMAR-GMP / IPD — the main delivery models (see §3.2).
  • OFE (owner-furnished equipment) — gear the owner buys directly and hands to the contractor to install.
  • GMP — Guaranteed Maximum Price (cost ceiling; overruns to contractor, savings often shared).
  • Liquidated damages — pre-agreed daily amount the contractor owes for late completion.
  • AIA / ConsensusDocs / DBIA / FIDIC / NEC — the standard contract families (see §3.3).
  • OPR / BOD — Owner's Project Requirements / Basis of Design.
  • Long-lead equipment — long-manufacturing-time items (transformers, switchgear, generators).
  • Commissioning / CxA — verification testing / the commissioning agent (must be independent).
  • Interconnection — the utility process/agreement to connect the site to the grid (now often the critical path).
  • Capex / opex / TCO — upfront capital cost / recurring operating cost / lifetime total cost.
  • $/MW — cost per MW of IT load (usually excludes IT gear; scope varies).

What Surprised the Researcher

  1. Power, not steel or servers, is the whole game. The binding constraint on delivery is grid interconnection (4–7 years in constrained markets like PJM) and transformer lead times (~3 years in 2026) — both entirely outside a contractor's control. The building can be finished and sit dark waiting for power. This inverts the newcomer's intuition that "construction" is the hard part.
  2. The building shell is almost an afterthought cost-wise. Electrical and mechanical systems are ~75–80% of construction cost (T&T 2025); shell-and-core is only 15–20%. A data center is best understood as a giant power-and-cooling machine that happens to have a roof.
  3. Cost-per-MW figures that look contradictory usually aren't. JLL's $10.7M/MW (2025, shell-and-core) and Cushman's $17.6M/MW (2026, all-in modern/AI) differ mostly by scope and density, not disagreement — a reminder to always pin down what's in the number.
  4. "EPC" is borrowed vocabulary. The term comes from oil, gas, and power-plant contracting (FIDIC Silver Book) and is used loosely in data centers; true single-point EPC turnkey is less common than owner-controlled design-build with owner-furnished equipment.
  5. Vacancy near zero (1.4%) with record construction is a genuinely unusual market state — supply is being absorbed as fast as it completes, and 74–80% of it is preleased before completion, which is why colocation now behaves like a pre-sold, utility-like asset class rather than speculative real estate.

Open questions / what is changing fast in 2026

  • AI cost benchmarks are not yet settled. Turner & Townsend explicitly notes "extensive global benchmark data is not yet available" for AI/liquid-cooled builds; the $20–37M/MW figures circulating are early and wide.
  • Grid-bypass and behind-the-meter power (on-site gas, nuclear SMRs, storage) are moving from fringe to mainstream as owners try to escape the interconnection queue — this could reshape both capex and the lifecycle map within a couple of years.
  • Redundancy is being rethought for AI. Some AI training facilities accept lower power-resilience than cloud (fault-tolerant at the software/cluster level), which can lower cost — cutting against the assumption that denser always means more expensive per MW.
  • Community opposition and permitting are now first-order schedule risks ($64B+ of US projects reportedly blocked/delayed since 2023), pushing developers toward power-advantaged secondary metros like Dallas and Phoenix — exactly the reference-facility geography.
