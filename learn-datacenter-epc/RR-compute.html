Topic 8 — The IT Load of a New-Build Data Center: AI/HPC Clusters as the Design Driver

The Desktop Research report for Topic 8, run 2026-09-25 from briefs/ and published as delivered: no editing, no lint. The chapter and appendix were distilled from it; where they and this report disagree, the chapter is the course's position.

Course reference chapter, drafted as of September 25, 2026. Reference facility: a 1,000-rack, ~40 MW colocation campus outside Phoenix; four-zone density profile (300 racks @ 8 kW, 300 @ 15 kW, 200 @ 40 kW, 200 @ 120 kW in AI pods); Tier III concurrently maintainable; design PUE ≤1.4; built in ~10 MW phases.

TL;DR

  • The AI pod is the tail that wags the whole building. In the reference facility the 200 AI racks are only 20% of the cabinets but ~61% of the load, and a single GB200/GB300 NVL72 rack draws 120–142 kW — roughly 13–15× the ~9 kW modal enterprise rack (about 7.5 kW on a trimmed mean) Uptime Institute reported in its 2025 survey. Everything about the IT load — floor loading at ~1,400–1,580 kg/rack, mandatory direct-to-chip liquid cooling, the 120 kW busway, synchronized multi-megawatt power transients — is set by these racks. Design for them first; the 8/15/40 kW zones are comparatively easy.
  • A training cluster is ONE coherent machine, not N independent servers. 72 GPUs behave as one accelerator over NVLink; a 30,000-GPU run spans ~416 NVL72 racks all stepping power up and down in lockstep every ~1 second. That coherence drives grid-visible swings of tens to hundreds of MW, relaxed in-rack redundancy (single-corded nodes, N+1), and a "buy the whole rack, not the GPU" procurement model where the IT refreshes on a 3–5 year cycle inside a building meant to last 15–20 years.
  • The 120 kW pod is already mid-generation on day one, and the reader should plan for that. GB300 is 132–142 kW now; Vera Rubin (VR200 NVL72) is 120–190+ kW for H2 2026; Rubin Ultra/Kyber targets ~600 kW racks on 800 VDC in 2027. The reference facility's 120 kW busway, pre-provisioned liquid manifolds, and 1,500 kg slab are the right hedges — but the owner must treat the pod as a re-configurable shell and write the lease to carry density, not a specific vendor's rack.

1. AI clusters as a design driver

1.1 Rack-scale systems shipping and announced

The defining shift of 2024–2026 is that NVIDIA (and now AMD) stopped selling servers and started selling racks. The unit of compute is a factory-integrated, liquid-cooled cabinet in which every GPU is wired to every other GPU, so the rack behaves as a single accelerator. This is why Topic 8 owns "what generates heat" down to the coolant quick-disconnect: the rack IS the server now.

1.2 Cluster sizes in racks, MW, and halls

Because 72 GPUs form one NVLink domain, the "unit of training" becomes the rack. Rough conversions using the GB200/GB300 class (72 GPUs, ~120–140 kW/rack):

  • 1,000 GPUs ≈ 14 NVL72 racks ≈ ~1.7–2 MW IT ≈ a corner of one hall.
  • 10,000 GPUs ≈ ~139 racks ≈ ~17–19 MW ≈ one to two ~10 MW halls.
  • 100,000 GPUs ≈ ~1,390 NVL72 racks ≈ ~170–195 MW ≈ a multi-hall campus. (For calibration: xAI's Colossus reached 100,000 Hopper GPUs at ~150 rack density of 64 GPUs/rack and ~250 MW after doubling to 200,000 GPUs.)

Why one coherent load, not 200 independent ones. In a traditional enterprise hall, 200 racks run uncorrelated workloads whose peaks and troughs average out — the basis of the diversified design load from Topic 2. A training cluster is the opposite: all GPUs execute bulk-synchronous-parallel (BSP) steps, hit a global synchronization barrier together, and checkpoint together. There is essentially no diversity to bank on. The design load and the actual load converge, which is why AI halls are sized much closer to nameplate than enterprise halls.

1.3 Power ramps and load transients (the rack-side cause)

This is the single most under-appreciated fact for a newcomer. During synchronous training, tens of thousands of GPUs move between compute and communication phases in lockstep, producing power swings between near-idle and near-peak:

  • Magnitude: tens of MW for a large job, hundreds of MW at gigawatt scale (Microsoft/OpenAI/NVIDIA, Power Stabilization for AI Training Datacenters, arXiv:2508.14318, which states that when aggregated across tens of thousands of synchronized GPUs these power swings can reach hundreds of megawatts and create oscillations that threaten grid stability; corroborated by SemiAnalysis and Meta).
  • Rate/period: FFT analysis of production DGX-H100 workloads in the same paper shows oscillations in the 0.2–3 Hz band — i.e., the load rises and falls roughly every ~1 second — with >50% of TDP swinging within milliseconds at the rack (Univ. of Alberta, arXiv:2502.01647). This frequency band aligns dangerously with the resonant frequencies of turbine generators and transmission lines.
  • What it demands of UPS, generators and the grid (the Topic 5 view, seen from the rack): the swing must be supplied in the time it takes to happen, so it shows up as current pulses and harmonic distortion on the facility side, breaks utility load-forecasting, and stresses transformers (driving K-rated designs). The reliability stakes are now regulatory: NERC issued a rare Level 3 "Essential Actions" Alert on May 4, 2026 (its highest-urgency tier) after 2024–2025 events in which 1,000+ MW of computational load dropped off the bulk power system in seconds, including a July 2024 Northern Virginia event where ~1.5 GW of data-center load tripped offline within seconds (NERC 2025 State of Reliability Report; Utility Dive).

Rack-side mitigations (the numbers): 1. Rack-level energy buffers. GB300 NVL72 power shelves integrate electrolytic capacitors — about half the shelf volume, 65 joules per GPU (~4.68 kJ/rack) — that charge in idle intervals and discharge on spikes; NVIDIA's Developer Blog states the peak power demand seen by the grid is reduced by 30% when training the Megatron LLM, with the shelf optimized together with LITEON Technology. Critical caveat: this is smoothing, not ride-through — it buys no UPS relief, so UPS sizing is unchanged. 2. Software power smoothing / ramp control: minimum power floors (keep GPUs ~90% of rated during all-reduce), gradual startup ramp caps, and "GPU burn" tail-off. Meta's engineers even created pytorch_no_powerplant_blowup=1 to inject dummy load. 3. GPU power capping and cluster-level peak-shaving with megawatt-hour storage.

1.4 Network fabric implications (counts only — Topic 9 owns fabric design)

  • Scale-up (NVLink): inside the rack, a passive copper backplane (GB200/GB300 uses ~2 miles/3.2 km of copper) gives all-to-all 130 TB/s — no optics, because copper inside the rack saves ~20 kW of optics power.
  • Scale-out (InfiniBand/Ethernet): one 400–800 Gb/s NIC per GPU leaves the rack — 72 ports/rack on GB300 (ConnectX-8 @ 800 Gb/s), plus separate storage NICs. A 100,000-GPU cluster therefore terminates ~100,000+ high-speed optical links.
  • Why the fabric sets placement: the scale-up domain is rack-shaped and the all-reduce traffic pattern is bandwidth-hungry, so racks that share a training job must be physically clustered to keep cable lengths and latency in budget. Fabric topology, not floor tiles, dictates row layout.

1.5 Floor loading and cost

  • Mass: ~1,360 kg base, ~1,500–1,580 kg populated, on a ~0.64 m² footprint = 2,200–2,470 kg/m² (21–24 kN/m²) — roughly 1.6–2.4× what a typical enterprise raised floor (1,000–1,500 kg/m²) or even TIA-942's recommended ~1,224 kg/m² will carry. This is why the reference facility uses a slab rated for ~1,500 kg racks; no cooling redesign fixes an under-rated floor.
  • Cost: a GB200 NVL72 rack is widely estimated at ~$3 million; GB300 estimates run higher. IT cost per MW dwarfs the building (see §6.2 and §6.3).
  • Why AI facilities relax redundancy (dated). Because the GPUs, not the facility, are the scarce asset and because software checkpointing already provides job-level resilience, AI deployments frequently accept N+1 (not 2N) and single-corded GPU nodes — NVIDIA reference guidance is a minimum of three rPDUs on separate feeds so losing one still leaves servers energized, rather than full 2N at the rack. The reference facility reflects this: Tier III concurrently maintainable, with only one optional 2N suite.

1.6 Delivery and risk

  • Who specifies the AI pod: typically the tenant or their IT vendor (NVIDIA reference architecture via an OEM — Supermicro/Dell/HPE) specifies the rack; the owner must pre-provision the shell: busway capacity, liquid manifolds, CDU space, floor loading, and fiber pathways before the tenant is known.
  • Stranded-asset risk: design too tightly around one vendor's rack (e.g., a 132 kW GB300 footprint) and a tenant who arrives with a 190 kW Rubin rack, an AMD double-wide ORW rack, or a 600 kW Kyber rack in 2027 may not fit. The hedge is to provision to a power/cooling/weight envelope, not a SKU.

2.2 What fills a rack (ordered inventory)

A liquid AI rack (GB300 NVL72 class), top to bottom: 1. Compute trays — 18 trays × (4 GPUs + 2 Grace CPUs) = 72 GPUs + 36 CPUs, each with cold plates and dripless quick-disconnects to the in-rack manifold. 2. NVLink switch trays — 9 trays forming the all-to-all copper backplane. 3. Power shelves — 8× ~33 kW shelves (each 6× 5.5 kW PSUs) feeding a 50–54 V DC busbar; GB300 shelves carry the 65 J/GPU capacitors. 4. Liquid manifold — vertical supply/return with per-tray quick-disconnects. 5. Scale-out NICs — one 800 Gb/s SuperNIC per GPU; plus BlueField DPUs and local NVMe. 6. Cable management — front-access; copper NVLink internal, fiber out.

A conventional ORv3 rack inherits the 48 V busbar + power shelf from Topic 6, replacing per-server AC cords with blind-mate DC connections — one AC-to-DC conversion for the whole rack.

2.3 Airflow discipline and physical handling

  • Blanking panels on every empty U, strict front-to-back flow, and awareness that some switches are side-breathing (need baffles) — still relevant even on liquid racks because ~10% of GB300 heat and all NIC/SSD/DIMM heat still goes to air.
  • Weight as shipped vs loaded: vendors ship rack-scale systems factory-integrated and populated, so a ~1,400–1,580 kg rack must be moved from dock to position with the load in place — special casters/pallets, ramps, and slab (not raised-floor) staging. A ~1,400 kg rack cannot ride a standard raised-floor tile.

2.4 Delivery and risk (the fit-out boundary)

  • Who buys racks: for AI, the tenant/IT vendor buys the pre-integrated rack-scale system; for general-purpose zones, the owner or tenant buys empty racks and does rack-and-stack as a trade (or buys factory-integrated racks to compress schedule).
  • The fit-out boundary sits at the rack. The owner delivers busway tap-off, A/B feeds, liquid manifold stubs and network pathways to the rack position; the IT vendor owns everything inside the rack up to the quick-disconnect. Factory integration + shipping loaded racks is now standard for AI to hit aggressive timelines — xAI's Colossus (Memphis, a former Electrolux factory) brought 100,000 NVIDIA H100 GPUs (~130–150 MW) online in 122 days (live July 22, 2024) and then doubled to 200,000 GPUs in 92 more days, a feat Jensen Huang called "superhuman."

3. Server platforms

3.1 CPU servers

  • Form factor: 1U/2U two-socket. Core counts and TDP have exploded: AMD EPYC "Turin" (Zen 5) up to 192 cores / 384 threads at 500 W TDP; Intel Xeon 6 "Granite Rapids" up to 128 P-cores (86-core SKUs most common in enterprise) and "Sierra Forest" up to 288 E-cores, also reaching 500 W. Memory is DDR5 (up to 12 channels) with CXL for memory expansion.
  • Per-server power: a two-socket general-purpose server is typically ~300–1,000 W depending on config; ~0.5–1.0 kW loaded.
  • How many fit a 15 kW rack: at ~0.5–0.7 kW/server you can rack ~20–30 servers before hitting 15 kW — i.e., power, not U-space, is the limit even in the "medium-density" zone.

3.3 Power supplies, efficiency, refresh

  • Efficiency: 80 PLUS Titanium PSUs; GB300 shelves cited at ~94.5% at full load. Dual-cording (2N at the server) doubles PSU count and cost; AI nodes often go single-corded to save it.
  • 48 V and 800 VDC shelves are the alternative to per-server AC — one conversion for the rack, less copper, less loss. 800 VDC is the 2027 megawatt-rack answer (§6.1).
  • Refresh cycles: CPU 5+ years; GPU 2–5 years and accelerating (Hopper→Blackwell→Rubin in ~30 months). This is the core tension: IT refreshes 3–6× inside one 15–20-year building life, so the facility must be built for the envelope of several GPU generations, not one.

3.4 Delivery and risk

  • Lead times/allocation (2025–26): Blackwell allocation is largely consumed by hyperscaler forward orders through 2026–27; H100 1-year rental rose ~40% (to ~$2.35/hr/GPU) by March 2026 and all new capacity through ~Aug–Sep 2026 was booked. GPUs are the gating scarce asset.
  • Warranty when facility water cools the server: a genuine open issue — the IT vendor warrants the server to the quick-disconnect; coolant chemistry, filtration and facility-water quality beyond it are the operator's responsibility, so contracts must define the coolant-quality SLA at the interface.
  • Hot-swap: front-serviceable trays; but a populated rack is a coherent machine, so servicing one tray can mean draining part of the liquid loop.

4.2 Tiers, media, and the "light in kW, heavy in kg" fact

  • Media: NVMe flash (hot) → SATA/SAS SSD → nearline HDD (warm/cold) → tape (archive).
  • W per TB / TB per RU: Solidigm 122 TB QLC SSD ≈ 0.20 W/TB and >1 PB/RU class; Seagate 30 TB HAMR HDD ≈ 0.32 W/TB; Micron 6600 ION 245 TB SSD idles <5 W, <30 W max, enabling >176 PB/rack. QLC SSDs deliver ~43–79% better TB/W than HDD.
  • Why storage racks are light in kW, heavy in kg: a rack full of HDDs is dense in mass and capacity but draws only a few kW — the opposite of an AI rack. Tiers are facility facts: the operator provisions different power/weight/cooling per storage row, and storage rows are low-density relative to compute.

4.3 What AI training needs vs enterprise

  • AI training demands a parallel/distributed file system — Lustre, IBM Spectrum Scale (GPFS), WEKA, or VAST — feeding GPUs at scale. Rules of thumb: ~4 GB/s read per GPU for data-heavy workloads; GPUDirect Storage at 40+ GB/s straight to GPU memory; a 512-GPU cluster needs ~400–600 GB/s sustained; DDN delivers ~4 TB/s to NVIDIA Eos. Checkpointing writes hundreds of GB (a 70 B model ≈ 140 GB FP16; with optimizer state ~420 GB) periodically; large clusters want 100 GB/s–1 TB/s write, though VAST's analysis of 85,000 production checkpoints found real needs often well below 1 TB/s.
  • Enterprise halls need general NAS/SAN/object at far lower bandwidth.
  • Storage-to-compute rack RATIO: an AI training cluster is compute-heavy — storage is a small fraction of racks (a few flash rows feeding hundreds of GPU racks), whereas an enterprise or cloud hall is far more storage-balanced. In the reference AI pod, expect on the order of a handful of storage racks per ~50–200 GPU racks.

4.4 Delivery and risk

  • Storage in colocation is typically tenant-owned. Data gravity — the cost and latency of moving petabytes — is the main reason tenants stay put once their data lands. The end-of-life ITAD / data-sanitization hand-off is named here only (Topic 11 owns the procedure).

5. Virtualization and orchestration at a glance

5.1 The stack and why the facility cares

  • Hypervisors/VMs (VMware, KVM) and containers/Kubernetes dominate enterprise and cloud; Slurm (SchedMD) owns HPC batch scheduling; bare metal is the norm for large AI training because it removes virtualization overhead and exposes GPU topology.
  • The typical split: Slurm owns the training cluster, Kubernetes owns everything else. Hybrid designs (NVIDIA's Slinky Slurm-on-K8s, validated at 8,000+ GPUs) are emerging.
  • Why it matters to the facility: the orchestration model sets the load shape. Bare-metal gang-scheduled training = one coherent, spiky, near-nameplate load (§1.3). Virtualized/containerized enterprise = many uncorrelated, lower loads that diversify.

5.2 Utilization and the three loads

Virtualization and scheduling raise actual utilization above the low levels of dedicated bare-metal enterprise servers (historically 10–20%), which widens the gap between nameplate and actual draw — and is exactly the basis for Topic 2's diversified design load. But AI training inverts this: a gang-scheduled job pins GPUs near 90–100% for hours, collapsing diversity. So the same building may need a diversified design load for its enterprise halls and a near-nameplate design load for its AI pods.

5.3 Software fault tolerance vs facility redundancy

  • Checkpointing, replication and availability zones let an operator buy less Tier: if a job can restart from a checkpoint and data is replicated across zones, a single facility fault is survivable in software. This is a major reason AI colos accept N+1 rather than 2N.
  • Where the logic fails: a single training job on 10,000 GPUs is one coherent job — a facility fault that drops the cluster loses the whole run back to the last checkpoint. Software resilience does not remove the need for concurrent maintainability; it changes the cost of an outage from "many tenants" to "one very expensive job."

5.4 Delivery and risk

Orchestration is run by the tenant, cloud provider, or GPU cloud (neocloud). A colocation owner typically sees only power, cooling and space telemetry — not the job scheduler — which is precisely why the lease, not the facility, must carry density: the owner commits kW/rack, cooling and weight envelopes, and the tenant commits to stay within them.

6. Where density is heading, and how IT procurement differs

6.1 Density trajectory 2026–2030

  • By segment (2026): enterprise density clusters around a ~9 kW mode (about 7.5 kW on a trimmed mean, per Uptime's 2025 survey, with the mode rising into the 10–30 kW band); high-density colo 15–30 kW; AI training 40–142 kW; inference somewhat lower but rising.
  • Roadmap: GB300 132–142 kW → VR200 NVL72 ~120–190 kW (H2 2026) → Rubin Ultra / Kyber ~600 kW on 800 VDC (2027). Schneider/NVIDIA note that 415 VAC and 54 VDC both become impractical above ~200 kW/rack and impossible near 400 kW — hence the 800 VDC transition, which moves >150% more power through the same copper and eliminates the ~200 kg/rack busbar a 1 MW 54 V rack would need.
  • Physical limits that bite: busbar current, coolant flow, floor loading and weight. The reference facility's 120 kW busway and slab are near today's practical ceiling and below the 2027 roadmap.
  • Is the 120 kW pod obsolete on day one? Effectively yes as a maximum — GB300 already exceeds it — but not as a design point: 120 kW is a defensible, coolable, buildable envelope for 2025–26 GB200/early-Rubin tenants. The failure mode is designing only for 120 kW with no headroom path.

6.3 The reference AI pod as a worked example

The pod: 200 racks @ 120 kW = 24 MW IT (61% of the campus's ~39 MW on 20% of racks). Assume GB200/GB300 NVL72-class racks.

Per-rack inventory (GB300 NVL72 class, indicative): - 72 GPUs + 36 Grace CPUs, 18 compute trays + 9 NVLink switch trays + 8 power shelves. - Ports/fiber: 72× 800 Gb/s scale-out NICs (one per GPU) = 72 fiber links out of the rack, plus storage/management links; NVLink stays on internal copper. - Weight: ~1,400–1,580 kg on ~0.64 m² → slab at ~2,200–2,470 kg/m². - Coolant at the quick-disconnects: ~130 L/min per rack (≈1.5 LPM/kW), inlet ≤45 °C, return ~55 °C, dripless UQD couplings — matching the facility's 30–45 °C water and 70–80% liquid capture. Air handles the residual ~10% (~13 kW/rack). - Busway interface: 120 kW busway with A/B feeds pre-provisioned; GB300's ~155 kW peak is why HPE advises provisioning busway to ~192 kW — a flag that a strict 120 kW busway is tight for GB300 and will constrain to GB200-class or power-capped operation.

Pod totals (200 racks): ~14,400 GPUs, ~7,200 Grace CPUs, ~24 MW IT, ~26,000 L/min aggregate coolant, ~14,400 scale-out fiber links, plus a handful of flash storage racks (WEKA/VAST/Lustre) feeding the pod at hundreds of GB/s to ~TB/s.

Phase 1 slice (~50 AI racks in Hall 1): ~6 MW IT, ~3,600 GPUs, ~1,800 CPUs, ~6,500 L/min coolant, ~3,600 fiber links. This is the "day-one" AI load on a mixed Phase-1 profile.

Load profile over a training day: near-idle at job launch → ramp (deliberately rate-limited to protect the grid) → sustained ~90–100% of ~120 kW/rack during compute → periodic ~1 Hz swings between compute and all-reduce → periodic checkpoint write stalls → tail-off ("GPU burn") at job end. Rack capacitors shave ~30% off the grid-facing peak of each swing; the facility still sees a fast-cycling, near-nameplate 24 MW block, not a diversified curve.

IT vs facility cost: a single GB200 NVL72 rack ≈ ~$3 M, so the 200-rack pod's IT hardware ≈ ~$600 M — versus the facility cost to house 24 MW at, say, ~$11–20 M/MW ≈ ~$260–480 M. The GPUs cost more than the building that holds them, and they will be replaced 3–5× over the building's life. That single ratio explains why IT procurement (allocation, lead time, refresh) dominates the economics.

6.4 Delivery and risk (the interface)

  • CDU-to-server interface ownership: the clean line is IT vendor to the quick-disconnect, facility beyond. The CDU may be brought by either party depending on lease (powered shell vs turnkey); coolant quality, filtration and the temperature/flow SLA at the manifold must be contractually pinned.
  • The tenant that changes GPU generation mid-lease: the biggest owner risk. Protect the pod by (1) leasing to a power/cooling/weight envelope not a SKU; (2) pre-provisioning busway headroom (toward ~192 kW) and manifold capacity; (3) writing refresh/return conditions and coolant SLAs into the lease; (4) keeping a path to 800 VDC/higher density in the electrical room.

8. Open questions and what is changing fast in 2026

  • Rack-kW roadmap: 120 → 142 → ~190 → ~600 kW in three years. How far, how fast, and where the physical ceiling sits is unsettled (Kyber's 600 kW target itself may have been revised at GTC 2026).
  • 800 VDC racks: production coincides with Kyber in 2027; ecosystem (Vertiv, Schneider, Eaton, silicon vendors) is standing up now. Whether it becomes the default for sub-megawatt racks is open.
  • GPU allocation: the binding constraint on the whole industry; forward-order dynamics and HBM/CoWoS supply gate everything.
  • Inference vs training density: inference (Rubin CPX, TPU 8i, Trainium) is splitting into its own lower-but-rising density class; how halls should be zoned for a training/inference mix is an active design question.
  • Disaggregated storage: NVMe-oF/composable (VAST DASE) vs traditional parallel FS — the balance is shifting toward flash-dense disaggregation.
  • AI-ready lease terms: the market has not settled on how to price and bound density, refresh rights, coolant SLAs, and stranded-asset risk.

9. Glossary (one line each)

  • NVL72 / rack-scale system: a factory-integrated liquid-cooled rack whose 72 GPUs act as one accelerator over NVLink.
  • NVLink / NVSwitch: NVIDIA's in-rack scale-up interconnect (130 TB/s all-to-all on copper).
  • Scale-up vs scale-out: scale-up = inside the rack (NVLink); scale-out = between racks (InfiniBand/Ethernet, one NIC/GPU).
  • Grace / Vera CPU: NVIDIA's Arm server CPUs paired with GPUs in a superchip.
  • HBM (High-Bandwidth Memory): stacked GPU memory (HBM3e/HBM4); the main driver of GPU power.
  • Bulk-synchronous parallel (BSP): the compute→barrier→checkpoint cycle that makes a training cluster one coherent, spiky load.
  • Power smoothing / GB300 capacitors: 65 J/GPU rack-level energy buffer that cuts grid-facing peak ~30% (smoothing, not ride-through).
  • Direct-to-chip / cold plate / quick-disconnect (UQD): liquid cooling to the chip; the UQD is Topic 8's boundary with the cooling plant.
  • EIA-310: the 19-inch rack standard (mounting width, U-height, hole pattern only).
  • ORv3 / Open Rack Wide (ORW): OCP 21-inch (and double-wide) rack with 48 V DC busbar and power shelves.
  • Power shelf / busbar: centralized AC-to-DC conversion feeding a 48–54 V (future 800 V) DC busbar for the rack.
  • Parallel file system (Lustre/GPFS/WEKA/VAST): storage that feeds many GPUs in parallel at GB/s–TB/s.
  • GPUDirect Storage: direct storage-to-GPU-memory path (40+ GB/s), bypassing the CPU.
  • Checkpointing: periodic save of model state so a training job can restart after a fault.
  • Slurm / Kubernetes / bare metal: HPC batch scheduler / container orchestrator / un-virtualized nodes for training.
  • Data gravity: the pull that keeps tenants in place because moving petabytes is costly.
  • Diversified design load: the sized load assuming uncorrelated peaks — valid for enterprise, largely void for training.
  • 800 VDC: the 2027 megawatt-rack power architecture that cuts copper and conversion losses.
  • Kyber / Oberon: NVIDIA rack architectures (Oberon = current NVL72; Kyber = 2027 ~600 kW class).

10. What surprised the researcher

  1. The building is the cheap part. For the reference AI pod, the GPUs (~$600 M) cost more than the facility that houses them (~$260–480 M) — and they're replaced 3–5× over the building's life. The mental model of "expensive building, commodity servers" is inverted for AI.
  2. A training cluster is one machine, and it fights the grid. Tens of thousands of GPUs swinging in lockstep at ~1 Hz produce hundreds-of-MW oscillations in a frequency band that can physically fatigue turbine shafts — enough that NERC issued alerts and Meta shipped a pytorch_no_powerplant_blowup flag. The load is not just big; it is dynamic in a way ordinary data centers never were.
  3. NVIDIA's own capacitors don't help your UPS. The 65 J/GPU smoothing cuts grid-facing peak ~30% but explicitly provides no ride-through — a subtle point that trips up first-time capacity planners.
  4. Copper, not silicon, forces 800 VDC. A 1 MW rack at 54 V would need ~200 kg of copper busbar; a 1 GW site, ~200,000 kg. Ohm's law, not compute, is driving the next power architecture.
  5. The 120 kW "high-density" pod is already behind the roadmap — GB300 exceeds it and Rubin Ultra targets 5× it — which reframes "future-proofing" as "build a re-configurable envelope," not "pick a number."
  6. Storage inverts the rack intuition: an AI compute rack is heavy in kW and moderate in kg; a storage rack is light in kW and heavy in kg — so a single "kg per kW" rule of thumb cannot serve both.
