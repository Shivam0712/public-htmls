Appendix: Design Requirements, Capacity and Load Planning

Sources and fine detail behind the chapter. Sections are numbered so the chapter can point at them.

The detail behind the Topic 2 chapter: the two requirements documents, who writes them and who pays when they are vague, the 2025–26 density data and the profile method, cooling thresholds by source, stranded capacity, phasing, the availability frameworks, the binding codes, the audit frameworks' physical demands, and the reference facility's arithmetic. The chapter cites sections by number (APX-requirements §n). Non-obvious claims carry their source in parentheses; where sources disagree, the text says which to lean on. Figures are indicative 2025–26 benchmarks. Topic 1's appendix covers delivery models, contract families and cost per MW; none of that is repeated.

OPR and BOD: definitions and contents

ASHRAE Guideline 0-2019, which governs the commissioning process, defines the Owner's Project Requirements as the record of the owner's objectives and criteria "from which all design, construction, acceptance, and operation decisions are made," and the Basis of Design as "a document that records the concepts, calculations, decisions, and product selections used to meet the OPR and to satisfy applicable regulatory requirements, standards, and guidelines" (ASHRAE, Guideline 0-2019). The OPR is written in pre-design, issued with the RFP and used as the benchmark for acceptance, yet is deliberately kept out of the contract documents: ASHRAE's OPR for its own headquarters "is not considered to be a 'Contract Document' to avoid the possibility of conflicting" language (ASHRAE, Headquarters Building OPR). The OPR states outcomes in the owner's words; drawings and specifications state means. Making both contract documents would turn every discrepancy into a dispute. Acceptance still runs against the OPR because the commissioning specification, which is a contract document, requires it (§2).

Who writes each, and when

The owner, or its representative or owner's engineer, writes the OPR; the design team writes the BOD, which under design-build or EPC means the design-builder's engineer of record. Under Guideline 0 the OPR originates in pre-design and a stated design-phase objective is "verifying the BOD document with the OPR document" (ASHRAE, Guideline 0-2019). AIA's programming, schematic design (SD), design development (DD) and construction documents (CD) map onto Guideline 0's pre-design, design, construction, and occupancy. The OPR is a pre-design, owner-owned document; the BOD begins at SD, is refined through DD, and commissioning requirements are folded into the CD set. Both are revisited at each phase gate under change control, because the people who close out commissioning are often not those who set the requirements (USGBC, LEED commissioning guidance; general commissioning practice).

Guideline 0 and ASHRAE/IES Standard 202, with which it is harmonised, make the OPR the acceptance yardstick: commissioning exists for "verifying that the facility and its systems meet the Owner's Project Requirements (OPR)," and functional performance tests are written to confirm OPR compliance (ASHRAE, Guideline 0-2019; Standard 202). The process itself is Topic 11. For the requirements author the consequence is that anything to be tested at handover must be a measurable sentence in the OPR. "Highly available" cannot be tested; "concurrently maintainable per Uptime Tier III, PUE ≤ 1.4 at design load, 10 MW IT per phase" can.

Who owns the gap between specified and built

Specification type and delivery model together decide who pays when the built facility diverges from intent (AIA Contract Documents, How Construction Delivery Methods Allocate Risk, 2026).

  • Design-bid-build: the owner carries design risk. "If the design contains errors or omissions, the consequences typically flow through to the contractor in the form of change orders" (AIA, 2026), which the owner pays.
  • Design-build: one entity carries both risks, but "if the scope is not well defined when the design-build contract is signed, the owner may lose cost certainty" (AIA, 2026); the builder fills gaps with the cheapest compliant reading.
  • EPC turnkey: the owner writes a performance specification and the contractor guarantees the result for a fixed price, backed by performance security of typically 5–15% of contract price and performance liquidated damages. Data center EPC guarantees are written around Uptime Tier, PUE (commonly ≤ 1.4), MW and redundancy, enforced through commissioning gates with final payment tied to integrated systems testing (research synthesis of EPC practice). Strongest for an owner who can write a precise specification; weakest for one who cannot.

Change-order evidence. The AIA's "Market Standard Range" rests on "892,457 change orders" across "18,229 completed building projects" of "8.11 to 32.88 months" average duration (AIA, The Truth About Change Orders, 2023). Average cost impact is roughly 4–5% of value; the middle 80% of projects reach up to about 15%; projects over $50M average 11.29 change orders, most in the final half, when the owner has the fewest options (AIA, 2023). Academic work found change-order overruns of 11–15% of contract value on large building projects, design changes and errors or omissions the dominant cause (Alexandria Engineering Journal, 2013). Vague requirements are deferred change orders.

Three prevention mechanisms. Performance specifications with measurable acceptance criteria (the Uptime Tier is one: Uptime "does not prescribe any specific technology, schematic or other design criteria beyond those resulting outcomes"; Uptime Institute, Tier Standard: Topology). Reference designs and owner standards under a master agreement with per-phase work authorisations, the hyperscaler approach. And locking irreversible decisions, above all the tier and the density profile, into the OPR before design begins (§9 for the retrofit economics).

Rack density in 2025–26: what the surveys measure

Density is chosen from tenant mix, the IT roadmap, the expected share of accelerated compute, and what colocation is actually leasing. The 2025–26 figures look contradictory until the populations are separated.

  • Installed base: "Average server rack power densities continue to rise slowly ... Few facilities exceed 30 kW, and extreme densities remain rare" (Uptime Institute, Global Data Center Survey 2025, over 800 operators). The 2024 edition put the modal average near 8 kW; in 2025 "the average of the modal densities in our total sample reaches almost 9 kW," and "more than 80% of the operators responding to our survey say their facility has no racks" above the high-density thresholds (Uptime Institute, 2024 and 2025).
  • Newer builds: density rose "from 7 kW/rack in 2021 to 16 kW/rack in the latest survey," and 79% expect further growth (AFCOM, State of the Data Center 2025). The tenth edition reports 27 kW, up from 16, the largest annual increase in the report's history, a "step-function change" per AFCOM's Bill Kleyman (AFCOM, State of the Data Center 2026, via Data Center Knowledge).
  • Leading edge: fully loaded NVIDIA GPU racks "require 132 kW," the next generation "240 kW per rack" (Schneider Electric, Steven Carlini); Vera Rubin is specified up to 246 kW (NVIDIA roadmap; §8).
  • Market: vacancy 1.6% and 74.3% of under-construction capacity pre-leased (CBRE Research, North America Data Center Trends H1 2025), with rent premiums for liquid-cooled space (CBRE, H2 2025). Topic 1's 1.4% is the year-end figure; different dates, not a disagreement.

Reconciliation. Uptime surveys the whole installed base, so its modal figure describes what the average existing hall runs today; AFCOM's respondents and the vendors weight newer and AI deployments, so 16–27 kW describes what new colocation is designed to. Different populations, not a contradiction. The course uses about 9 kW for the installed base and 16–27 kW for new builds, and plans a new 2026 hall to a mixed profile anchored well above the installed-base average.

The density profile method and its cascade

An "average 15 kW" hall can hold 100 kW AI pods and 5 kW storage rows at once; the average hides the design problem. Planners describe a profile in bands: low density (≤ 10 kW, air), mainstream (10–20 kW), high density (20–50 kW, containment plus rear-door exchangers), AI (50–150 kW, direct-to-chip). Schneider Electric's method builds the facility as rooms, then pods, then cabinets, each pod specified for an average and a peak per-cabinet power, with the rule that no cabinet may exceed the pod's peak because the distribution is not rated for it (Schneider Electric, White Paper 150 and power-density white papers). A colocation provider reserves dense zones by pre-provisioning busway, floor loading and liquid manifolds in specific pods, so the capability does not strand the general floor.

Density sets the power block, cooling method, floor loading, room size and cost per MW and per rack. Practitioners' rules (trade practice; verify against a specific design):

Stranded capacity and the three loads

Stranded capacity is capacity paid for but unusable because a different resource ran out first: power available but cooling at its limit, floor free but the busway full. Schneider Electric finds this endemic: operators "typically do not have the information they need," so facilities are "grossly over-designed or under-utilized," and load-balancing failures across PDUs strand power at row level (Schneider Electric, White Paper 150, Neil Rasmussen). The density profile sets the exposure: a dense band that outgrows its plan exhausts cooling or busway before floor; a hall built dense and leased light exhausts floor before power.

Design load, nameplate, actual. Nameplate is the sum of equipment ratings; design load is what the facility is sized to carry after diversity, contingency and headroom; actual is the operating draw. Each is smaller than the last. Modelling on LBNL parameters finds that applying server-level utilisation to facility capacity overstates facility load by about 9–18 percentage points against a facility-level estimate, with facility-load coefficients clustering around 0.66 (LBNL-parameter power-flow modelling, arXiv preprint, 2026). The power block is sized to a diversified design load, not the sum of nameplates; Topic 5 takes the number forward.

Oversizing, phasing, modular. Building the ultimate plant on day one strands capital until load arrives. Phasing (shell for all, MEP for some) and modular MEP blocks or prefabricated skids defer capex to match leasing at a modest premium. A common rule holds about 15–20% of white and gray space in reserve (trade practice).

Hyperscalers and colocation. Hyperscalers plan to a known, standardised workload; colocation plans for speculative flexibility. Meta open-sourced its Prineville design and founded the Open Compute Project in 2011; OCP's Open Systems for AI, with NVIDIA's GB200 NVL72 and Meta's Catalina contributions, now standardises AI rack power and liquid interfaces (Open Compute Project). Meta historically ran about 5.5 kW per rack and scaled by adding racks, the opposite of the AI-era trend (OCP and trade accounts). Colocation cannot know its tenants' hardware, so it designs a flexible profile and markets reserved zones; vendors offer "standardized IT Pods as reference designs or even prefabricated modules" (Schneider Electric, Steven Carlini). If OCP designs become common tenant hardware, colocation's flexibility penalty should shrink: a shift to watch, not yet a fact.

Phasing, future-proofing and "AI-ready"

A campus is almost never built to ultimate capacity on day one: shell, and often site power, for the ultimate campus; MEP for the first phase. Phases are sized against leasing velocity and interconnection delivery, and power availability was the top site and leasing constraint of 2025 (CBRE Research, 2025; JLL, 2026 Global Data Center Outlook), so the utility increasingly sets the phase clock. Day-one versus ultimate MW is an explicit OPR decision (section 2) that fixes how much plant to buy now.

"AI-ready." In 2025–26 the label means a bundle: liquid-cooling readiness (chilled-water loops, CDU space, secondary-loop provisions), higher per-rack power (50–120+ kW busway), higher floor loading, higher supply temperatures. WWT puts current requirements near 50 kW per rack rising to 80–120 kW, racks deeper than 1,200 mm, and fully populated AI racks near 1,500 lb (WWT, AI data center readiness glossary). The test of engineering versus marketing is whether the specification names numbers (kW per rack, lb/ft², supply temperatures, GPM per rack) or just the label.

(verify) Forward densities are projections, not facts. GB200 NVL72 at ~120–132 kW is shipping (NVIDIA); Vera Rubin is cited up to 246 kW (NVIDIA roadmap); Deloitte projects racks near 370 kW in 2026 (Deloitte, via Data Centre & Technology). Any "AI-ready" specification risks obsolescence within one hardware generation, an argument for pathways and reserve space rather than buying today's ceiling as plant.

Availability frameworks

The Uptime Tier Standard classifies by redundancy topology, not a statistic: "an objective basis for comparing the functionality, capacity, and expected availability ... of a particular site infrastructure design topology" (Uptime Institute, Tier Standard: Topology). Tier I, basic capacity: single path, no redundancy. Tier II: N+1 components, single path. Tier III, concurrently maintainable: redundant components and distribution paths, so any component can be maintained without shutting down IT. Tier IV, fault tolerant: independent, physically isolated, simultaneously active paths; any single failure is survived automatically. Certification runs in stages: Design Documents (TCDD, review of the full design set), Constructed Facility (TCCF, on-site verification of the as-built) and Operational Sustainability (TCOS, staffing, maintenance, management). Uptime is the sole licensed certifier, which is its commercial basis (Uptime Institute, Tier Certification).

The criticisms are real: cost and time; rigidity (meet every requirement of a tier or drop to the one below, so one cannot "add a generator to get to Tier III"); and self-declaration. Uptime itself notes companies that obtained a design certification then walked away, "willfully misrepresenting the Tier Certification, using a design foil to market a site that was not physically tested" (Uptime Institute). Many "Tier III" plaques are design-only or expired; confirm the specific hall and that the constructed facility was certified.

The arithmetic and the money. Annual unplanned downtime is (1 − availability) × 8,760 h: 99.671% → ~28.8 h (Tier I); 99.741% → ~22.7 h (Tier II); 99.982% → 1.58 h, ~95 min (Tier III); 99.995% → 0.438 h, ~26 min (Tier IV) (trade figures, 2025–26).

(verify) Uptime de-emphasises the percentages. They come from early Tier material and are repeated across trade sources, but Uptime stresses that real uptime depends heavily on operations, not topology alone (Uptime Institute; its Annual Outage Analysis, cited in Topic 1, finds power and human factors dominate). The course states them once, as indicative design targets, not SLAs.

Tier IV costs roughly 25–60% more than Tier III for the same IT capacity (Global Data Center Hub: 25–40%; other trade sources 40–60%), because every path doubles into separated rooms; one consultancy puts Tier III power architecture at about 2.5 times Tier II. Illustrative fit-out ranges: Tier II ~$4,000–5,600/kW, Tier III ~$7,000–10,000/kW, Tier IV ~$11,000–16,000/kW (trade sources; fit-out, not Topic 1's all-in $/MW, see §12). Break-even heuristic: Tier IV pays when downtime costs over ~$500,000 per hour and expected unplanned downtime exceeds ~1.3 h a year (trade analysis). Hyperscalers build Tier III topology and handle failures in software across availability zones, so they self-declare; colocation markets its level (CBRE quotes wholesale rates for "N+1/Tier III requirements"; CBRE Research, 2025), and Tier IV is reserved for financial, government and healthcare loads, under 5% of certified facilities (trade sources). Retrofitting upward is usually uneconomic: Tier II to III needs a second distribution path, "often impossible without major demolition," and a Tier IV retrofit can exceed 70–90% of new-build cost (trade sources). Hence the tier is locked in the OPR before design (§3).

Codes and standards

Taxonomy. A code is minimum legal rules, enforceable once a jurisdiction adopts it; NFPA 70 becomes law where a state or city adopts it. A standard is detailed good practice (BICSI 002, TIA-942-C, ASHRAE 90.4) that binds when a code adopts it by reference. A guideline is advisory (ASHRAE Guideline 0) unless a contract adopts it. A certification is third-party verification against a standard, voluntary and market-driven. The Authority Having Jurisdiction (AHJ), usually the local building or fire official, decides which editions apply and enforces them with local amendments, which is why BOD section 1 names exact editions.

The binding US codes. - NFPA 70 (NEC). Article 645, Information Technology Equipment, is optional: it offers alternative wiring methods only if every 645.4 condition is met (dedicated HVAC, room restricted to qualified personnel, fire-rated separation, a compliant disconnecting means, the Emergency Power Off or EPO). Otherwise NEC Chapters 1–4 apply. Also relevant: Articles 110 (working clearances), 250 (grounding), 408 (switchboards), 480 (batteries), 646 (modular data centers), 685. The 2026 NEC tightens 645 cabling and listing rules in alignment with NFPA 75 (NFPA, NFPA 70, current and 2026 editions). - NFPA 75 (Fire Protection of Information Technology Equipment) and 76 (Telecommunications Facilities): not referenced directly by the IBC but industry practice, cross-referenced with NEC 645. NFPA 855 (energy storage) drives UPS battery-room fire ratings and suppression as lithium-ion replaces lead-acid; NFPA 72 (alarm), 2001 (clean agent), 70E (electrical worker safety, arc flash) (NFPA, current editions). - IBC and IFC, the architectural and fire basis in most US jurisdictions, with IMC and IPC (International Code Council). Seismic and structural via IBC and ASCE 7; what the density plan asks of the structure is §12.

ASHRAE 90.4. The data-center energy standard, companion to 90.1 (which still governs envelope, lighting, service water), applying above 20 W/ft² and 10 kW IT. Compliance is two calculated ratios against maxima: the Mechanical Load Component (MLC), cooling, fan, pump and heat-rejection power over IT power, and the Electrical Loss Component (ELC), power-chain losses. The 2022 edition lowered both, replaced the design-condition MLC snapshot with an annualised MLC (8,760-hour model at 25, 50, 75 and 100% load), aligned ELC to the same part-load method and brought transformer efficiency in at realistic loading. MLC maxima vary by climate zone; ELC maxima do not, because losses do not depend on weather. 90.4 defines PUE but does not use it for compliance (ASHRAE, ANSI/ASHRAE 90.4-2022). The 2024 IECC adopts Sections 6 and 8 of 90.4-2022 by reference; the enforceable edition varies by jurisdiction (International Code Council). The reference facility's PUE ≤ 1.4 and its 90.4 compliance are different tests; the BOD must pass both.

Outside the US. IEC publishes the electrical standards; EN 50600 / ISO/IEC 22237 is the dominant design standard in Europe, covering construction, power, environmental control, cabling, security and management, with ISO/IEC 30134 energy KPIs. National codes and, in the EU, the Energy Efficiency Directive's data center reporting sit on top; EN 16798 plays the indoor-climate role of parts of ASHRAE's suite (CENELEC; ISO/IEC; European Commission).

Into the contract. Targets belong in OPR section 8 as measurable commitments ("Uptime Tier III Certification of Constructed Facility," "SOC 2 Type II ready," "LEED Gold") and flow into the contract as performance obligations. Under DBB the owner owns the risk and gaps become change orders; under DB or EPC the contractor typically guarantees the certifiable outcome, backed by performance security and liquidated damages, provided the OPR states it unambiguously (AIA, 2026; EPC practice). Audit frameworks split: the building's physical controls go to the builder; SOC 2 Type II, ISO 27001 and ENERGY STAR's twelve-month score are the operator's to earn after handover.

Reference facility worked example

The facility Topic 1 fixed: 1,000 racks of multi-tenant colocation, about 40 MW IT, hot US metro, PUE 1.4, Tier III class, design-build with owner-furnished long-lead equipment. Every number is indicative.

OPR headline. Mission: wholesale and retail colocation for cloud, enterprise and AI tenants with AI-ready zones. Capacity: about 40 MW ultimate; day-one Phase 1 about 10 MW IT (shell and site power for 40 MW, MEP for 10 MW), phased in ~10 MW increments against leasing and interconnection; Phase 1 is Hall 1 of Topic 1's four-hall campus with a mixed profile (course decision, 2026-09-24). Availability: Uptime Tier III as campus standard, with an optional 2N suite for financial and government tenants; Tier IV's 25–60% premium is not justified for general multi-tenant load (§9). Efficiency: PUE ≤ 1.4 at design load, ASHRAE 90.4-2022 MLC/ELC compliance for the hot climate zone, WUE reported. Certifications: Uptime Tier III TCDD and TCCF; SOC 2 Type II and ISO 27001 ready; PCI DSS Requirement 9 physical controls; NIST 800-53 PE controls for one federal-capable suite; LEED target. Budget: the dump's Tier III "$7–10M/MW" is fit-out only and sits inside Topic 1's $10–12M/MW all-in, which adds shell, site, soft costs and land; different scopes, not a disagreement.

The dump notes that reaching 40 MW needs the AI band to expand (150 racks at ~160 kW → 24 MW) or more dense pods, and reads the profile as "15% of racks carry ~50% of load" (16.5 / 32.15 = 51%). The adopted profile reaches the headline with 200 AI racks at a shipping density (120 kW is inside the GB200 NVL72 range) rather than 150 at a speculative 160 kW. The lesson survives: the AI band, not the rack count, drives the megawatts.

Day one versus ultimate. Shell and site built for 40 MW on day one (structure, floor loading, pathways, pipe stubs and valved taps). Phase 1 MEP of ~10 MW in Hall 1 with a mixed profile, so the first plant is sized against a diversified load across all four bands; later phases add ~10 MW blocks on pads and in electrical rooms reserved on day one. About 15–20% of white and gray space held in reserve; AI pods pre-provisioned with busway and manifolds so they do not strand the general floor.

Stranded-capacity watch. If the AI band grows faster than planned, cooling or busway in the dense pods reaches its limit before floor does (§7). Two guards sit in the requirements: the OPR's phasing triggers (section 2), which tie the next block of plant to leasing and interconnection milestones rather than the calendar, and the BOD's diversified design-load assumption (section 5), which sizes the power block below the sum of nameplates.
