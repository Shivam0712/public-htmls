OPR and BOD: definitions and contents

ASHRAE Guideline 0-2019 defines the Owner's Project Requirements (OPR) as the record of the owner's objectives and criteria "from which all design, construction, acceptance, and operation decisions are made." It defines the Basis of Design (BOD) as "a document that records the concepts, calculations, decisions, and product selections used to meet the OPR and to satisfy applicable regulatory requirements, standards, and guidelines" (ASHRAE, Guideline 0-2019). The owner writes the OPR before design, issues it with the RFP and retains it as an owner-owned planning and acceptance benchmark. The OPR need not itself be a signed contract document: ASHRAE's headquarters OPR "is not considered to be a 'Contract Document' to avoid the possibility of conflicting" language (ASHRAE, Headquarters Building OPR). Excluding the OPR does not make an unwritten outcome enforceable. Carry its measurable capacities, stated design conditions, verification methods and required certificates into the signed performance specifications and commissioning requirements. The engineer answers each outcome in the BOD with a design basis, calculations and a proposed means of verification. Acceptance then checks the installed work against the contracted tests and the owner's OPR benchmark (§2).

Who writes each, and when

The owner, its representative or owner's engineer drafts and owns the OPR in pre-design, before design choices are made. The design team develops the BOD during design; under design-build or EPC, this is the design-builder's engineer of record. A design-phase objective is "verifying the BOD document with the OPR document" (ASHRAE, Guideline 0-2019). AIA's programming, schematic design (SD), design development (DD) and construction documents (CD) map onto Guideline 0's pre-design, design, construction and occupancy. The BOD begins at SD, is refined through DD, and commissioning requirements enter the CD set. At each phase gate, the owner formally reviews and approves OPR revisions, the engineer submits BOD revisions for review against the approved OPR, and the owner approves consequential scope or acceptance changes before they enter the contract. Keep a record of decisions and unresolved items under change control, because the people closing out commissioning may not have set the original requirements (USGBC, LEED commissioning guidance).

Guideline 0 and the harmonised ASHRAE/IES Standard 202 make the OPR the acceptance yardstick: commissioning exists for "verifying that the facility and its systems meet the Owner's Project Requirements (OPR)" (ASHRAE, Guideline 0-2019; Standard 202). The commissioning process is Topic 11. Anything to be tested at handover needs a measurable OPR statement, stated conditions and a test or evidence method carried into the contract (§1). "Highly available" cannot be tested. "Concurrently maintainable per Uptime Tier III, design PUE ≤ 1.4 at stated design load, 10 MW IT per phase" provides outcomes the engineer can answer and the parties can specify how to verify.

Who owns the gap between specified and built

Specification type and delivery model together allocate the cost of a gap between intent and installed work (AIA Contract Documents, How Construction Delivery Methods Allocate Risk, 2026). Under design-bid-build, the owner holds the design contract and ordinarily bears design omissions: "if the design contains errors or omissions, the consequences typically flow through to the contractor in the form of change orders" (AIA, 2026). The builder remains responsible for building the specified work. Under design-build, one entity carries defined design and construction obligations, but "if the scope is not well defined when the design-build contract is signed, the owner may lose cost certainty" (AIA, 2026). An unspoken zone rating or test is a scope gap, not automatically a builder guarantee. Under EPC turnkey, the owner supplies a written output specification and the contractor guarantees the specified result for a fixed price, with performance security typically 5–15% of contract price, liquidated damages and final payment linked to integrated systems testing (research synthesis of EPC practice). Write guarantees around specified MW, redundancy, design PUE conditions and the required Tier certificate, not an assumed uptime percentage. Transfer works only when outputs, exclusions, tests and remedies are precise.

This course's design-build owner also furnishes long-lead equipment. The agreement must allocate its specification, purchase, delivery, storage, installation, integration and test responsibilities, and the effect of late or nonconforming deliveries on price and schedule. Identify utility service capacity, interconnection timing and the test boundary too: neither a builder's defined work nor an owner's equipment order by itself guarantees utility power. Allocate each interface rather than treating every delay as the builder's design failure (course decision).

Change-order evidence. The AIA's "Market Standard Range" rests on "892,457 change orders" across "18,229 completed building projects" of multiple building types, not a forecast for this data center (AIA, The Truth About Change Orders, 2023). Average cost impact was roughly 4–5% of value; the middle 80% of projects reached up to about 15%. Projects over $50M averaged 11.29 change orders, most in the final half, when the owner had fewer options. Academic work puts change-order overruns at 11–15% of contract value for large building projects, with design changes and errors or omissions the dominant cause (Alexandria Engineering Journal, 2013). Vague requirements can become late changes, but neither sample predicts this campus's cost.

Three prevention mechanisms. Use performance specifications with measurable acceptance criteria. Uptime "does not prescribe any specific technology, schematic or other design criteria beyond those resulting outcomes" (Uptime Institute, Tier Standard: Topology). Use reference designs under a master agreement with per-phase work authorisations. Lock the tier and density profile into the owner-approved OPR before design begins, because both are expensive to change later (§9); then incorporate the deliverables and tests into the signed agreement (§1).

Rack density in 2025–26: what the surveys measure

Choose density from tenant mix, the IT roadmap, expected accelerated compute and what colocation tenants are leasing. These figures describe different samples and dates, not one universal rack requirement.

  • Installed facilities: The Uptime Institute's Global Data Center Survey 2025 surveyed more than 800 operators in April–May 2025. It reports that "Few facilities exceed 30 kW, and extreme densities remain rare." Its 2024 modal average was near 8 kW; the average of modal densities in its 2025 total sample was "almost 9 kW." More than 80% of responding operators reported no racks above the survey's high-density thresholds (Uptime Institute, 2024 and Global Data Center Survey 2025). This is a survey of existing operations, not a design specification for a new hall.
  • Industry-association respondents: AFCOM's State of the Data Center 2025 reported survey rack density rising from 7 kW/rack in 2021 to 16 kW/rack in its 2025 survey, and 79% of respondents expected further growth. The 2026, tenth-edition survey reported an average of 27 kW/rack among its respondents, up from 16 kW the prior year, the largest annual increase in that series, called a "step-function change" by AFCOM's Bill Kleyman (AFCOM, State of the Data Center 2025; AFCOM, 2026, via Data Center Knowledge). These respondent averages are not a measured average for every new build or a requirement for every new rack.
  • Leading edge: Fully loaded NVIDIA GPU racks "require 132 kW" and a next generation "240 kW per rack" (Schneider Electric, Steven Carlini); Vera Rubin is cited up to 246 kW as a roadmap figure (NVIDIA roadmap; §8). These are hardware cases, not fleet averages.
  • Market: Vacancy of 1.6%, 74.3% of under-construction capacity pre-leased and rent premiums for liquid-cooled space describe the North American colocation market, not rack density (CBRE Research, North America Data Center Trends H1 and H2 2025).

Reconciliation. Uptime's existing-operator sample and AFCOM's respondent series do not have interchangeable denominators. The research uses Uptime's near-9 kW modal figure to describe the installed base and AFCOM's 16 and 27 kW figures as evidence of rising density in its industry surveys, with newer and AI deployments helping explain the difference. Neither sets this campus's design. Its owner-approved 300/300/200/200 profile does (§12).

The density profile method and its cascade

An "average 15 kW" hall can contain 100 kW AI pods and 5 kW storage rows at once; the average hides the local design problem. Planning bands include low density (≤10 kW, air), mainstream (10–20 kW), high density (20–50 kW, containment or rear-door exchangers) and AI (50–150 kW, often direct-to-chip). Schneider Electric builds its capacity model from rooms to pods to cabinets. Each pod has a planned average and a peak per-cabinet rating that no cabinet may exceed without checking the distribution (Schneider Electric, White Paper 150). A rating is provisioned capability, not a prediction that each occupied rack draws it continuously.

For this course, fix 300 racks at 8 kW, 300 at 15 kW, 200 at 40 kW and 200 AI racks at 120 kW. Their rated zone sums are 2.4, 4.5, 8 and 24 MW, respectively, or 38.9 MW for the 1,000 planned positions (§12). Specify the pod-level average, maximum rack rating and diversity assumptions separately. Actual draw changes with occupancy and workload (§7). Put 120 kW-class electrical feeds, liquid manifolds and CDU space in the AI pods, not in every rack row; route cooling and electrical distribution to the 40 kW zone separately from the contained-air 8 and 15 kW zones. Check concentrated rack loads and equipment paths with the structural engineer, including convertible space. Reserve 15–20% of white and gray space for reconfiguration rather than paying for high-density plant across the whole floor (course decision).

Stranded capacity and the three loads

Stranded capacity is capacity paid for but unusable because another resource runs out first: power available but cooling at its limit, or floor space free while its electrical feed or busway is full. Schneider Electric finds that operators "typically do not have the information they need," leaving facilities "grossly over-designed or under-utilized"; PDU load imbalance can strand power at row level (Schneider Electric, White Paper 150, Neil Rasmussen).

Nameplate, design load and actual draw answer different questions. Nameplate sum adds equipment ratings. Diversified design load is the engineered capacity basis after stated diversity, contingency and growth assumptions. Actual draw varies with occupancy, workload and time; it need not have an invariant position below every other figure at every instant. Record the assumptions and operating limits rather than asserting a fixed ordering. LBNL-parameter power-flow modelling finds that applying server-level utilisation to facility capacity can overstate facility load by about 9–18 percentage points, with illustrative facility-load coefficients near 0.66 (LBNL-parameter power-flow modelling, arXiv preprint, 2026). Size the power block to its justified diversified design case, not an unexplained sum of nameplates (Topic 5).

Oversizing, phasing, modular. Building the ultimate plant on day one strands capital until load arrives. Phasing, shell for all and MEP for some, and modular or prefabricated MEP blocks defer capex toward leasing and utility delivery at a modest premium. A common planning reserve is about 15–20% of white and gray space (trade practice). Reserve space alone does not create a usable rack when its feed is full.

Hyperscalers and colocation. Hyperscalers can plan around a known, standardised workload; colocation plans for tenants it does not yet know. Meta founded the Open Compute Project in 2011 on its open-sourced Prineville design, historically ran about 5.5 kW per rack and scaled by adding racks. OCP's Open Systems for AI, including NVIDIA GB200 NVL72 and Meta Catalina contributions, now standardises AI rack power and liquid interfaces (Open Compute Project). Colocation markets flexible, reserved zones; vendors offer "standardized IT Pods as reference designs or even prefabricated modules" (Schneider Electric, Steven Carlini). Common tenant interfaces could reduce that flexibility penalty.

Phasing, future-proofing and "AI-ready"

A large campus rarely receives its ultimate plant on day one. For this course's four-hall campus, the $180–230 million first phase includes shared site and shell work for the eventual approximately 40 MW IT campus, as well as the first approximately 10 MW MEP block. Later roughly 10 MW blocks of transformers, switchgear, UPS, generators and chillers wait for signed demand and available utility power (course decision). Leasing velocity and interconnection delivery both matter; power availability was a leading site and leasing constraint in 2025 (CBRE Research, 2025; JLL, 2026 Global Data Center Outlook). The cutaway's "Slab for 40 MW" is shorthand for ultimate campus IT capacity, not a structural floor rating or a claim that one hall or one slab carries 40 MW.

Passive provisions belong early: structural headroom checked for the proposed rack footprints and point loads, taller floor-to-floor heights, pipe stubs and valved taps for future liquid cooling, spare conduit and busway pathways, and reserved plant and electrical-room space. Expensive, technology-dependent equipment can be installed in blocks. These distinctions explain why a prepared shell need not contain four phases of operating plant.

"AI-ready." The label can mean chilled-water and secondary-loop provisions, CDU space, 50–120+ kW busway, greater floor-loading allowance and specified supply conditions. WWT describes requirements around 50 kW per rack rising to 80–120 kW and racks deeper than 1,200 mm; its illustrative fully populated AI rack is near 1,500 lb (WWT, AI data center readiness glossary). The chapter picture's 1,500 kg is a separate, illustrative heavy-rack equipment allowance to check with the structural engineer, not WWT's pound figure, a typical weight or a floor rating. A GB200 NVL72 is cited at about 1,360 kg dry and about 1,500 kg with coolant (NVIDIA; see APX-building §4). Specify rack mass, footprint, point loads, kW per rack, cooling supply temperatures and flow, rather than relying on the label.

Forward densities are projections, not specifications for this build. GB200 NVL72 is cited at ~120–132 kW (NVIDIA); Vera Rubin up to 246 kW is a roadmap figure (NVIDIA roadmap), and Deloitte projects racks near 370 kW in 2026 (Deloitte, via Data Centre & Technology). Changing hardware is a reason to reserve paths and space, not to purchase every possible future plant capacity now.

Availability frameworks

The Uptime Tier Standard classifies site-infrastructure topology, not a guaranteed uptime statistic: it provides "an objective basis for comparing the functionality, capacity, and expected availability ... of a particular site infrastructure design topology" (Uptime Institute, Tier Standard: Topology). Tier III means covered components can be maintained without shutting down IT, using redundant components and distribution paths. Tier IV adds survival of a covered single failure through isolated, active paths. A drawing with an active and standby route is one Tier III illustration, not a prescribed layout or a promise that every Tier III fault is harmless.

Certification proceeds through Design Documents (TCDD), Constructed Facility (TCCF, on-site verification of the as-built) and a separate Operational Sustainability track (TCOS); Uptime is the sole licensed Tier certifier (Uptime Institute, Tier Certification). A design review does not certify the built hall. Confirm a TCCF certificate covers the particular hall being sold: Uptime notes firms that took design certification and then "willfully misrepresenting the Tier Certification, using a design foil to market a site that was not physically tested" (Uptime Institute). Certification cost, all-or-nothing tier requirements and self-declaration are practical concerns.

The arithmetic. Applying (1 − quoted availability) × 8,760 h gives 99.671% → ~28.8 h for Tier I, 99.741% → ~22.7 h for Tier II, 99.982% → ~1.58 h or 95 min for Tier III, and 99.995% → ~0.438 h or 26 min for Tier IV (trade figures, 2025–26). These older, widely repeated percentages are indicative arithmetic, not certified SLAs. Uptime de-emphasises them because real uptime also depends on operations (Uptime Institute; Annual Outage Analysis, cited in Topic 1).

The money. An indicative Tier IV premium is roughly 25–60% over Tier III for the same IT capacity (Global Data Center Hub: 25–40%; other trade sources: 40–60%). Illustrative fit-out ranges are Tier III ~$7,000–10,000/kW and Tier IV ~$11,000–16,000/kW, not this course's all-in campus budget (trade sources; §12). A trade break-even heuristic puts Tier IV's case above ~$500,000 per downtime hour and ~1.3 h expected annual unplanned downtime; neither figure predicts this site's losses (trade analysis). Hyperscalers may handle failures across software availability zones without certification, whereas colocation markets a verified level (CBRE quotes wholesale rates for "N+1/Tier III requirements"; CBRE Research, 2025). Tier IV is under 5% of certified facilities (trade sources). Retrofitting Tier II to III can require a second distribution path, "often impossible without major demolition"; a Tier IV retrofit can exceed 70–90% of new-build cost (trade sources). Decide the required tier before design (§3).

Codes and standards

Taxonomy. A code supplies minimum legal rules once a jurisdiction adopts it. A standard supplies technical requirements and can bind through an adopted code's reference or a contract. A guideline is advisory unless adopted by the owner or contract. A certification is third-party verification against stated criteria. The Authority Having Jurisdiction (AHJ), usually a local building or fire official, enforces locally adopted editions and amendments. The BOD must list those editions and the applicable references, not assume that the newest published edition governs (BICSI 002; TIA-942-C; ASHRAE Guideline 0; International Code Council).

US code basis. NFPA 70, the National Electrical Code (NEC), is the core electrical code where adopted. Article 645 for information technology equipment is optional: its alternative wiring methods apply only when every condition of the locally adopted edition's 645.4 is met, including dedicated HVAC, restricted qualified-personnel access, fire-rated separation and a compliant disconnecting means or Emergency Power Off (EPO). Otherwise ordinary NEC Chapters 1–4 apply. The published 2026 NEC tightens Article 645 cabling and listing rules in alignment with NFPA 75, but publication alone does not make it local law (NFPA, NFPA 70, current and 2026 editions). NFPA 75 and 76 address IT and telecommunications fire protection; NFPA 855 addresses energy storage and affects UPS battery-room fire protection. NFPA 72, 2001 and 70E address alarms, clean agents and electrical worker safety (NFPA). Locally adopted IBC and IFC provide building and fire bases, with IMC and IPC for mechanical and plumbing; IBC and ASCE 7 inform structural and seismic design (International Code Council; ASCE).

ASHRAE 90.4 versus owner PUE. ASHRAE 90.4 is the data-center energy standard; 90.1 still covers envelope, lighting and service water. For its stated applicability above 20 W/ft² and 10 kW IT, 90.4 compliance checks two calculated components against edition-specific limits: the Mechanical Load Component (MLC), covering cooling, fan, pump and heat-rejection power relative to IT power, and the Electrical Loss Component (ELC), covering power-chain losses. The 2022 edition lowered limits, uses an annualised 8,760-hour MLC model at 25, 50, 75 and 100% load rather than a design-condition snapshot, and aligns ELC with part-load calculation. MLC maxima vary by climate zone; ELC maxima do not. The standard defines PUE but does not use the owner's PUE ≤ 1.4 design target as its compliance test (ASHRAE, ANSI/ASHRAE 90.4-2022). The published 2024 IECC references Sections 6 and 8 of 90.4-2022, subject to local adoption (International Code Council). The BOD must show both the contracted PUE design case and compliance with the edition actually applicable at the site.

Outside the US. IEC publishes electrical standards. EN 50600 / ISO/IEC 22237 is a European and international data-center design family, alongside ISO/IEC 30134 energy KPIs and EU Energy Efficiency Directive reporting; national rules still determine what binds (CENELEC; ISO/IEC; European Commission).

Reference facility worked example

The course facility is a 1,000-rack, multi-tenant colocation campus outside a hot US metro, with approximately 40 MW ultimate IT capacity, a design PUE target of 1.4 and a Tier III campus standard. It uses design-build with owner-furnished long-lead equipment. All figures are indicative teaching numbers (course decision).

OPR headline. Wholesale and retail colocation with named AI zones; roughly 10 MW IT in Phase 1, then roughly 10 MW plant blocks subject to leasing and utility delivery. The $180–230 million first phase funds its plant and shared site and shell work for all four halls. Full campus construction is $400–500 million excluding IT equipment. The operating budget is about $65 million per year, including about $34 million per year for electricity; it models operation, not continuous draw at design maximum (course decision). Specify Uptime Tier III maintainability and, if marketed as certified, TCDD and TCCF for the named hall; an optional separately specified 2N suite can serve particular tenants (§9). Specify PUE ≤1.4 at stated design-load conditions, compliance with the locally applicable ASHRAE 90.4 edition, WUE reporting, SOC 2 Type II and ISO 27001 readiness, in-scope PCI DSS Requirement 9 controls, NIST 800-53 PE controls for a federal-capable suite and a LEED target (§§10–11). The indicative Tier III $7–10M/MW fit-out range is not the course's all-in construction budget (trade sources; §9).

Arithmetic: 300 × 8 + 300 × 15 + 200 × 40 + 200 × 120 = 2,400 + 4,500 + 8,000 + 24,000 = 38,900 kW, or 38.9 MW, rounded to the course's approximately 40 MW planning headline. The 200 AI racks carry 24/38.9 ≈ 62% of planned IT capacity; both dense bands carry 32/38.9 ≈ 82%. The unrounded design-PUE calculation is 38.9 × 1.4 = 54.46 MW, about 54.5 MW total site power at the stated design condition. Using the rounded campus headline gives 40 × 1.4 = 56 MW. Neither number is a forecast of constant draw or annual electricity consumption. Racks per MW range from 125 at 8 kW to about 8.3 at 120 kW, a fifteen-fold spread.

Cascade checks. At about 20 ft² per air-cooled rack, 600 racks need ~12,000 ft². At ~12–15 ft² per dense or AI rack, 400 need ~4,800–6,000 ft². That gives ~17,000–18,000 ft² of indicative white space, with substantial gray space, approximately 1:1 in this course's planning example (Topic 1). The pictured 1,500 kg AI-rack allowance requires footprint and point-load checks, including where a pod might move; it is not a floor rating or WWT's 1,500 lb example (§8; APX-building §4). Contained air serves the 8 and 15 kW bands, rear-door or in-row cooling the 40 kW band, and direct-to-chip liquid the 120 kW band, subject to site-specific checks (§6). Reserve 120 kW-class busway, manifolds and CDU space for the AI pods (Topics 6 and 7).

Day one versus ultimate. Provide the four-hall site's structure, floor-loading provisions, pathways, pipe stubs and valved taps early. Install only the first roughly 10 MW of MEP plant in Hall 1; reserve pads and rooms for later roughly 10 MW blocks and hold 15–20% of white and gray space for reconfiguration. The first block's diversified design-load basis and mix must be documented rather than assuming all rated positions draw at once (Topic 5). If AI occupancy outruns the plan, a dense pod's cooling or busway may fill while floor remains free (§7). Tie later plant to leasing and interconnection triggers, and check the feed and cooling capacity of each saleable zone before offering its rack positions.
