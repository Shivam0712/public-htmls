Appendix: Design Requirements, Capacity and Load Planning

Sources and fine detail behind the chapter. Sections are numbered so the chapter can point at them.

The detail behind the Topic 2 chapter: the two requirements documents, who pays when they are vague, the 2025–26 density data, cooling thresholds, stranded capacity, phasing, availability frameworks, codes, audit frameworks and the reference facility's arithmetic. The chapter cites sections by number (APX-requirements §n). Non-obvious claims carry their source in parentheses; where sources disagree, the text says which to lean on. Figures are indicative 2025–26 benchmarks. Delivery models, contract families and cost per MW are in Topic 1's appendix and not repeated.

OPR and BOD: definitions and contents

ASHRAE Guideline 0-2019, which governs the commissioning process, defines the Owner's Project Requirements as the record of the owner's objectives and criteria "from which all design, construction, acceptance, and operation decisions are made," and the Basis of Design as "a document that records the concepts, calculations, decisions, and product selections used to meet the OPR and to satisfy applicable regulatory requirements, standards, and guidelines" (ASHRAE, Guideline 0-2019). The OPR is written in pre-design, issued with the RFP and used as the benchmark for acceptance, yet is kept out of the contract documents: ASHRAE's OPR for its own headquarters "is not considered to be a 'Contract Document' to avoid the possibility of conflicting" language (ASHRAE, Headquarters Building OPR). ASHRAE's stated reason is to keep the owner's outcome language from conflicting with the contract's means language; acceptance still runs against the OPR through the commissioning specification (§2).

Who writes each, and when

The owner, or its representative or owner's engineer, writes the OPR; the design team writes the BOD, under design-build or EPC the design-builder's engineer of record. The OPR originates in pre-design and a design-phase objective is "verifying the BOD document with the OPR document" (ASHRAE, Guideline 0-2019). AIA's programming, schematic design (SD), design development (DD) and construction documents (CD) map onto Guideline 0's pre-design, design, construction and occupancy: the BOD begins at SD, is refined through DD, and commissioning requirements are folded into the CD set. Both are revisited at each phase gate under change control, because the people who close out commissioning are often not those who set the requirements (USGBC, LEED commissioning guidance).

Guideline 0 and ASHRAE/IES Standard 202, with which it is harmonised, make the OPR the acceptance yardstick: commissioning exists for "verifying that the facility and its systems meet the Owner's Project Requirements (OPR)" (ASHRAE, Guideline 0-2019; Standard 202); the process is Topic 11. So anything to be tested at handover must be a measurable sentence in the OPR. "Highly available" cannot be tested; "concurrently maintainable per Uptime Tier III, PUE ≤ 1.4 at design load, 10 MW IT per phase" can.

Who owns the gap between specified and built

Specification type and delivery model together decide who pays when the built facility diverges from intent (AIA Contract Documents, How Construction Delivery Methods Allocate Risk, 2026). Under design-bid-build the owner carries design risk: "if the design contains errors or omissions, the consequences typically flow through to the contractor in the form of change orders" (AIA, 2026). Under design-build one entity carries both risks, but "if the scope is not well defined when the design-build contract is signed, the owner may lose cost certainty" (AIA, 2026). Under EPC turnkey the owner writes a performance specification and the contractor guarantees the result for a fixed price, backed by performance security of typically 5–15% of contract price and liquidated damages, with guarantees written around Uptime Tier, PUE, MW and redundancy and final payment tied to integrated systems testing (research synthesis of EPC practice). The transfer works only for an owner who can write a precise specification.

Change-order evidence. The AIA's "Market Standard Range" rests on "892,457 change orders" across "18,229 completed building projects" (AIA, The Truth About Change Orders, 2023): average cost impact roughly 4–5% of value, the middle 80% of projects up to about 15%, and projects over $50M averaging 11.29 change orders, most in the final half, when the owner has the fewest options. Academic work puts change-order overruns at 11–15% of contract value, design changes and errors or omissions the dominant cause (Alexandria Engineering Journal, 2013). Vague requirements are deferred change orders.

Three prevention mechanisms. Performance specifications with measurable acceptance criteria (the Uptime Tier is one: Uptime "does not prescribe any specific technology, schematic or other design criteria beyond those resulting outcomes"; Uptime Institute, Tier Standard: Topology); reference designs under a master agreement with per-phase work authorisations; and locking the tier and the density profile into the OPR before design begins, because both are extremely expensive to change later (§9).

Rack density in 2025–26: what the surveys measure

Density is chosen from tenant mix, the IT roadmap, the expected share of accelerated compute, and what colocation is actually leasing. The 2025–26 figures look contradictory until the populations are separated.

  • Installed base: "Few facilities exceed 30 kW, and extreme densities remain rare" (Uptime Institute, Global Data Center Survey 2025, over 800 operators). The modal average was near 8 kW in 2024 and "almost 9 kW" in 2025, and more than 80% of operators report no racks above the high-density thresholds (Uptime Institute, 2024 and 2025).
  • Newer builds: density rose "from 7 kW/rack in 2021 to 16 kW/rack in the latest survey" (AFCOM, State of the Data Center 2025); the 2026 edition reports 27 kW, the largest annual increase in the report's history, a "step-function change" per AFCOM's Bill Kleyman (AFCOM, 2026, via Data Center Knowledge).
  • Leading edge: fully loaded NVIDIA GPU racks "require 132 kW," the next generation "240 kW per rack" (Schneider Electric, Steven Carlini); Vera Rubin up to 246 kW (NVIDIA roadmap; §8).
  • Market: vacancy 1.6%, 74.3% of under-construction capacity pre-leased, rent premiums for liquid-cooled space (CBRE Research, North America Data Center Trends H1 and H2 2025).

Reconciliation. Uptime surveys the whole installed base, so its modal figure is what the average existing hall runs today; AFCOM's respondents and the vendors weight newer and AI deployments, so 16–27 kW is what new colocation is designed to. Different populations, not a contradiction. The course uses about 9 kW for the installed base and 16–27 kW for new builds, and anchors a new 2026 hall's mixed profile well above the installed-base average.

The density profile method and its cascade

An "average 15 kW" hall can hold 100 kW AI pods and 5 kW storage rows at once; the average hides the design problem. Planners describe a profile in bands: low density (≤ 10 kW, air), mainstream (10–20 kW), high density (20–50 kW, containment plus rear-door exchangers), AI (50–150 kW, direct-to-chip). Schneider Electric builds the facility as rooms, then pods, then cabinets, each pod specified for an average and a peak per-cabinet power that no cabinet may exceed because the distribution is not rated for it (Schneider Electric, White Paper 150). Dense zones are reserved by pre-provisioning busway, floor loading and liquid manifolds in specific pods, so the capability does not strand the general floor. The reference facility's 40 kW band sits inside every RDHx range and its 120 kW band above every air ceiling (§12).

Stranded capacity and the three loads

Stranded capacity is capacity paid for but unusable because a different resource ran out first: power available but cooling at its limit, floor free but the busway full. Schneider Electric finds this endemic: operators "typically do not have the information they need," so facilities are "grossly over-designed or under-utilized," and PDU load imbalance strands power at row level (Schneider Electric, White Paper 150, Neil Rasmussen).

Design load, nameplate, actual. Nameplate is the sum of equipment ratings; design load is what the facility is sized to carry after diversity, contingency and headroom; actual is the operating draw; each is smaller than the last. Modelling on LBNL parameters finds that applying server-level utilisation to facility capacity overstates facility load by about 9–18 percentage points, with facility-load coefficients near 0.66 (LBNL-parameter power-flow modelling, arXiv preprint, 2026). The power block is sized to a diversified design load, not the sum of nameplates (Topic 5).

Oversizing, phasing, modular. Building the ultimate plant on day one strands capital until load arrives; phasing (shell for all, MEP for some) and modular or prefabricated MEP blocks defer capex to match leasing at a modest premium, and a common rule holds about 15–20% of white and gray space in reserve (trade practice).

Hyperscalers and colocation. Hyperscalers plan to a known, standardised workload; colocation plans for speculative flexibility. Meta founded the Open Compute Project in 2011 on its open-sourced Prineville design, historically ran about 5.5 kW per rack and scaled by adding racks; OCP's Open Systems for AI (NVIDIA GB200 NVL72, Meta Catalina) now standardises AI rack power and liquid interfaces (Open Compute Project). Colocation cannot know its tenants' hardware, so it designs a flexible profile and markets reserved zones; vendors offer "standardized IT Pods as reference designs or even prefabricated modules" (Schneider Electric, Steven Carlini). If OCP designs become common tenant hardware, that flexibility penalty should shrink.

Phasing, future-proofing and "AI-ready"

A campus is almost never built to ultimate capacity on day one: shell, and often site power, for the ultimate campus; MEP for the first phase. Phases are sized against leasing velocity and interconnection delivery, and power availability was the top site and leasing constraint of 2025 (CBRE Research, 2025; JLL, 2026 Global Data Center Outlook), so the utility increasingly sets the phase clock.

Cheap to future-proof on day one, and passive: structural floor-loading headroom, taller floor-to-floor heights, pipe stubs and valved taps for liquid cooling, spare conduit and busway pathways, space for future plant and electrical rooms. Expensive, technology-dependent and deferred or modularised: transformers, switchgear, UPS, chillers, generators. The first list costs little at pour time and is ruinous to add to a live hall.

"AI-ready." The label means a bundle: liquid-cooling readiness (chilled-water loops, CDU space, secondary loops), 50–120+ kW busway, higher floor loading, higher supply temperatures. WWT puts current requirements near 50 kW per rack rising to 80–120 kW, racks deeper than 1,200 mm, and fully populated AI racks near 1,500 lb (WWT, AI data center readiness glossary). The test is whether the specification names numbers (kW per rack, lb/ft², supply temperatures, GPM per rack) or just the label.

(verify) Forward densities are projections, not facts. GB200 NVL72 at ~120–132 kW is shipping (NVIDIA); Vera Rubin is cited up to 246 kW (NVIDIA roadmap); Deloitte projects racks near 370 kW in 2026 (Deloitte, via Data Centre & Technology). Any "AI-ready" specification risks obsolescence within one hardware generation, an argument for pathways and reserve space rather than buying today's ceiling as plant.

Availability frameworks

The Uptime Tier Standard classifies by redundancy topology, not a statistic: "an objective basis for comparing the functionality, capacity, and expected availability ... of a particular site infrastructure design topology" (Uptime Institute, Tier Standard: Topology). The four tiers are progressive (table). Certification runs in stages: Design Documents (TCDD), Constructed Facility (TCCF, on-site verification of the as-built) and Operational Sustainability (TCOS); Uptime is the sole licensed certifier (Uptime Institute, Tier Certification). The criticisms are cost, rigidity (meet every requirement of a tier or drop to the one below) and self-declaration: Uptime itself notes companies that obtained a design certification then walked away, "willfully misrepresenting the Tier Certification, using a design foil to market a site that was not physically tested" (Uptime Institute). Confirm the specific hall and that the constructed facility was certified.

The arithmetic. Annual unplanned downtime is (1 − availability) × 8,760 h: 99.671% → ~28.8 h (Tier I); 99.741% → ~22.7 h (Tier II); 99.982% → 1.58 h, ~95 min (Tier III); 99.995% → 0.438 h, ~26 min (Tier IV) (trade figures, 2025–26).

(verify) Uptime de-emphasises the percentages. They come from early Tier material and are repeated across trade sources, but Uptime stresses that real uptime depends heavily on operations, not topology alone (Uptime Institute; its Annual Outage Analysis, cited in Topic 1, finds power and human factors dominate). The course states them once, as indicative design targets, not SLAs.

The money. Tier IV costs roughly 25–60% more than Tier III for the same IT capacity (Global Data Center Hub: 25–40%; other trade sources 40–60%), because every path doubles into separated rooms. Illustrative fit-out ranges: Tier III ~$7,000–10,000/kW, Tier IV ~$11,000–16,000/kW (trade sources; fit-out, not Topic 1's all-in $/MW, §12). Break-even: Tier IV pays when downtime costs over ~$500,000 per hour and expected unplanned downtime exceeds ~1.3 h a year (trade analysis). Hyperscalers build Tier III topology, handle failures in software across availability zones and self-declare; colocation markets its level (CBRE quotes wholesale rates for "N+1/Tier III requirements"; CBRE Research, 2025); Tier IV is under 5% of certified facilities (trade sources). Retrofitting upward is usually uneconomic: Tier II to III needs a second distribution path, "often impossible without major demolition," and a Tier IV retrofit can exceed 70–90% of new-build cost (trade sources). Hence the tier is locked before design (§3).

Codes and standards

Taxonomy. A code is minimum legal rules, enforceable once a jurisdiction adopts it. A standard is detailed good practice (BICSI 002, TIA-942-C, ASHRAE 90.4) that binds when a code adopts it by reference. A guideline is advisory (ASHRAE Guideline 0) unless a contract adopts it. A certification is voluntary third-party verification against a standard. The Authority Having Jurisdiction (AHJ), usually the local building or fire official, picks and enforces the editions, with local amendments; hence BOD section 1 names exact editions.

The binding US codes. NFPA 70, the NEC, is the core. Article 645, Information Technology Equipment, is optional: alternative wiring methods are allowed only if every 645.4 condition is met (dedicated HVAC, room restricted to qualified personnel, fire-rated separation, a compliant disconnecting means, the Emergency Power Off or EPO); otherwise NEC Chapters 1–4 apply. The 2026 NEC tightens 645 cabling and listing rules in alignment with NFPA 75 (NFPA, NFPA 70, current and 2026 editions). NFPA 75 (IT equipment) and 76 (telecommunications) are industry practice cross-referenced with NEC 645; NFPA 855 (energy storage) drives UPS battery-room fire ratings and suppression as lithium-ion replaces lead-acid; NFPA 72, 2001 and 70E cover alarm, clean agent and electrical worker safety (NFPA). IBC and IFC are the architectural and fire basis, with IMC and IPC; structure and seismic run through IBC and ASCE 7 (International Code Council; ASCE).

ASHRAE 90.4. The data-center energy standard (90.1 still governs envelope, lighting and service water), applying above 20 W/ft² and 10 kW IT. Compliance is two ratios against maxima: the Mechanical Load Component (MLC), cooling, fan, pump and heat-rejection power over IT power, and the Electrical Loss Component (ELC), power-chain losses. The 2022 edition lowered both, replaced the design-condition MLC snapshot with an annualised MLC (8,760-hour model at 25, 50, 75 and 100% load) and aligned ELC to the same part-load method. MLC maxima vary by climate zone; ELC maxima do not. 90.4 defines PUE but does not use it for compliance (ASHRAE, ANSI/ASHRAE 90.4-2022). The 2024 IECC adopts Sections 6 and 8 of 90.4-2022 by reference; the enforceable edition varies by jurisdiction (International Code Council). The reference facility's PUE ≤ 1.4 and its 90.4 compliance are different tests; the BOD must pass both.

Outside the US. IEC publishes the electrical standards; EN 50600 / ISO/IEC 22237 is the dominant design standard in Europe, with ISO/IEC 30134 energy KPIs and the EU Energy Efficiency Directive's reporting on top (CENELEC; ISO/IEC; European Commission).

Reference facility worked example

The facility Topic 1 fixed: 1,000 racks of multi-tenant colocation, about 40 MW IT, hot US metro, PUE 1.4, Tier III class, design-build with owner-furnished long-lead equipment. All numbers indicative.

OPR headline. Wholesale and retail colocation with AI-ready zones; ~40 MW ultimate, day-one Phase 1 ~10 MW IT (shell and site power for 40 MW, MEP for 10 MW) in ~10 MW increments, Phase 1 being Hall 1 of Topic 1's four-hall campus with a mixed profile (course decision, 2026-09-24); Uptime Tier III as campus standard, optional 2N suite for financial and government tenants (§9); PUE ≤ 1.4 at design load, ASHRAE 90.4-2022 compliance, WUE reported; Tier III TCDD and TCCF, SOC 2 Type II and ISO 27001 ready, PCI DSS Requirement 9 controls, NIST 800-53 PE controls for one federal-capable suite, LEED target. The dump's Tier III "$7–10M/MW" is fit-out only and sits inside Topic 1's $10–12M/MW all-in (shell, site, soft costs and land added).

Arithmetic: 300 × 8 + 300 × 15 + 200 × 40 + 200 × 120 = 2,400 + 4,500 + 8,000 + 24,000 = 38,900 kW ≈ 39 MW, rounded to the "40 MW" headline; the AI band expands to reach 40 MW as tenants arrive. Racks per MW run from 125 in the low band (1,000 / 8) to ~8.3 in the AI pods (1,000 / 120), a fifteen-fold spread under one roof.

Cascade checks. Area: 600 air-cooled racks at ~20 ft² need ~12,000 ft²; 400 dense and AI racks at 12–15 ft² need ~4,800–6,000 ft²; white space ~17,000–18,000 ft², plus gray space at roughly 1:1 (Topic 1). Floor loading: AI racks near 1,500 lb (§8) need structural headroom on day one wherever an AI pod could go, including the reserve (Topic 4). Cooling: the 8 and 15 kW bands sit under every air ceiling, the 40 kW band at or inside every RDHx range (Budlong's upper edge), the 120 kW band inside the direct-to-chip band (§6). The AI pods need 120 kW-class busway and liquid manifolds pre-provisioned (Topics 6 and 7).

Day one versus ultimate. Shell and site for 40 MW on day one (structure, floor loading, pathways, pipe stubs and valved taps); Phase 1 MEP of ~10 MW in Hall 1 with a mixed profile, so the first plant is sized against a diversified load across all four bands; later ~10 MW blocks on pads and in rooms reserved on day one; 15–20% of white and gray space in reserve; AI pods pre-provisioned with busway and manifolds.

Stranded-capacity watch. If the AI band grows faster than planned, cooling or busway in the dense pods reaches its limit before floor does (§7). Two guards: the OPR's phasing triggers (section 2), tying the next block of plant to leasing and interconnection milestones, and the BOD's diversified design-load assumption (section 5), sizing the power block below the sum of nameplates (Topic 5).
