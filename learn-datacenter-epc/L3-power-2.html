From clean power to the rack

Deep detail and sources: the appendix for this topic. Short version: sixty words. The research report this chapter was distilled from. Earlier version: archived 2026-09-26.

An uninterruptible power supply (UPS) has prepared electricity for a data hall, a room of computer racks, and we begin at its output. Our example is a 1,000-rack colocation campus near a hot United States city, where multiple customers rent space for information technology (IT) equipment. The racks draw about 39 megawatts (MW), rounded to 40 MW for campus planning. Power usage effectiveness (PUE) divides total site electricity by IT electricity; at 1.4, the site takes about 56 MW, including cooling, not 56 MW through the rack feeds. The densest 200 racks support artificial intelligence (AI) at 120 kilowatts (kW) each.

Follow one rack feed

The UPS output bus is a switchboard where clean power is ready for distribution. A floor power distribution unit (PDU) splits its large feed into smaller circuits protected by breakers, switches that open when a circuit faults. Busway is a prefabricated overhead conductor track; a tap-off box draws a protected circuit from it. Alternatively, a remote power panel (RPP) is a breaker cabinet fed by cable near a rack row.

A whip is the flexible cable drop into a rack. The rack PDU is the outlet strip inside that rack, not the floor cabinet. A dual-corded server has two power cords, one on each independent route we call path A and path B. The diagram follows just one route.

Takeaway: The B route needs its own protected connection all the way to the rack; two plugs on one outlet strip do not make independent routes. Only the IT load, not the site's cooling, follows these rack feeds. The next choice is the voltage delivered along them (APX-power-2 §1).

Voltage sets the feed size

A volt (V) measures electrical potential, and an ampere (A) measures current. Three-phase alternating current (AC) uses three coordinated waveforms; 415/240 V means 415 V between phases and 240 V from one phase to neutral. An ordinary server can use the 240 V connection. This site's UPS delivers 480/277 V, which must be changed before reaching a standard server. The comparison holds each circuit at 60 A and allows 80% of its rating for continuous demand.

Takeaway: At the same current, the 415 V circuit provides about twice the usable rack power of the 208 V circuit. This site still needs a smaller autotransformer at its floor PDU; it removes the older isolation transformer, not every transformer. The autotransformer changes voltage but does not create an isolated supply. Removing the isolation transformer also calls for a fresh fault-current study (APX-power-2 §1 and §4).

Check the arithmetic and the assumption
  • For these three-phase feeds, √3 × 415 × 60 × 0.8 ≈ 34.5 kW; replacing 415 with 208 gives approximately 17.3 kW. The A in 60 A means amperes; path A is only a route name.
  • The 80% factor is a conservative continuous-load planning assumption, not a universal requirement for every listed circuit. Actual ratings and the adopted electrical code govern the finished design.
  • A UPS that already outputs 415/240 V can omit this site's 480-to-415 V step. Neither arrangement guarantees a fixed server-efficiency gain: check the actual equipment and load (APX-power-2 §4).

Fit power around each row

At the floor PDU, the facility owner chooses how easily a customer, or tenant, can change a rack connection. A cable-fed RPP provides fixed cable runs from breakers; busway provides an overhead track with repositionable tap-offs. This site uses cable for its stable 8 kW racks and busway for the denser groups. Both approaches still need protected rack circuits.

Takeaway: Some listed busways permit a qualified installer to add a suitable tap without de-energizing the track. That does not make arbitrary live electrical work safe. The physical reason to plan busway early becomes clear above the dense racks (APX-power-2 §3).

Over a 120 kW rack, a coolant distribution unit (CDU) circulates cooling liquid through shared pipes, while data cables need their own tray. The sidecar bay in the drawing is empty space reserved for a possible future power cabinet, not equipment already installed.

Takeaway: The drawing shows two drops and four rack outlet strips. Their ratings must let either route carry the full rack; four ordinary 60 A strips in total would not suffice for 120 kW. Reserving space keeps an option open without committing to the future power system.

A changeable track is not a safety exemption
  • A supplier's approximately $350 price for adding a busway plug came from a 2019 vendor cost model, not a current installed-price promise. The useful comparison is reconfiguration effort over the hall's life (APX-power-2 §3).
  • A tap must be listed for its specific track. Only qualified workers following the manufacturer's instructions and the site's electrical-safety procedure may use an energized-installation feature.

When a rack path fails

The two power supplies in a dual-corded server are called power supply units (PSUs). If they share demand, A and B normally draw roughly half the rack's power each. Nevertheless, breakers, whips and rack outlet strips on either route must support the whole rack. Otherwise losing one route merely overloads the survivor.

Takeaway: A rack with two cords needs no switch to transfer a server between them. A single-corded appliance does: a nearby static transfer switch (STS) or automatic transfer switch (ATS) can select its surviving source. That switch can itself fail, so placing it near the appliance limits the damage to one load rather than a whole row. Metering after repair checks that the two cords really use different routes (APX-power-2 §2 and §5).

What a local transfer switch changes
  • An STS can transfer in roughly 4 milliseconds (ms) under suitable conditions; an ATS uses mechanical contacts and is generally slower. Neither turns a single-corded device into two independently powered server supplies.
  • The roughly 50% normal draw is a rack-level illustration for supplies that share evenly, not a rule that every upstream UPS block must run at half load. Per-outlet or branch metering can expose a disconnected supply before the remaining path fails.

Make the upstream paths maintainable

Two rack cords are useless if both routes rejoin at one power room. Tier III means covered equipment can be maintained without interrupting the IT load, not a guaranteed uptime percentage. The reference design uses three-to-make-two distributed redundancy (3M2): three separate UPS rooms, each with a 2.5 MW block, feed rack rows in pairs. Any two rooms can provide 5 MW.

See what remains when room B is shut.

Takeaway: Row 1 keeps A, row 2 keeps C, and row 3 keeps both. At 5 MW usable load, each block normally carries about 1.67 MW and can rise to 2.5 MW when one room fails. That two-thirds block loading differs from the roughly half-and-half draw in a balanced dual-corded rack.

Exactly enough installed capacity is N; one spare block is N+1; two complete separate systems are 2N. For 10 MW usable in the first hall, compare the equipment counts.

Takeaway: Six blocks give the baseline 3M2 hall; an all-2N hall would need eight. An optional 2N customer suite sits outside that six-block count. Uptime Institute removed expected-downtime figures from its Tier standard in 2009; the complete paths and maintenance design determine a Tier classification (APX-power-2 §6, §7 and §12).

Other reserves and what reliability arithmetic cannot promise
  • Four-to-make-three (4M3) can use 75% of installed capacity. A block-redundant or catcher arrangement keeps one reserve block and switches affected loads to it; this reference 3M2 layout does not put a central transfer switch in every row path. Two full systems plus additional spare capacity is called 2N+1.
  • A spare module inside a UPS block is not the same as a spare 2.5 MW block. The comparison treats 2.5 MW as each block's available rating after its internal module policy; the optional 2N suite needs a separate equipment count.
  • A simplified model gives two independent 99.9% paths 99.9999% joint availability. Shared failures, operating mistakes and repair times defeat its assumptions. It is not an Uptime Institute Tier prediction (APX-power-2 §7).
  • Some AI training jobs can restart from saved progress, so a particular customer may accept less compute-path redundancy. That does not silently change this facility's Tier III design.

Work the 120 kW rack back to its breakers
  • One 415 V, three-phase, 60 A circuit provides approximately 34.5 kW under the stated allowance. Four such protected circuits are needed on each route to support 120 kW, meaning eight single-input 60 A rack strips if that is the selected equipment.
  • With the four strips in the existing overhead drawing, use two suitably rated strips of about 125 A per route instead, with appropriately protected branches from each approximately 250 A tap-off. The rack strips, connectors, branch protection and feed sharing all need a coordinated equipment design.
  • A 160 A tap-off would supply only about 92 kW on the surviving side. If future racks need substantially more than 120 kW, reduce the racks per busway run or increase its rating rather than assuming the original four-rack grouping still works.

A dense rack changes its power interface

At 120 kW, a rack can still accept AC, but some customers move conversion into a shared power shelf. The OCP Open Rack v3 (ORv3) design turns facility AC into a 48 V direct-current (DC) busbar, a rigid conductor shared by servers. A proposed neighboring power cabinet, or sidecar, would instead supply ±400 V DC, which is 800 V between its rails. Its 2027 label is a vendor target; the dashed owner-to-tenant boundary is illustrative, not a standard contract.

Takeaway: At 120 kW, distributing 48 V alone implies about 2,500 A before losses, making copper and connectors difficult to manage. Dividing watts by DC volts shows the scale.

Takeaway: Higher voltage reduces current but not the safety work. A DC arc, electricity jumping a gap, can persist because DC does not naturally pass through zero as AC does. This hall keeps AC distribution today and reserves sidecar space for a later decision (APX-power-2 §9).

A reserved bay is not a conversion plan

The owner buys AC busway for the first phase; a later tenant's 800 V DC equipment may not be able to reuse it. Reserve floor area, structural support and cable routes before ordering, and name the owner of a future DC connection in the lease.

Read efficiency claims as comparisons, not guarantees
  • The reported gain from Google's move to 48 V was relative to its older 12 V design, not a promised gain over every modern AC rack. Supplier estimates for 800 V DC, including efficiency and copper savings, depend on the compared architecture.
  • The left rack keeps individual server power supplies and cords; the 48 V rack shares its conversion shelf. A 48 V rack power shelf can take power from today's AC busway. An 800 V sidecar requires appropriately rated conversion, disconnection, protection and approval; large-scale 2027 deployment remains a vendor target (APX-power-2 §9).

Contain faults and protect people

A short circuit is an unintended low-resistance route that can draw a large current. Available fault current is what the source could deliver into that short; a kiloampere (kA) is 1,000 A. The nearest breaker should open first so other racks stay lit, an arrangement called selective coordination. Removing the older isolation transformer can raise available fault current, as these published examples show.

Takeaway: This site's 480-to-415 V autotransformer is not either complete example circuit, so its engineer must calculate the actual fault current. The study checks normal and maintenance configurations, plus arc-flash hazards, the intense heat and light released by an electrical arc. Bonding each rack to a common grounding network gives fault current an intended return path. Changing a tenant feed can invalidate the study and its equipment labels (APX-power-2 §10).

Grounding, harmonics and electrical work
  • Check each breaker's ability to interrupt the modeled short circuit and each cabinet's short-circuit rating. Verify that a rack fault opens its branch protection before an upstream row device.
  • Bond cabinets to the common network with approved individual connections, not a chain through neighboring racks. Modern server supplies often use active power factor correction (PFC), reducing harmonic distortion; dense groups of similar AI supplies still merit an engineering check before transformer ratings are chosen.
  • NFPA 70E addresses safe electrical work. Arc-flash results inform labels, work controls and protective equipment; a single incident-energy threshold must not be presented as a universal ban on work (APX-power-2 §10).

Choose an emergency shutdown route

An emergency shutoff can protect responders, but accidental activation can stop healthy racks. The National Electrical Code (NEC) Article 645 permits special wiring methods in qualifying equipment rooms and generally requires an emergency power-off (EPO) disconnect, which also stops dedicated cooling. Another route is ordinary Chapter 3 wiring, the code's general wiring methods, without invoking that Article 645 trade. The authority having jurisdiction (AHJ), the local code approver, and the fire marshal must check what applies to this room.

Takeaway: The reference plan proposes ordinary wiring and no Article 645 EPO, not an exemption granted in advance. If a disconnect is required, guard and test it, zone it where permitted, and never bypass it after approval. Investigators called activation of an uncovered EPO the likely cause of a 2021 rail-control outage even though its UPS had ridden through the initial power disturbance (APX-power-2 §11).

The code question does not end with Article 645
  • Check the NEC edition and amendments actually adopted locally. NFPA 75 can independently affect shutdown when certain gaseous fire-suppression systems operate; an approved risk analysis may change that result.
  • The rail investigation identified EPO activation as the most likely cause, not a proved universal failure mode. A frequently quoted 2010 survey combined accidental EPO and human error into one category; its combined percentage is not the percentage of outages caused solely by EPO (APX-power-2 §11 and §13).

Build and hand over the design

The owner freezes paths before ordering gear. In design-build, one contractor designs and builds, while this owner buys some long-lead switchgear, large assemblies of switches and breakers. Commissioning tests completed feeds with a route deliberately out of service.

Takeaway: Wholesale customers usually fit out leased suites; retail customers may receive rack-ready space. The lease must specify who supplies tap-offs, whips and rack PDUs (APX-power-2 §5 and §8).

Who keeps the handoff safe?

Owner-supplied power equipment meets builder-installed wiring and tenant-supplied rack gear. Assign A/B tests, authorized tap work and fault-study updates after tenant changes. Do not assume a later DC interface can use today's AC busway.

The budget behind these commitments
  • The first approximately 10 MW phase costs about $180–230 million because it includes shared site and shell work. The full approximately 40 MW campus costs about $400–500 million excluding IT equipment; phase-one cost is not a price to multiply by four.
  • Annual operating cost is about $65 million, including about $34 million for electricity. The owner must compare reserve equipment, wiring, losses and future flexibility rather than buying on first cost alone (APX-power-2 §8 and §12).
