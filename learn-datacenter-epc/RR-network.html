The Network of a New-Build Data Center: Logical Layer First, Physical Layer Second (2025–2026)

The Desktop Research report for Topic 9, run 2026-09-25 from briefs/ and published as delivered: no editing, no lint. The chapter and appendix were distilled from it; where they and this report disagree, the chapter is the course's position.

TL;DR

  • The logical fabric decides the building. A new-build campus in 2025–26 runs a Layer-3 spine-leaf Clos on eBGP (RFC 7938), and for its AI pods a second, physically separate rail-optimized back-end fabric that dominates the switch, optics and fiber budget — for a GB300 NVL72-class cluster the network is roughly 15% of total cluster cost (SemiAnalysis, 2025), optical transceivers alone are ~60% of that network cost and ~45% of its power, and each GPU pulls roughly six optical transceivers.
  • Copper reach is now a floor-plan rule, not a cabling detail. At 800G, passive DAC reaches only ~2 m and active copper ~5–7 m, so same-rail leaf switches must sit within a rack or two of the GPUs; everything else is optics. The physical plant should be single-mode OS2 with MPO-12/APC and MPO-16 trunks, because the cable plant lives 15–25 years while optics refresh every 3–5.
  • Ethernet has overtaken InfiniBand for AI back-ends in 2025 (Dell'Oro), the Ultra Ethernet Consortium 1.0 spec shipped June 11 2025, 51.2T (64×800G) switches are the workhorse, 1.6T/102.4T and co-packaged optics arrive in 2026, and the whole thing is delivered as Division 27 low-voltage work under an RCDD stamp — for a colo operator the meet-me room and entrance facilities are owner deliverables commissioned before day one.

1. The Logical Fabric

1.1 Three-tier vs spine-leaf Clos — why the industry moved

The legacy three-tier design (access → aggregation → core) was built for north-south traffic (client-to-server) and leaned on Spanning Tree Protocol to break loops, which blocked redundant links and left half the fabric idle. Modern workloads — virtualization, microservices, distributed storage and above all AI training — are overwhelmingly east-west (server-to-server). In a three-tier network, two servers in different racks must traverse up to the aggregation or core layer and back, adding hops, latency, buffering risk and dropped packets.

The spine-leaf (folded-Clos) topology, named for Charles Clos (1953), flattens this. Every leaf connects to every spine; any server reaches any other in exactly two hops (leaf→spine→leaf), with ECMP load-balancing across every uplink and no blocked links. Scaling is linear: add leaves for ports, add spines for bandwidth. This is the de facto standard for new builds.

FIGURE — the Clos fabric in one shape (2-tier, non-blocking, 51.2T switches): - Tier 1 (leaf/ToR): radix-64 switch (e.g., Broadcom Tomahawk 5, 64×800G). Half the ports face servers, half face spines. - Tier 2 (spine): same radix-64 switch. Number of spines = uplinks per leaf. - ECMP spreads flows across all spine paths; eBGP is the control plane.

1.2 Oversubscription — what 3:1 vs 1:1 costs

Oversubscription is the ratio of downlink (server-facing) to uplink (spine-facing) bandwidth on a leaf. Legacy enterprise fabrics ran 3:1 or worse (three times as much server bandwidth as uplink), which is fine for bursty web traffic. AI back-end fabrics run 1:1 (non-blocking) — every server-facing bit has a matching uplink bit — because collective operations (all-reduce) synchronize thousands of GPUs at once and stall on the slowest link.

The cost is direct. On a radix-64 leaf, a 1:1 split gives 32 server ports and 32 uplinks; a 3:1 split gives 48 server ports and 16 uplinks. Non-blocking therefore needs 50% more leaf switches, 50% more spine switches, and roughly double the inter-switch optics and fiber to land the same number of servers. That doubled optics count is the single biggest reason AI fabrics are expensive.

1.3 Two tiers vs super-spine at pod, hall and campus scale

A 2-tier Clos on radix-64 800G silicon reaches roughly 2,048 GPUs at 1:1 non-blocking (IP Infusion). Beyond that, a third tier — the super-spine (or core/spine-leaf-group) — interconnects multiple pods and scales a 3-stage Clos to 16,000+ GPUs. NVIDIA's own reference architecture introduces a super-spine layer at roughly 1,024 nodes and above. The rule of thumb: pod = 2 tiers; hall or campus = 3 tiers. Each added tier adds a hop of latency and another full layer of optics, which is why operators avoid it until forced.

1.4 What 51.2T switches do to tier count

The radix of the switch chip determines how many tiers you need. Broadcom's Tomahawk 5 (BCM78900) delivers 51.2 Tb/s on a single chip: 64×800G, 128×400G or 256×200G. Marvell's Teralynx 10 is also 51.2T with a radix up to 512×100G. 650 Group's point is blunt: one 51.2T switch replaces twelve 12.8T switches, and Marvell notes a higher-radix chip can collapse a three-tier network to two tiers, saving over 1 MW in a large cluster. Without 51.2T silicon, AI fabrics would have needed so many tiers they'd be infeasible. The 2026 successor is 102.4T (512→1,024 lanes) and 1.6T ports.

1.5 ToR vs EoR/MoR

  • Top-of-Rack (ToR): a leaf switch in each rack. Server-to-switch links are short in-rack copper (DAC); only uplinks leave the rack. This minimizes long cable runs but multiplies switch count and can strand ports in low-density racks. Failure domain = one rack.
  • End-of-Row / Middle-of-Row (EoR/MoR): one larger switch (or pair) per row; every server cables back to the row end. This consolidates switches and reduces stranded ports but demands long, dense structured copper/fiber runs down the row and makes the row-end switch a bigger failure domain.

For AI back-end fabrics, copper reach forces a hybrid: leaves must sit close (MoR/adjacent-rack) because 800G passive copper reaches only ~2 m (see §2.4, §5.1).

1.6 Underlay and overlay

Underlay — BGP as the fabric protocol. RFC 7938 (Aug 2016) codified using eBGP as the single routing protocol for large Clos fabrics: a unique private ASN per ToR (from 64512–65534), a single ASN for all Tier-1 devices, single-hop sessions on point-to-point links, L3 to the ToR. This is chosen for scalability and stability over large Layer-2 domains, and it minimizes blast radius — the 2021 Meta outage (§4.1) is the cautionary tale of BGP withdrawals gone wrong.

Overlay — EVPN/VXLAN. On top of the L3 underlay, EVPN/VXLAN tunnels give multi-tenancy, L2 adjacency where applications still need it, and VM/workload mobility. Who skips it: dedicated single-tenant AI back-end fabrics often run pure routed/RoCE without an overlay because the workload is one tenant and every microsecond of encapsulation overhead matters. Colo operators offering multi-tenant network services lean heavily on EVPN/VXLAN.

1.7 Colocation operator vs tenant — who runs what

  • Operator runs: carrier handoff at the entrance facilities, the meet-me room (MMR), cross-connects, and the facility LAN/OOB for building systems. In a wholesale deal (a tenant leasing a whole hall or several MW), the operator hands over conduit, tray and power and the tenant does nearly everything inside the cage. In retail colo (a cage or a few racks), the operator provides far more — structured cabling to the cabinet, interconnection services, remote hands.
  • Tenant runs: everything inside the cage — the compute fabric, its spine-leaf, its ToR, its servers and storage.
  • Who designs it: the operator's design team (often with an RCDD) designs the facility cabling and MMR; the tenant (or its IT/cluster vendor) designs the in-cage fabric. For AI pods the cluster vendor's reference architecture (e.g., NVIDIA's) effectively dictates the tenant's fabric.

2. AI Back-End Fabrics

2.1 The four networks of an AI pod

An NVL72-class pod runs four logically and often physically separate networks (per NVIDIA's NVL72 AI Factory reference architecture): 1. Back-end / GPU compute (east-west): the RDMA fabric between GPUs. Highest bandwidth, rail-optimized, non-blocking. ConnectX-8 SuperNICs, 800G per GPU (operated as 2×400G in the dual-plane design). 2. Front-end / CPU converged (north-south): node management, storage access, user traffic. Each node has a BlueField-3 DPU with 2×400G to two switches for redundancy. 3. Storage: converged connectivity for storage infrastructure; often merged with the front-end at small scale. 4. Out-of-band management: 1 GbE RJ45 to every node's BMC (§4).

2.2 Scale-up vs scale-out — where one ends

  • Scale-up is the very-high-bandwidth interconnect inside the rack: NVLink within an NVL72, where 72 GPUs form one NVLink domain at 900 GB/s (1,800 GB/s bidirectional) and behave as a single accelerator. This is copper (NVLink spine) inside the rack — out of scope here except as the boundary.
  • Scale-out is the network between racks and pods: the rail-optimized Clos fabric this section describes. A collective operation uses scale-up first inside the NVLink domain, then hands the remainder to the scale-out fabric. The boundary is the rack.

2.3 InfiniBand vs Ethernet/RoCEv2 vs Ultra Ethernet

InfiniBand (NVIDIA Quantum): NDR = 400G/port, XDR = 800G/port (Quantum-X800, Q3400 switch, 144×800G). Lossless by design via credit-based flow control (no PFC tuning). Adaptive routing in hardware. SHARP does in-network reduction (gradient summing in the switch), cutting all-reduce traffic up to 9× — InfiniBand's key differentiator. Latency ~1–2 µs. The purist's choice for large training.

Ethernet with RoCEv2 (NVIDIA Spectrum-X, Broadcom Tomahawk 5/6): encapsulates RDMA in UDP/IP over standard Ethernet. Needs PFC + ECN/DCQCN tuning to stay lossless. Spectrum-X (SN5600 switch + BlueField-3/ConnectX-8) claims ~95% effective bandwidth via adaptive routing + SuperNIC packet reordering. Latency ~2–5 µs. The ecosystem choice — runs on tooling teams already know.

Ultra Ethernet 1.0 (UEC, released June 11 2025): an open, multi-vendor, 562-page stack with a new Ultra Ethernet Transport (UET) — modern RDMA, packet-level multipathing (replacing RoCEv2 head-of-line blocking), and modern congestion control. Backed by AMD, Arista, Broadcom, Cisco, Intel, Meta, Microsoft, Oracle and (later) NVIDIA. Products emerging late 2025 into 2026.

Market-share shift (Dell'Oro). In Dell'Oro's words (VP Sameh Boujelbene, July 15 2025), "When we first initiated our coverage of AI Back-end Networks in late 2023, the market was dominated by InfiniBand, holding over 80 percent share." By late 2025 that had inverted: Dell'Oro (Dec 5 2025) reported Ethernet "accounted for more than two-thirds of data center switch sales in AI back-end networks both during the quarter and across the first three quarters of the year—up from less than half in the same period last year," and for full-year 2025 Ethernet "more than doubles size of InfiniBand" (Mar 10 2026). 2025 is the crossover year — Ethernet surpassed InfiniBand in AI back-end adoption, driven by hyperscalers and neoclouds. InfiniBand switch sales still surged in 2Q2025 on Blackwell Ultra demand, but Ethernet holds the lead.

2.4 Speeds, optics and copper reach

Shipping now: 400G and 800G in volume; 800G ports surpassed 20 million within three years of shipments (vs 6–7 years for 400G). 2026: 1.6T ports (via 8×200G) and 102.4T switch silicon; IEEE 802.3dj (200G/lane, defining 200/400/800G/1.6T over copper and SMF to 40 km) is at draft D2.4 and tracking to completion late 2026 (September 2026 target); a 400G/lane project is already starting.

Optics form factors and power: pluggable OSFP (larger thermal envelope, up to ~24 W, favored for 800G/1.6T) vs QSFP-DD (denser, tighter thermals). Standard 800G gray optics draw 14–18 W; the DSP alone is 6–7 W (12–14 W at 1.6T). Linear-drive (LPO) removes the DSP to save power; co-packaged optics (CPO) move the optical engine onto the switch package for the lowest power per port at the cost of field-replaceability — expected to begin volume ramp in 2026 on both IB and Ethernet.

Optics as a share and a failure source: optical transceivers consume approximately 50% of a traditional switch system's power and constitute more than 50% of its cost (Broadcom). In a fully loaded 32-port 800G switch, optics alone can exceed 500 W — more than the switch ASIC. Because each optic is an active, powered, laser-bearing component, thousands of them are also the dominant hardware failure source in an AI fabric.

The physics: at 800G each lane runs 112 Gbps PAM4 (~28 GHz Nyquist); copper loss roughly doubles vs 400G, collapsing passive reach to ~2 m. IEEE 802.3ck specifies 2 m maximum at 112G. This is why the "3–7 m dead zone" exists and why AEC (retimed copper) fills it.

2.5 Rail-optimized topology

A "rail" is a same-index NIC position across all GPU servers. In an 8-GPU server, GPU-1's NIC on every server connects to the same leaf switch (the "GPU-1 rail leaf"), GPU-2's to another, and so on. Because the dominant all-reduce traffic is same-rail, it stays on one leaf hop — the lowest-latency path. GPUs on different rails or racks traverse the spine.

For NVL72-class racks NVIDIA uses a dual-plane design: each GPU's 800G ConnectX-8 port breaks into 2×400G, one to each of two identical planes, each plane scaling to 1,024×400G interfaces. Scalable Unit (SU) = one GB300 NVL72 rack (72 GPUs, 18 trays, each tray with 4 ConnectX-8 SuperNICs). Reference designs show 8 leaf + 4 spine per 2-rack SU for compute, scaling to super-spine above ~1,024 nodes.

Floor-plan consequence: because same-rail NICs must reach their leaf within copper distance, leaf switches sit in or immediately adjacent to the GPU racks (MoR/adjacent-rack), and the fiber lengths and leaf placement follow the rail map, not convenience.

2.6 Proportions — how much of an AI pod is network

For a GB300 NVL72 cluster (SemiAnalysis, 2025): - Networking = 15% of total cluster cost for a 3-layer InfiniBand network, rising to 18% for a 4-layer network. - Optical transceivers = ~60% of networking cost and ~45% of networking power (3-layer, NVIDIA LinkX optics). - Back-end network = ~85% of networking cost and ~86% of networking power for 3-layer GB300 clusters. - ~6 optical transceivers per GPU in rail-optimized designs; across a million GPUs, transceiver power alone approaches 180 MW. - Broadcom CEO Hock Tan (Q1 FY2026 earnings call): AI networking reached ~40% of AI semiconductor revenue — which he called "almost a situation where stars are aligned," a high-water mark — and guided the share to normalize closer to 30% as XPUs ramp (SDxCentral, 2026).

Delivery and risk: the fabric may be bundled with the cluster (NVIDIA SuperPOD/DGX ships validated switches, optics and cables as one BOM) or independently designed (operator picks Ethernet vendors). Someone must physically pull and certify thousands of fiber jumpers — a low-voltage contractor or the cluster vendor's install team. 2025–26 lead times on 800G optics and switches are a live supply risk; Dell'Oro flags supply constraints and vendor diversification as the 2026 theme, and optics are the pacing item.

3. Peering, Interconnection and the Edge of the Building

3.1 Carrier entry

TIA-942 calls for two diverse entrance facilities (entrance rooms) fed by separate duct banks / maintenance holes, physically separated so a single backhoe cannot cut both. TIA-942's redundancy guidance (rated-tier language) asks for two access-provider entrance pathways at least 20 m (66 ft) apart. For a 40 MW campus expect multiple (typically 4+) carriers for competitive diverse transport, with a mix of dark fiber (customer lights it) and lit/wavelength services (carrier-provided). Fiber availability and carrier diversity is a top site-selection criterion — applied here, the Phoenix/Dallas site was chosen partly for its metro fiber density.

3.2 Meet-me rooms

An MMR is the neutral room where carriers and tenants interconnect. It can be carrier-neutral (operator-owned, any carrier) or carrier-owned. For a four-hall campus, expect two MMRs (one per entrance facility, for diversity) sized to hold carrier racks, the cross-connect fields and DWDM/DCI gear. Cross-connects are the revenue line: a cross-connect is physically a single-mode fiber jumper (Equinix standardized to SMF-only on March 31 2024) between two customers' patch panels. Monthly recurring pricing is ~$100–$300 per cross-connect (Vendr, 2026), with real-world metro quotes $220–$350. Interconnection is 19% of Equinix's recurring revenue (FY2024) — a very high-margin franchise; per Equinix's Q4/FY2024 results (Feb 12 2025), interconnection revenues "stepped up 9% year-over-year...accounting for 19% of Equinix's recurring revenue," with "more than 482,000 total interconnections." That compares with "interconnection and other" at ~8% of Digital Realty's total revenue (FY2024). Wholesale halls have far fewer cross-connects because a single tenant owns the whole hall and does its interconnection privately.

3.3 Data center interconnect (DCI)

DCI links buildings and campuses. Options: dark fiber (you own the wavelengths) vs wavelength/lit services; DWDM multiplexing many wavelengths on a fiber pair; and coherent pluggables — 400ZR/800ZR (OIF), which plug straight into router/switch ports and reach ~80–120 km amplified for metro DCI, with ZR+ variants reaching 600–1,000+ km for regional/long-haul. 800ZR was finalized by OIF in October 2024, products in 2025. Latency is ~5 µs/km of fiber, the governing number for multi-site AI training links (synchronization across sites gets expensive fast). Metro (≤120 km) vs long-haul (ZR+, hundreds–thousands of km).

3.4 Internet exchanges and peering

Transit = paying an upstream for full routes; peering = settlement-free (or paid) direct exchange with other networks, usually at an IXP. Cloud on-ramps (Direct Connect, ExpressRoute, Cloud Interconnect) give private paths to hyperscalers. In Phoenix, the reference campus would peer at the local exchange fabric (PHX-IX and the carrier hotels); in Dallas, at the Infomart / major IXPs. Retail tenants peer to cut transit costs and latency; wholesale tenants often bring their own backbones.

3.5 Delivery and risk

Carrier lead times to reach a new building with a lateral can run months and are a pre-opening critical-path item. Who pays the lateral is negotiated (carrier, developer or a shared build). Carrier agreements are a pre-opening milestone, and the MMR is an owner deliverable — commissioned before the first tenant can light a circuit.

4. Out-of-Band Management and the NOC as Network

4.1 The OOB network

The out-of-band (OOB) network is a physically separate management LAN with its own switches and its own uplink — a cellular modem or a second carrier — that must survive a failure of the production fabric. It connects console servers, and every device's BMC/IPMI/Redfish management port. NVIDIA's RA uses SN2201 switches (48×1G + 4×100G) to reach every node's BMC and every switch's management port, uplinked into a separate core.

The 2021 Meta/Facebook outage is the case where OOB did not save them. On October 4 2021 a backbone-audit command withdrew Meta's BGP routes, partitioning its data centers and taking its DNS offline for ~6 hours. In Meta's own words (Meta Engineering, "More details about the October 4 outage," Oct 5 2021), "Our primary and out-of-band network access was down, so we sent engineers onsite to the data centers to have them debug the issue and restart the systems." Engineers had to physically reach routers' console ports (and cut into badge-locked cages) because the OOB path shared fate with the production network. The lesson every new build now applies: the OOB must be truly independent in path, power and reachability.

4.2 What rides it (names only)

Facility OT: BMS, EPMS, DCIM, security systems, rack PDU and CDU controllers. Tenant IT: server BMCs, switch management ports. (These systems are owned by later topics — here they matter only as ports on the OOB LAN.) The principle: facility OT and tenant IT ride logically separate segments, often on the same physical OOB plant with VLAN isolation.

4.3 The NOC's demands

Monitoring collectors and remote-hands access, 1–2 management ports per device, on copper Cat6A at 1–10G (RJ45). Cheap, bulk, reliable. Delivery and risk: in a colo, the operator owns the facility OOB (building systems), the tenant owns its own device OOB inside the cage; the OOB is commissioned first because you cannot safely bring up power, cooling or the production fabric without management visibility.

5. The Physical Layer

5.1 Copper

  • Cat6: 1G to ~55 m at 10G (legacy).
  • Cat6A: 10GBASE-T to 100 m — the workhorse for management and PoE.
  • Cat8: 25/40G to 30 m — short switch-to-switch in-row.
  • PoE powers cameras and environmental sensors over the same copper.
  • Where copper still wins: OOB management (Cat6A), and in-rack DAC for server-to-ToR at 800G (≤2 m). Copper is dead for high-speed reach beyond a rack.

5.2 Fiber

  • Single-mode OS2 (yellow): effectively unlimited data-center reach; the correct default for new builds.
  • Multimode OM3/OM4/OM5 (aqua/lime): cheaper optics historically, but reach collapses at high speed and it has no 800G+ future beyond SR8; use only for short existing runs.

TIA-942-C (2024) updates: recognizes single-pair Ethernet for horizontal cabling, requires a minimum of two Category 6A cables to high-throughput wireless access points, requires minimum two fibers for horizontal/backbone, and allows any TIA-568.3-compliant fiber connector outside equipment outlets. 5.4 Pathways

Overhead cable tray, ladder rack and fiber raceway (the new-build default) vs underfloor. TIA-569 and the NEC (725/770/800 series) govern separation from power to prevent EMI. In the AI pods, the ceiling is shared with 120 kW busway and liquid manifolds — the stack order matters: typically busway highest, then cable tray, with liquid manifolds routed to avoid dripping on either (drip exposure is a real risk; trays should not sit directly under manifold joints), all seismically braced, with clearance for access. Fill ratios ≤40–50% (never fill a tray more than half). Expect multiple trays per row (separate fiber and copper; separate power).

5.5 Cross-connect vs interconnect; testing; management

  • Cross-connect = two panels joined by a jumper through a distributor (structured, flexible). Interconnect = direct equipment-to-equipment (fewer connection points). Structured cabling (patch panels, trunks) beats point-to-point for manageability at scale.
  • TIA-606-D governs labeling (every cable, panel, port). TIA-607 governs bonding/grounding.
  • Testing: TIA-568.3 insertion-loss limits; Tier 1 (basic insertion loss/length with a light source) vs Tier 2 (adds OTDR trace). AI-pod fiber typically requires Tier 2 + manufacturer certification for the 25-year warranty.
  • Airflow: blanking panels, brush grommets, and sealed containment penetrations (also firestopped per code) keep hot and cold air separated.

6. Delivery and Risk: Who Builds the Cabling, and When

6.1 Structured cabling as Division 27

Under design-build, structured cabling is Division 27 (communications) low-voltage work — either a subcontract under the electrical contractor or a separate low-voltage contractor. The design and installation typically require an RCDD (Registered Communications Distribution Designer) stamp; outside-plant work may need an OSP designation. Materials are owner-furnished or contractor-furnished per the contract. Timing: conduit and tray go in with the MEP rough-in; trunks and terminations follow once rooms are weather-tight; certification is near the end before commissioning.

6.2 Facility vs tenant cabling — the demarcation

  • Owner's: ER/MMR to the MDA — the facility backbone.
  • Negotiable: MDA to a tenant's HDA.
  • Tenant's: everything inside the cage.
  • Wholesale vs retail: wholesale tenants take raw conduit/tray/power and build their own; retail tenants get structured cabling to the cabinet.
  • What the owner pre-provisions: conduit, cable tray, and fiber trunks to each pod (the 15–20% reserve / pre-provisioning principle), so a tenant can light a pod without new construction.

6.3 The AI pod — who pulls and certifies

For the AI pod, the cluster vendor, a low-voltage contractor, or the tenant may pull and certify the fiber. NVIDIA-path builds often use cluster-vendor-certified install teams; otherwise a Division 27 contractor certifies to the connectivity manufacturer's spec to earn the 25-year manufacturer warranty. Cost per rack and per port rises sharply for AI pods versus general compute — far more fiber, far more optics, far more labor per rack — which is why network is 15–18% of AI cluster cost (§2.6) versus a small fraction in a general hall. Hand-offs (names only): test reports, as-builts, and the labeling audit.

Worked Example — The Reference Facility (all numbers INDICATIVE)

1,000-rack, ~40 MW campus outside Phoenix; four density zones; Tier III; ~10 MW phases. Phase 1 = Hall 1 ≈10 MW IT.

Part A — Hall 1 structured cabling plan (INDICATIVE)

  • Entrance facilities: 2 diverse ERs, fed by 2 separate duct banks ≥20 m apart (TIA-942). 4+ carriers, mix of dark and lit fiber.
  • MMR: positioned centrally between the two ERs so cross-connect runs to each hall's MDA are balanced; 2 MMRs campus-wide for diversity.
  • MDA count: 1 MDA per hall (Hall 1 = 1 MDA), cross-connected to both ERs.
  • HDA per pod: 1 HDA per pod (≈ per several rows). Hall 1 mixed profile ≈ 250 racks → ~4–6 HDAs.
  • Fiber strand counts (INDICATIVE): a general-compute pod of ~40 racks with ToR uplinks at 2×400G DR4 (MPO-12, 8 fibers each) to the HDA: 40 racks × 2 uplinks × 8 fibers = 640 fibers/pod, provisioned as 80× base-8 trunks, plus 50% reserve → ~960 fibers/pod.
  • Per row: ~20 racks/row × 2 uplinks × 8 fibers = 320 fibers/row, as 40× base-8 trunks.
  • Trunk sizes: base-8 MPO-12/APC trunks aggregated into 144-fiber backbone cables MDA↔HDA.
  • Pathway routes: overhead fiber raceway (separate from copper tray, separate from power), MDA→HDA→EDA in a star.
  • Trays per row / fill: ≥2 trays per row (one fiber, one copper), each at ≤40–50% fill.

Part B — The 200-rack AI zone back-end network (~24 MW; INDICATIVE)

Given: 200 racks × 120 kW = 24 MW; NVL72-class = 72 GPUs/rack → 14,400 GPUs; each GPU 1× ConnectX-8 800G (operated 2×400G, dual-plane).

GPU/NIC counts: 14,400 GPUs × 800G = 14,400 back-end NIC ports (28,800 × 400G logical links across two planes).

Leaf/spine switches (rail-optimized, non-blocking, 51.2T = 64×800G, per plane): - Per SU (72 GPUs, 2-rack): ~8 leaf + 4 spine (NVIDIA RA). 200 racks ≈ 100 SUs of 2 racks... scaled: ~200 leaf + ~64 spine per plane; dual-plane → ~400 leaf + ~128 spine ≈ 530 back-end switches, plus a super-spine tier above ~1,024 nodes → add ~32–64 core switches. Call it ~560–590 switches for the back-end alone. - Optics count: every fiber link = 2 transceivers. At ~6 optics/GPU (SemiAnalysis): 14,400 × 6 ≈ 86,400 optical transceivers in the zone. - Transceiver power: 86,400 × ~16 W ≈ 1.38 MW just in back-end optics. - Switch power: ~580 switches × ~2.5 kW ≈ 1.45 MW (of which optics are ~50%, consistent with the line above). - Network power as share of 24 MW: (~1.4 MW optics + switch-ASIC/other) ≈ ~2.5–3 MW ≈ 10–12% of the zone's IT power — consistent with SemiAnalysis's ~15% of cost / ~45% of network power being optics. - Cable counts and lengths by bin: - In-rack copper (NVLink scale-up): internal to rack — out of scope. - GPU→leaf (rail): ~2 m — mostly passive DAC (≤2 m) or AEC (to ~7 m) where the leaf is an adjacent rack. ≈ 28,800 links. - Leaf→spine: cross-hall, ~10–100 m — DR4/DR8 optics on SMF. ≈ leaf-uplink count (tens of thousands of fibers). - Spine→super-spine: 100 m+ — DR/FR optics. - Pathway fill: an 800G DAC bundle is 10–14 mm thick; at 64 cables/switch, DAC bundles alone can block airflow — another reason optics (thin fiber) dominate beyond the rack, and trays must be sized at ≤50% fill.

(Every count above is INDICATIVE and rounds NVIDIA's published per-SU ratios up to the zone; exact BOM depends on the chosen SU size, plane count and oversubscription.)

Open Questions / What's Changing Fast in 2026

  • Ultra Ethernet vs Spectrum-X vs InfiniBand: UEC 1.0 products are landing; whether open UEC displaces NVIDIA's vertically integrated Spectrum-X (and the remaining InfiniBand base) is the biggest live question. Ethernet has the lead; the size of the lead is moving monthly.
  • 1.6T and 102.4T: IEEE 802.3dj (200G/lane) completes late 2026; 400G/lane work is starting. Early 1.6T products in 2026.
  • Co-packaged optics: initial volume ramp expected 2026 on both IB and Ethernet — could break the "optics = 50% of switch power" wall, but at the cost of field-replaceability.
  • MPO-16 vs VSFF: the physical-plant bet — MPO-16 (proven, but bulky) vs SN-MT/MMC (3× density, immature tooling/test). Guessing wrong means re-patching in three years.
  • Supply risk: 800G optics and switch lead times remain the pacing item; Dell'Oro's 2026 theme is vendor diversification and SONiC adoption to de-risk supply.

Glossary

  • Clos / spine-leaf: non-blocking fabric where every leaf connects to every spine; any endpoint two hops from any other.
  • ECMP: equal-cost multipath — spreading flows across all equal-length paths.
  • Oversubscription: ratio of server-facing to uplink bandwidth on a leaf (1:1 = non-blocking).
  • Super-spine: third Clos tier interconnecting multiple pods.
  • Radix: number of ports on a switch chip (64 for a 51.2T 800G switch).
  • eBGP / RFC 7938: external BGP used as the sole data-center routing protocol.
  • EVPN/VXLAN: overlay giving L2 adjacency and multi-tenancy over an L3 underlay.
  • ToR / EoR / MoR: top-of-rack / end-of-row / middle-of-row switch placement.
  • Scale-up / scale-out: in-rack NVLink domain vs the between-rack fabric.
  • Rail: same-index NIC position across servers, sharing one leaf.
  • Scalable Unit (SU): the repeatable building block of an AI cluster (one NVL72 rack).
  • RoCEv2: RDMA over Converged Ethernet, needing PFC/ECN for losslessness.
  • SHARP: InfiniBand in-network reduction of collective operations.
  • UEC / UET: Ultra Ethernet Consortium and its transport protocol.
  • DAC / ACC / AEC / AOC: passive copper / active copper / active-electrical (retimed) / active optical cable.
  • OSFP / QSFP-DD: the two 800G pluggable optic form factors.
  • CPO / LPO: co-packaged optics / linear-drive pluggable optics.
  • DR/FR/LR/SR: parallel-SMF 500 m / duplex-SMF 2 km / duplex-SMF 10 km / multimode short-reach optics.
  • MPO / VSFF (MMC/SN/CS): multi-fiber push-on connector / very-small-form-factor high-density connectors.
  • OS2 / OM4: single-mode / multimode fiber grades.
  • MMR: meet-me room where carriers and tenants cross-connect.
  • Cross-connect: a jumper joining two customers' panels; the colo interconnection revenue line.
  • DCI / 400ZR/800ZR: data-center interconnect / coherent pluggables for metro/regional DWDM.
  • OOB: out-of-band management network, physically separate from production.
  • BMC / IPMI / Redfish: baseboard management controller and its management interfaces.
  • TIA-942 spaces (ER/MDA/IDA/HDA/ZDA/EDA): the standard's cabling distribution hierarchy.
  • RCDD: Registered Communications Distribution Designer (the Division 27 stamp).
  • Division 27: the construction spec division for communications/structured cabling.

What Surprised the Researcher

  1. Optics are the story, not switches. That optical transceivers are ~50% of switch power and cost, ~60% of AI-network cost, and ~6 per GPU means the "network" is really an optics-and-fiber problem wearing a switching costume. It reframes the whole build.
  2. Copper reach is now architecture. The collapse of passive DAC to ~2 m at 800G quietly dictates rack adjacency and leaf placement — a physics limit reaching up to redraw the floor plan.
  3. The crossover already happened. InfiniBand went from >80% of AI back-end share (late 2023) to losing the lead to Ethernet in 2025 — faster than almost anyone predicted, and largely because hyperscalers voted with Ethernet.
  4. Meta's OOB shared fate. The 2021 outage is remembered as a BGP story, but the sharper lesson is that even Meta's out-of-band path depended on the thing it was meant to rescue — the strongest possible argument for genuine OOB independence.
  5. Interconnection is a franchise, not a utility. That cross-connects — literally a fiber jumper priced at ~$100–$300/month — are 19% of Equinix's recurring revenue shows how much of colocation economics lives in the patch panel, not the power bill.
