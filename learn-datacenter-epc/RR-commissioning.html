Proving, Running and Retiring a New-Build Data Center: Commissioning, Operations and Decommissioning (2025–2026)

The Desktop Research report for Topic 11, run 2026-09-25 from briefs/ and published as delivered: no editing, no lint. The chapter and appendix were distilled from it; where they and this report disagree, the chapter is the course's position.

Scope note. This is Topic 11 ("handover") of a 12-topic course. It assumes the reader knows the reference facility (1,000-rack, ~40 MW IT colocation campus outside a hot US metro; Tier III concurrently maintainable with one optional 2N suite; design-build with owner-furnished long-lead gear; ~10 MW phases; Phase 1 live ~T+42 months; capex $400–500M ex-IT; opex ≈$65M/yr of which ≈$34M electricity; OPR targets PUE ≤1.4 and WUE ≤0.2 L/kWh) and treats generators, UPS and CDUs as things to be proven, operated and retired, not designed. Every worked number is indicative.

TL;DR

  • A new-build data center is proven through a five-level commissioning ladder that climbs from the factory bench (Level 1) to a full "pull-the-plug" integrated systems test (Level 5), witnessed by an owner's independent commissioning agent; it is accepted at substantial completion against punch lists, retainage and performance guarantees; run under a disciplined regime of MOPs/SOPs/EOPs, preventive maintenance and 24/7 staffing that is the single biggest lever on reliability (per Uptime Institute Annual Outage Analysis 2025, published May 6, 2025: "Nearly 40% of organizations have suffered a major outage caused by human error over the past three years. Of these incidents, 85% stem from staff failing to follow procedures or from flaws in the processes and procedures themselves."); and retired through certified data sanitization (NIST SP 800-88) and ITAD with unbroken chain of custody.
  • The dominant risk has shifted from "will the steel stand up" to "will the operators follow the procedure and will the liquid loop be clean" — the Uptime Institute Annual Outage Analysis 2025 reports that "50% of data centers experienced at least one impactful outage over the past three years, down from 53% in 2024," while "outages from IT and networking issues increased in 2024, totaling 23% of impactful outages," and AI liquid cooling makes commissioning of CDUs and coolant loops the fastest-changing part of the whole handover.
  • For the reference 10 MW Phase 1, budget roughly 12–16 weeks of Levels 3–5 commissioning with ~10 MW of rented load banks, a day-one operations headcount around the mid-20s growing toward ~50–60 at 40 MW, and an annual labor cost that is a minor line against the ≈$65M opex dominated by the ≈$34M electricity bill.

1. Commissioning Levels 1–5

1.1 The five levels defined

Data-center commissioning is organized not the way ASHRAE Guideline 0 describes general construction (by pre-design/design/construction/occupancy phases) but into numbered Levels whose verification scope widens from a single component to the whole integrated plant. This is an industry convention, not an ASHRAE scheme: as CxPlanner puts it, "Rather than describing the process the way ASHRAE Guideline 0 does for general construction, data center projects organise testing into numbered levels… Instead of talking about an OPR and a BOD, the data center industry talks about Levels." Five is standard; some programs stretch from Level 0 (design review) to Level 6 (handover/turnover) or Level 7 (a first-year check-in).

Level ladder (the industry-standard five):

  1. Level 1 — Factory Witness Testing (FWT/FAT). Components proven on the manufacturer's bench before shipment (switchgear, UPS, gensets, CDUs). Sometimes tagged "red tag."
  2. Level 2 — Site delivery / receiving inspection. Delivered gear checked against the submittal for transport damage before anything is bolted down ("yellow tag").
  3. Level 3 — Start-up and pre-functional checks. Each device installed, terminated, energized and started individually in place ("green tag").
  4. Level 4 — Functional performance testing (per system). Each system energizes and runs its own sequence of operations under normal and fault conditions, usually on load banks ("blue tag").
  5. Level 5 — Integrated Systems Testing (IST). All systems tested together at design load, riding simulated failures — the "black-building" or "pull-the-plug" test ("white tag"). "A typical Level 5 test simulates a full power failure — a blackout test — to confirm the UPS units carry the load and the generators start exactly as designed" (CxPlanner).

How ASHRAE and NETA map on. ASHRAE Guideline 0-2019 and ASHRAE/IES Standard 202 (the Commissioning Process; Std 202-2013 defines the minimum acceptable Cx while Guideline 0 defines best-practice order of phases) supply the wrapping process — OPR, BOD, the Cx plan, functional test requirements, the systems manual and training — that the Levels execute against. Guideline 0's four phases (pre-design, design, construction, occupancy/operations) map roughly as: OPR/BOD and design review → Level 0; construction-phase functional testing → Levels 3–4; and occupancy/operations verification → Levels 5–6. For electrical acceptance, the governing document is ANSI/NETA ATS-2025 (Standard for Acceptance Testing Specifications for Electrical Power Equipment and Systems, ANSI-approved 20 February 2025), which specifies the pre-energization field tests and inspections for transformers, switchgear, breakers, protective relays, cables and — new in the 2025 edition — Section 7.28 Battery Energy Storage Systems and UPS. NETA acceptance testing should be performed by a NETA Accredited Company (NAC) with certified technicians; it is the "last line of defense before commissioning."

1.2 Data-center specifics

The Cx plan and specification. The Cx plan and the commissioning specification (a division of the construction spec) name what is commissioned, the test scripts, the acceptance criteria, and who witnesses. Scripts are written to the specific topology — "the plan and the project specification, not a generic standard, set the actual scenarios, the load, and the acceptance criteria" (Anvilfield).

Load banks. Levels 4–5 need artificial load because there is no live IT yet. - Resistive load banks provide pure kW for thermal and generator testing; reactive (or resistive/reactive) units add kVAR to test power factor and alternator/AVR response; DC units test battery strings and rectifiers; MV units test transformers and utility entry. - Sizing: enough to reach design load in steps — a 10 MW hall needs on the order of 10 MW of load bank capacity, staged up from zero to full. - Where they plug in: resistive trailers (up to ~3 MW each) on the pad or at busway/PDU tap-offs; server-emulating 4–20 kW units on the actual rack PDUs (C19/C20); liquid-cooled ~1 MW units into the chilled-water/CDU loop for AI pods. - Rental cost (2026 indicative, equipment-only): a 3 MW-class trailer runs roughly $1,100–$2,000/day, $2,600–$5,200/week, or $7,800–$14,500/month (CountBricks, Austin, 2026); a specialized liquid-cooled/high-capacity unit can rent around $18,000/week against a $400,000+ purchase price (PW Consulting). For a full hall, expect a Northern-Virginia-style 800 kW unit at roughly $18,000–$25,000 for a 48-hour rental inclusive of fuel and technician support.

Heat-load and thermal testing. Server simulators/load banks are distributed across the hall to build heat load, and thermal mapping confirms containment, CRAH/fan-wall performance and hot-spots against the OPR temperature band. For AI pods, liquid-loop flushing and CDU testing is now a discrete, high-stakes workstream (see §1.5).

The black-building / "pull-the-plug" day. The Level 5 climax: with load banks at design load, the utility feed is opened on purpose to simulate a real outage. The critical sequence proven is: UPS picks up the load instantly → generators start and reach voltage/frequency → ATS/paralleling transfers load to generator → cooling rides through the gap (fans/CRAHs on UPS or generator, chilled-water thermal mass / TES holding the loop through chiller restart) → after a hold, utility is restored and load retransfers. "Pass" means the critical bus never dropped, room temperature stayed inside the OPR band throughout, and every sequence-of-operations step fired in the right order. In the room: the CxA (running/witnessing), the design-builder's QA/QC and subs, the electrical (NETA) and controls vendors, the OEM field engineers, and the owner's operations team.

1.3 Durations and cost

  • Weeks per level (10 MW hall, indicative): Levels 1–2 overlap with procurement and delivery; Level 3 start-up ≈ 3–5 weeks; Level 4 functional ≈ 4–6 weeks; Level 5 IST ≈ 2–4 weeks. A realistic Level 3–5 field window for one 10 MW hall is ~12–16 weeks.
  • Commissioning as a share of construction cost: the economics of commissioning are "often estimated to be 0.5% to 2% of the construction costs" (CxPlanner). On $400–500M ex-IT that is broadly $2–10M campus-wide, weighted to the MEP scope.
  • Load-bank + utility energy cost during Cx: load banks convert utility (or generator diesel) energy to heat for weeks; at ~10 MW run intermittently over the Cx window this is a real six-figure energy line, plus rental (§1.2) and rented fuel tanks (~$81–$120/day for 251–552-gallon units).
  • Schedule compression → defect rates: NETA's 2026 data-center analysis is explicit that "accelerated schedules, dense electrical infrastructure, and heightened performance expectations now place extraordinary pressure on acceptance testing and commissioning teams" — compression pushes teams toward shortcuts (e.g., primary-current verification instead of full secondary injection) that must be documented as deviations.

1.4 What typically fails, and who pays for re-tests

Common findings (the "crevices… the interfaces between systems," per Gravel2Gavel): sequence-of-operations errors; generator paralleling faults; ATS timing (the 10-second transfer criterion); breaker settings and coordination across large populations of LV breakers; BMS points mis-mapped; control loops hunting; and leaks in liquid loops. Uptime's own experience is that "more than 85% of all data center designs are incorrectly executed in construction" — the reason a witnessed as-built check exists at all.

Delivery/risk. The design-builder/subs typically write the level scripts to the spec; the owner's CxA reviews/approves and witnesses (often 100% of Level 4–5 activities); the vendors/subs execute. Re-tests and failed-test standby time are a commercial fault line: the construction contract should place the cost of re-tests and of idle load-bank/vendor standby on the party responsible for the failure, or the owner absorbs schedule and rental cost.

2. The Commissioning Agent, Acceptance and Handover

2.1 Independence and the CxA mechanics

The commissioning agent (CxA / commissioning authority) must be engaged by and reporting to the owner, not the design-builder — the argument for this is established in Topic 1; here the mechanics matter. The owner (or its representative) contracts the CxA directly under a separate agreement so the CxA's findings cannot be suppressed by the entity whose work is being judged. ACG (AABC Commissioning Group) enforces this structurally: its CxA credential is for professionals who are "independent and work on behalf of building owners," and ACG member firms "cannot have ties to contractors, manufacturers, or other" interested parties.

Qualifications. The recognized commissioning credentials are ACG's CxA, BCxA's CCP (Certified Commissioning Professional), NEBB's certified commissioning professional, and ASHRAE's BCxP. On data-center work, employers routinely accept 8–10 years of field commissioning experience in lieu of a degree, and value a PE and NFPA 70E. (ACG's CxA exam passed 84 of 145 candidates — a 58% pass rate — in the year ending 31 December 2025.) A design-builder's in-house Cx is tolerated only for lower-criticality scope or where the owner lacks the budget for an independent CxA — never ideal for a Tier III mission-critical hall.

2.2 Uptime Tier Certification of Constructed Facility (TCCF) vs Cx

TCCF is an independent, third-party witnessed demonstration that the built plant performs to its claimed Tier — it is not a substitute for commissioning but a layer on top. Uptime does not accept the Cx report as proof; it requires Level 5 IST to be completed first as a prerequisite, then runs its own live re-demonstration. Per Uptime's Tier Standards leadership, "Uptime Institute consultants observe the Operations team as it performs the different demonstrations, such as interrupting the incoming utility power supply to see that the UPS carries the critical load until the engine generators come on line… and that the cooling system maintains the critical load during the transition," plus "removing redundant capacity components from service and showing that… N engine generators can support the data center critical load or that the redundant chilled water distribution loop can be removed from service while still sufficiently cooling the data center." The verdict is binary — compliant with the Tier objective or not — and the team also identifies discrepancies between the design drawings and installed equipment (the as-built check).

  • Duration: a team of Uptime consultants visits "for 3–5 days, generally a little more than a month after construction of critical systems ends, allowing plenty of time for commissioning."
  • What it adds beyond Cx: an independent as-built-vs-design inspection and a standards-measured (not spec-measured) verdict; the IST failure scenarios and redundancy demonstrations "often serve as both the commissioning test and the Tier witness" (Anvilfield). Load banks are reused to simulate live IT.
  • Cost: Uptime does not publish fees. Third-party estimates for a full three-stage Tier III program range widely — roughly $150,000–$400,000 (dcgeeks), with Tier IV 30–50% higher; other estimators cite lower single-stage figures. Treat as indicative only.
  • Alternative: TIA-942 conformity/rating audits (Rated-1 to Rated-4) are performed by TIA-licensed Certification Bodies (a third-party accreditation scheme, unlike Uptime's self-issued certificate), cover the full facility (site, architecture, security, fire, telecom, electrical, mechanical) with separate ratings per domain, and are valid for 3 years; third-party cost estimates run lower (≈$20,000–$50,000 initial).

2.3 Acceptance as the payment gate

  • Substantial vs final completion. Substantial completion = the facility is usable for its intended purpose and entitles the contractor to the contract balance minus the value of remaining punch-list work; it typically starts the warranty and stops delay liquidated damages. Final completion = all punch items closed and conditions for final payment met. Data-center contracts should tie substantial completion to defined milestones — integrated systems / failover testing, energization, PUE and uptime metrics — not "generic 'substantial completion' language" (Gravel2Gavel).
  • Retainage. Typically 5–10% of completed-work value withheld; a common pattern is 5% with half released at completion and half at the end of the defects liability period (often 12 months).
  • Liquidated damages. Delay LDs (a daily/weekly sum for missing substantial completion) and performance LDs (for failing to hit a specified output — capacity, PUE). A distinctive data-center risk is the LD gap: SLA LDs owed to tenants can far exceed construction-contract LDs, and the owner/developer "carries the liability gap between both" (Munich Re).
  • Warranties and the OFE gap. Equipment (OEM) warranties vs workmanship warranties differ in term and start date; the owner-furnished-equipment warranty gap is acute — long-lead OFE gear (gensets, switchgear, UPS) delivered early may see its OEM warranty clock start at delivery, expiring before the hall is even live, while the design-builder disclaims responsibility for owner-supplied kit.
  • Performance guarantees. Capacity and PUE ≤1.4 are tested during Cx (design-load thermal runs) and can be conditions of final payment/LDs. EPC close-out ends with the final account, record documents and release of retainage.

2.4 Documentation and handover

The handover package: O&M manuals, record ("as-built") drawings, the Cx report, the systems manual (ASHRAE Guideline 1.x deliverable), training hours for operators, the operator embedded during Cx, and increasingly a BIM/digital-twin model. What most often goes missing: complete, corrected as-builts (field changes not back-drafted), final BMS/EPMS point lists and sequences, vendor start-up reports, and settings/coordination study files — the exact data operations needs on day one. A best practice is to embed the operations team during Level 4–5 so they learn the plant by testing it.

3. Operations: Procedures, Maintenance and People

3.1 MOPs, SOPs and EOPs

These are the core site procedures (BICSI 009 requires SOPs, MOPs and EOPs). - SOP (Standard Operating Procedure): the high-level, repeatable way routine work is done (e.g., rounds, ticket creation, BMS operation). "The goal of an SOP is consistency." - MOP (Method of Procedure): a highly detailed, step-by-step script for a specific action that changes the state of a critical component (e.g., "replace UPS power module 2 while maintaining N+1 operation"). "The purpose of an MOP is to control actions to ensure the desired outcome" (Uptime). An SOP may contain several MOPs. - EOP (Emergency Operating Procedure): the response to an abnormal/failure condition — how to reach a safe state, restore redundancy and isolate the fault; must be usable under pressure.

Structure, approval, review. A good MOP carries objective, scope, roles/responsibilities, document control (owner, version, dates), prerequisites/approvals, tools, step-by-step actions with expected result per step, and a rollback plan. All change-bearing MOPs pass through change management and a change advisory board (CAB); MOPs are reviewed on a cycle and after any failed check, incident or system change. A 10 MW Tier III hall carries dozens of MOPs/SOPs/EOPs (one per critical evolution and failure mode — UPS to bypass, generator test, chiller changeover, ATS operation, leak response, EPO, fire alarm, etc.).

Incident management uses severity levels and root-cause analysis. Uptime's outage data is the reference: for 2024 (Annual Outage Analysis 2025), power remained the leading cause of impactful outages (54% of operators), cooling ~13% and network ~12%; but across all outages regardless of severity, IT and networking was 53% — attributed to change-management issues and misconfigurations. Human error is a contributing factor in ~two in five major outages: nearly 40% of organizations suffered a major human-error outage in the past three years, and per Uptime's 2025 report as reported by DPS Telecom, "58% of human error-related outages were caused by staff failing to follow established procedures. That's a significant jump up from 48% the year prior." The good news: 50% of operators had an impactful outage in the last three years (down from 53%), and only ~10% of outages cause serious/severe disruption.

3.2 Preventive maintenance

  • Intervals and philosophy. OEM intervals set the floor; mature operators layer reliability-centered maintenance (RCM) and condition-based maintenance (CBM). The maintenance-vs-risk debate is real — maintenance activity is itself an outage cause (human error during work), so every PM is a risk trade-off governed by MOPs and concurrent maintainability.
  • NFPA 70B became a standard (mandatory language) in its 2023 edition — electrical equipment maintenance is no longer merely "recommended practice." Tools include infrared (thermographic) scanning and breaker servicing; electrical maintenance testing follows ANSI/NETA MTS-2023.
  • Generator testing. NFPA 110 Chapter 8 requires weekly EPSS inspection, a monthly ≥30-minute run at ≥30% of nameplate kW (or the manufacturer's minimum exhaust-gas temperature) to avoid wet stacking, monthly ATS operation, an annual supplemental load-bank test (50% for 30 min, then 75% for 1 hour, per the 2025 edition) if the monthly 30% isn't met on building load, and a 4-hour continuous test every 36 months for Level 1 systems. The catch for a hot-metro campus: the air permit's run-hour cap limits how much the diesels can actually run for testing.
  • Battery maintenance. VRLA needs regular inspection (weekly electrolyte/voltage per NFPA 110), with life roughly halved for every ~8°C above 25°C; lithium-ion (increasingly the UPS chemistry per BICSI 002-2019) has a BMS and different inspection/thermal-runaway considerations.
  • Concurrent maintainability in practice. Tier III topology means any component can be removed for service without dropping IT load — but only if the MOP, the isolation, and the redundancy are actually respected. This is where design meets discipline.

3.3 Operations certifications

  • Uptime TCOS (Tier Certification of Operational Sustainability): for facilities that already hold TCCF; rated Bronze/Silver/Gold; assesses management, operations activities and building characteristics; validity two-year cycle.
  • Uptime M&O (Management & Operations) Stamp of Approval: independent of Tier status; assesses staffing/organization, maintenance/operations and management/planning; two-year validity. Per Uptime Institute, "More than 400 data centers globally have been awarded the M&O Stamp of Approval"; the enhanced M&O program (announced Nov 7, 2024) added seven new scope areas and a proprietary staff-assessment tool. Both target the #1 cause of downtime — human error.
  • BICSI 009 (2019, updated 2024) is the open-consensus operations and maintenance standard — the operational complement to design standard BICSI 002 — covering SOPs/MOPs/EOPs, staffing, maintenance and assessment across 10 chapters.

3.4 Staffing and the labor shortage

  • The shortage. Uptime forecast global data-center staff needs rising from ~2.0M FTE (2019) to ~2.3M (2025), with roughly 300,000 net new staff needed 2019–2025 (Asia-Pacific 140,000 + North America 84,200 + EMEA 81,500), per Uptime Institute's "Global Data Center Staffing Forecast 2021-2025" (Jan 26, 2020): requirements grow "from about 2.0 million full-time employee equivalents in 2019 to nearly 2.3 million in 2025." The "silver tsunami" of retirements compounds it. In 2025 operations management was the job category with the most serious skills gap (cited by 39% of operators), and nearly two-thirds of operators report difficulty retaining staff, finding candidates, or both. CBRE's 2025 North America report flags labor as a top-three risk on every major hyperscale build; ~42% of operators reported losing key staff to competitors offering 15–25% raises (notably Northern Virginia and Phoenix).
  • Headcount per MW. Highly automated hyperscale campuses (>100 MW) can run with as few as 20–30 permanent staff per 100 MW; smaller/enterprise or high-density colo sites need substantially more. A 24/7 site needs a minimum of 4–5 shift crews to cover 168 hours/week with coverage, holidays and leave.
  • In-house vs outsourced. Many operators outsource facilities management to CBRE, JLL and similar; the trade-off is cost and scalability vs. direct control of the #1 risk (procedure adherence).
  • Remote / lights-out / AI-assisted. Claims outrun practice: Uptime 2025 finds operators will trust AI for analyzing sensor data and predictive maintenance but not for configuration changes, controlling equipment or managing staff. Remote hands and NOC-from-afar are real; fully "lights-out" critical operations are not, in 2026, mainstream for Tier III colo.

3.5 Vendors, spares, SLAs and risk

  • Service contracts carry defined response times (critical issues often 15–30 minutes to respond); OEM coverage for gensets (Cummins, Caterpillar), UPS (Schneider, Vertiv, Eaton) and chillers.
  • Critical spares. The long-lead spare problem is acute in 2025–2026 — switchgear, transformers and large UPS modules have lead times of many months to years, so critical spares must be stocked on site or contractually guaranteed.
  • Colocation SLA. Typically promises power availability (often 99.999%+, i.e., <~5 minutes/year for redundant power), a temperature/humidity band (commonly ASHRAE allowable, e.g., 18–27°C / 64.4–80.6°F), and service credits (e.g., 1/30th of monthly recurring charge per breach event, escalating with duration) as the remedy. Uptime is reported to tenants via monthly reports and portals.
  • Operational risk after handover. Once accepted, the operator carries operational risk; SLA credits flow from operator to tenant when bands are breached, funded from the operator's opex — which is why procedure discipline and maintainability are direct financial levers.

4. Decommissioning

4.1 IT asset disposition (ITAD)

The ITAD flow: decommission ticket → de-install → serialized inventory → data sanitization/destruction → value recovery (remarketing) or recycling → certificate of destruction + downstream disclosures. Every outcome is recorded against the device serial number. Refresh cycles: servers 3–5 years (AI/GPU gear compressing to 18–36 months as H100→H200→next-gen transitions accelerate), storage and network similar-to-longer. At hyperscale, drive retirement is a massive continuous operation — the value-recovery clock matters because "server residual values fall monthly while a project deliberates" (Powergen). The data-center decommissioning services market was cited at $12.95B in 2026 growing ~7.37% CAGR, driven by AI-compressed lifecycles.

4.2 Data sanitization

  • NIST SP 800-88 is the reference. Rev. 2 was published 26 September 2025 and Rev. 1 (2014) was formally withdrawn the same day. Rev. 2 keeps the three methods (Clear / Purge / Destroy) but removes the media-specific technique tables, deferring device technique selection to IEEE 2883-2022 and NSA/CSS specs; it reorients around running a documented sanitization program, expands cryptographic-erase guidance, and adds a formal distinction between verification (the process ran) and validation (no data is actually recoverable). It confirms a single-pass overwrite suffices for Clear on HDDs and directs that Purge be used instead of Clear when possible; and it states overwriting SSDs is inadequate (wear-leveling/over-provisioning), so SSD/NVMe Purge relies on cryptographic erase or firmware sanitize commands. Degaussing is not an approved Destroy technique in Rev. 2 and does nothing to flash.
  • Tenant contracts typically require NIST 800-88-compliant sanitization plus a serialized certificate.
  • Chain of custody. Serialized tracking, sealed/tamper-evident transport, and witnessed destruction (on-site mobile shred so media never leaves unsanitized, or documented transport to a certified facility). Vendor credentials: R2v3 (SERI — environmental + chain of custody + downstream vendor management), e-Stewards (adds strict export bans), and NAID AAA (i-SIGMA — unannounced audits of physical security and destruction efficacy). "NIST 800-88 establishes what was done, NAID AAA establishes that someone outside your vendor verified it." Where breaches happen: most ITAD data breaches occur at downstream vendors/subcontractors, not the primary processor — a certification held by your vendor "doesn't extend to subcontractor operations."

4.3 Facility decommissioning and repurposing

  • MEP end-of-life spans (indicative): UPS electronics/frame ~8–15 years (batteries far sooner — VRLA ~4–5, needing replacement 2–3× over a UPS life; capacitors ~5–7 years); chillers ~15–20 years (major rebuild at 10–15); CRAC/CRAH ~12 years; diesel generators ~25–30 years; switchgear ~15–25 years; chilled-water piping ~30 years.
  • Repurposing a hall for higher density is now the dominant driver: retrofitting air-cooled halls for liquid-cooled AI pods means new CDUs, piping and power — and, critically, hot work / hot taps beside live white space (see §5.2).
  • Lease-end restoration ("make-good") obligations return the space to a defined condition.
  • Environmental obligations: refrigerants (F-gas / EPA Section 608), diesel fuel tanks (spill/SPCC, closure), lithium-ion batteries (transport/disposal hazards), SF6 in older switchgear, dielectric fluids/coolants from liquid systems, and general e-waste.
  • Delivery/risk: who pays for decommissioning is set in the lease/contract; tenant move-out mechanics must sequence data destruction before GCs and buyers walk the space; the ITAD contract allocates liability, requires certifications, and defines the certificate/chain-of-custody deliverables.

5. How Operations Feeds Back Into the Next Build

5.1 Lessons-learned and the reference-design loop

Hyperscalers run standard reference designs; colos run product platforms. Operations feeds the next phase's OPR and BOD through structured lessons-learned: Cx findings databases (recurring defects → design changes), operating data (PUE/WUE actuals vs targets, failure history), and digital twins updated from as-builts and BMS telemetry. The whole point of a phased campus is that Phase 1's commissioning findings and first-year operating experience harden the design for Phases 2–4.

5.2 Building Phases 2–4 beside a live Hall 1

Commissioning a new phase differs fundamentally from greenfield because Hall 1 is live: - Construction and IST happen next to operating white space under strict rules — isolation of shared systems, temporary barriers, vibration/dust/EMI control, and MOP-governed tie-ins. - Hot work in a live hall (welding for piping tie-ins) is "an extraordinary operational risk and often an unacceptable process" (Data Center Frontier), pushing operators toward press-fit/flame-free joining to extend liquid loops in the aisle without fire permits or fumes. - Level 5 IST for a new phase must prove the new load rides failures without disturbing the live phase — shared utility, generator plant and chilled-water headers make the "pull-the-plug" test far more constrained than on an empty greenfield.

Level schedule (indicative):

  1. Levels 1–2 (parallel to build): FWT at OEM works for gensets, UPS, switchgear, CDUs; site receiving inspections as gear lands.
  2. Level 3 — start-up (~4 weeks): energize and start each device; NETA ATS acceptance on all electrical gear before energization.
  3. Level 4 — functional (~5 weeks): each system's sequence under normal + fault, on load banks; generator paralleling, ATS timing, chiller/CDU functional, BMS point-to-point.
  4. Level 5 — IST (~3 weeks): full-hall integrated failure testing at design load.
  5. Total field window ≈ 12–16 weeks.

Load bank plan: ~10 MW total — a mix of 3 MW resistive/reactive trailers on the pad and at busway tap-offs for the air-cooled halls, server-emulating rack units on PDUs, and ~1 MW liquid-cooled units into the CDU loop for the AI pods. Staged 0 → 25% → 50% → 75% → 100% of hall design load.

People on site during IST (indicative): CxA lead + 2–3 commissioning engineers; NETA crew (3–4); controls/BMS integrator (2); OEM field engineers for genset, UPS, chiller, CDU (4–6); design-builder QA/QC + subs (6–10); owner's operations team embedded (4–6); plus Uptime consultants (3–5) if TCCF runs immediately after — ~25–35 people.

The IST "pull-the-plug" scenario, step by step (pass criteria in brackets): 1. Load banks at 100% Hall-1 design load; all systems normal. [Stable at design load] 2. Open the utility feed at the main switchboard (simulated outage). [Detected instantly] 3. UPS carries the critical load on battery. [Critical bus never drops; zero transfer of the IT bus] 4. Generators start, reach voltage/frequency, parallel. [Start and reach rated within the design window, typically ≤10 s to available power] 5. ATS/paralleling transfers load to generator. [Clean transfer; no bus disturbance] 6. Cooling rides through the gap — fans/CRAHs on UPS/gen, chilled-water thermal mass/TES holds the loop through chiller restart. [Room stays within OPR band throughout] 7. Hold on generator at full load (30+ min). [Stable; temps in band; no alarms] 8. Restore utility; retransfer load; UPS recharges. [Clean retransfer; return to normal] - "Pass" = the critical load never dropped, room temperature stayed inside the OPR band the whole time, and every SOO step fired in order. For TCCF, Uptime additionally witnesses concurrent-maintainability demos (take each redundant genset/chiller/loop offline while N carries the load).

6.3 One MOP in full structure — "Move UPS Block A to maintenance bypass (Tier III hall)"

  • Title / ID / version / dates: MOP-EL-014 Rev 3; effective 2026-01-15; next review 2026-07-15.
  • Objective: Transfer UPS Block A to maintenance bypass to enable filter/capacitor PM while maintaining conditioned power to the critical load.
  • Scope: UPS Block A (2–3 MW module) and its static/maintenance bypass; Hall 1 A-side only.
  • Roles: Lead technician (executes); second technician (verifies each step); shift engineer (authorizes); NOC (monitors); vendor FE (on call).
  • Risk / redundancy state: During the evolution the A-side load is on bypass (utility-derived, unconditioned) — redundancy is reduced; B-side must be confirmed healthy and no B-side work permitted. CAB-approved; scheduled in low-risk window.
  • Prerequisites: B-side UPS confirmed normal at full capacity; batteries healthy; EOP for "loss of bypass source" staged; comms check with NOC; PPE per NFPA 70E; no concurrent A-side MOPs.
  • Tools: approved single-line, torque tools, meter, breaker rack tool.
  • Steps (each with expected result + verify initials): (1) Notify NOC/tenants of maintenance window. (2) Confirm B-side healthy [B-side at 100%]. (3) Verify UPS A load and bypass source live [voltage present]. (4) Command UPS A to bypass per OEM sequence [load on static bypass, no disturbance]. (5) Rack/close maintenance bypass breaker [closed, confirmed]. (6) Open UPS input/output isolators [UPS isolated, load on maintenance bypass]. (7) Confirm critical bus stable [no drop; NOC concurs]. (8) Perform PM. (9) Reverse sequence to return UPS A to normal [UPS online, bypass opened, redundancy restored].
  • Rollback: at any failed check, stop and revert to the last known-good state per the embedded EOP; escalate to shift engineer/vendor.
  • Completion: NOC notified; log updated; redundancy confirmed restored.

6.5 Decommissioning plan for a 50-rack tenant move-out

  1. Notice & scope: tenant issues decommission ticket; 50 racks inventoried to serial number; ITAD vendor (R2v3 + NAID AAA) engaged; chain-of-custody plan agreed.
  2. Data first: sanitize per NIST SP 800-88 Rev. 2 — SSD/NVMe by cryptographic erase or firmware sanitize (overwrite inadequate); HDD by single-pass overwrite (Clear) or Purge; witnessed on-site shred for anything leaving unsanitized. Verification + validation; serialized certificates issued.
  3. De-install: power down per MOP; disconnect (for liquid racks, drain/cap CDU manifolds under MOP, flame-free where tie-ins needed); remove cabling; protect adjacent live racks.
  4. Custody & transport: sealed, tamper-evident, serialized manifests; downstream vendor disclosures.
  5. Value recovery/recycle: remarket viable gear promptly (residual value falls monthly); recycle the rest through certified downstream partners — nothing landfilled.
  6. Space make-good: restore cage/space per lease; reconcile power/cooling capacity back to the campus pool for re-lease or higher-density repurposing.
  7. Close-out: certificates, asset-disposition report and updated DCIM/records to the tenant; liability released per the ITAD contract.

7. Open Questions & What's Changing Fast in 2026

  • Labor shortage: operations-management skills gap is now the worst category (39% of operators, 2025); retention is the binding constraint, and AI-density builds need scarcer high-voltage and liquid-cooling skills.
  • AI-assisted & remote operations: trusted for sensor analytics/predictive maintenance, not for control or config changes — the "lights-out data center" remains largely aspirational for Tier III colo in 2026.
  • Commissioning of liquid cooling: the fastest-moving handover topic — "flush and fill" is now considered inadequate; CDU FWT, engineered flushing to low-single-digit-micron cleanliness, flow/balance proof, and leak integrity are becoming a distinct discipline. Vertiv (NYSE: VRT) completed its ~$1.0 billion acquisition of Houston-based PurgeRite (from Milton Street Capital) on December 4, 2025, per Vertiv/PR Newswire: "the approximately $1.0 billion acquisition enhances Vertiv's thermal management services capabilities and strengthens its position as a global leader in next-generation thermal chain services for liquid cooling systems." Vendor-responsibility boundaries between server OEM, CDU maker and fluid supplier remain unsettled.
  • NIST SP 800-88 Rev. 2 (Sept 2025): the program-and-validation reorientation, deference to IEEE 2883, and SSD-specific guidance are still propagating into tenant contracts and audit expectations.
  • ITAD at hyperscale: 18–36-month AI refresh cycles are flooding the retirement pipeline; the custody hand-off at the colo floor (when does colo custody end and ITAD's begin?) is an unresolved exposure.
  • Outage trends: frequency and severity keep declining, but human error and IT/networking causes are rising and per-incident cost is climbing — the operational-discipline dividend is real but fragile as complexity grows.

8. Glossary

  • FWT/FAT (Level 1): factory witness/acceptance test — components proven on the OEM bench before shipping.
  • IST (Level 5): integrated systems test — all systems tested together at load, riding simulated failures.
  • Black-building / pull-the-plug test: deliberate full power loss at design load to prove ride-through.
  • Load bank: device that applies artificial electrical (or thermal) load; resistive (kW), reactive (kVAR), DC, or liquid-cooled.
  • CxA (commissioning authority/agent): owner's independent party who plans, witnesses and verifies commissioning.
  • NETA ATS-2025: standard for field acceptance testing of electrical power equipment before energization.
  • TCCF: Uptime Institute Tier Certification of Constructed Facility — witnessed demonstration the built plant meets its Tier.
  • TCOS / M&O Stamp: Uptime operational certifications (Tier-track / independent) assessing management and operations.
  • BICSI 009: open-consensus data-center operations and maintenance standard (complement to design standard BICSI 002).
  • MOP / SOP / EOP: method of procedure (state-changing step-by-step) / standard operating procedure (routine) / emergency operating procedure (abnormal response).
  • CAB: change advisory board — approves change-bearing procedures.
  • Concurrent maintainability: any component removable for service without dropping IT load (Tier III attribute).
  • NFPA 110 Ch. 8 / NFPA 70B / NFPA 70E: emergency-power testing / electrical equipment maintenance (a standard since 2023) / electrical safety.
  • Retainage: percentage (5–10%) of payment withheld until completion/defects-liability end.
  • Liquidated damages: pre-agreed sums for delay (delay LDs) or under-performance (performance LDs).
  • Substantial vs final completion: usable-for-purpose (payment gate, warranty start) vs all-punch-closed.
  • ITAD: IT asset disposition — secure retirement, sanitization, value recovery and recycling of IT gear.
  • NIST SP 800-88 / IEEE 2883: media-sanitization guidance (Clear/Purge/Destroy) / device-specific sanitize techniques.
  • Clear / Purge / Destroy: software erase / lab-resistant erase (reusable) / physical destruction (unusable).
  • Cryptographic erase (CE): purge by destroying the encryption key — the reliable method for SSD/NVMe.
  • R2v3 / e-Stewards / NAID AAA: ITAD certifications for recycling+custody / export ethics / audited destruction.
  • Chain of custody: serialized, sealed, documented tracking of media from de-install to final disposition.
  • OFE (owner-furnished equipment): long-lead gear the owner procures directly; source of a warranty-timing gap.

9. What Surprised the Researcher

  • The five "levels" aren't ASHRAE's. Everyone treats Levels 1–5 as gospel, but they're an industry convention layered on top of ASHRAE Guideline 0/Std 202 — and even the count is fluid (0 to 7). The precision implied by "Level 5" is partly a shared fiction.
  • Uptime witnesses, it doesn't accept paper. TCCF is not a document review of the Cx report; Uptime makes the ops team re-run the failure demonstrations live, and finds that >85% of designs are built wrong — a startling indictment of construction QA that justifies the whole witnessed-handover model.
  • Human error is the headline, and it's getting worse in relative terms. With equipment reliability improving, the residual risk is overwhelmingly procedural — ~58% of human-error outages are simply staff not following the MOP/EOP, a share that jumped ten points in a single year.
  • The OFE warranty gap is a silent money leak. Long-lead owner-furnished gear can burn through its OEM warranty sitting in a yard before the hall is even commissioned — a structural consequence of 42-month schedules and multi-year lead times.
  • Sanitization got simpler and stricter at once. NIST 800-88 Rev. 2 (Sept 2025) dropped the detailed per-media tables (deferring to IEEE 2883) while raising the bar on documentation, validation and SSD handling — and quietly confirmed that a single overwrite pass is enough for HDDs, ending decades of three-pass folklore.
  • Load banks are now liquid. The commissioning supply chain has had to invent ~1 MW liquid-cooled load banks that dump heat into the CDU loop, because you can't prove a 120 kW AI pod's cooling with a resistive trailer in the yard — a neat illustration of how AI density reshapes even the test equipment.
