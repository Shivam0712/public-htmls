What a site must provide, in 2025–26 order

The ranking inverted. For decades the checklist led with connectivity. CBRE's 2026 outlook states that "power availability and certainty are now primary site selection criteria, overshadowing traditional connectivity considerations," and frames a market benchmark as the ability to secure 300 MW-plus deliveries in under 36 months (CBRE, U.S. Data Center Outlook 2026). That benchmark describes competition for very large sites. It is not a qualification threshold for this course's campus, whose planned full-site demand is about 56 MW at the utility connection. JLL calls power "the primary site-selection factor, with average grid-connection wait times in primary markets exceeding four years" (JLL, Global Data Center Outlook 2026). Bloom Energy's mid-2025 survey of 44 hyperscale and colocation developers found 84% placing power in their top three criteria; its November 2025 survey of 92 respondents found 51% (Bloom Energy, Data Center Power Report, June and November 2025). The chapter therefore cites the ranking, not the percentage. Practitioner order: power (availability, timeline, cost), fiber, land, water, hazard exposure, tax and incentive climate, workforce, community acceptance (research synthesis of CBRE, JLL, Bloom, Data Center Watch).

The Phoenix candidate. The chapter illustrates these screens with an option on roughly 120 contiguous acres near Phoenix. Its map shows a nearby 138 kV line, possible fiber entries and a possible reclaimed-water connection. None establishes spare grid capacity, an approved tap, independent fiber routes, allocated water, sewer capacity or permission to build. The first phase seeks roughly 14 MW at the utility connection; four phases reach roughly 56 MW of planned demand. The utility must study the proposed service ramp (§3).

The 18–24 month versus 4+ year tension. CBRE reports developers "sourcing parcels within close proximity to substations and locking in power purchase agreements before finalizing land acquisition," with the most sought-after sites offering power in 18–24 months (CBRE, North America Data Center Trends H1 2025). JLL's 4+ years describes typical primary-market waits. The first is a favorable powered-land case, the second a constrained-market observation. Proximity alone does not make the Phoenix candidate powered land; its shorter illustrative schedule in §12 depends on studies, agreements and construction succeeding.

Land. The average data center land transaction reached 224 acres in 2024, up 144% since 2022 (Cushman & Wakefield via TechTarget); weighted-average land cost was about $244,000 an acre in October 2024 (Gravel2Gavel). A 100 MW facility "might occupy roughly 20 to over 100 acres" depending on density; multi-phase campuses assemble 100–1,000+ acres; "large, unbroken tracts are far more valuable than the same acreage split by roads, easements, or separate parcels" (Passive Investments, developer practice summary). Rising rack density can shrink acres per MW (CBRE), but power, not floor area, is often the binding number. Industrial zoning is the first gate only: data centers may need rezoning, special or conditional-use permits, height variances for cooling equipment and switchgear, noise-ordinance compliance and setbacks. CBRE warns that development outside urban cores "will require rezoning and entitlements" (CBRE, 2025 outlook).

Hazards. FEMA floodways and 100-year Special Flood Hazard Areas are "generally disqualifying," and partial exposure "complicates insurance, financing, and tenant approval"; serious developers screen to the 500-year (0.2% annual chance) floodplain (Passive Investments). Seismic zones raise structural cost. Wildfire smoke fouls air-side economizers and forces intakes closed, degrading free cooling. Extreme heat raises cooling energy and water use when the grid is most stressed. Climate sets free-cooling hours and therefore operating cost: Phoenix measurements show water-cooled chillers achieving lower energy but higher water use and air-cooled systems the inverse (arXiv survey of 15 US climate zones). At the site stage this is the water-versus-energy decision (§7); the plant is Topic 7.

Fiber. Carrier diversity avoids monopoly pricing and single-provider outages. Two physically separate entries from different directions are the requirement; paths sharing a conduit or bridge crossing have a hidden single point of failure. Proximity to exchanges and long-haul routes cuts latency and backhaul cost, but fiber now follows power: providers including Fiberlight, Zayo and Flo Networks are building "thousands of route miles of new fiber" in West Texas to reach power-rich sites (CBRE, North America Data Center Trends H2 2025).

Load interconnection is not generator interconnection

A data center is a large electricity consumer, not a generating facility. White & Case summarized the 2025 distinction this way: "interconnection processes for new generating facilities are under FERC jurisdiction while the interconnection of large loads is conducted at the state level" (White & Case, 2025). That is useful historical shorthand, not a complete jurisdictional test for every project. The serving utility normally leads the load request under applicable utility and state procedures; a transmission owner or regional grid operator may also review its wider transmission effects. Generator interconnection was standardised federally through FERC Order 2023 and cluster studies. Load interconnection lacked a uniform equivalent, and utilities handled large loads "on an ad hoc basis" (RMI; GridLab), which strained under the 2024–26 surge. The processes overlap when a load triggers transmission upgrades or a project combines load with on-site generation.

Federal overlay, changing in 2025–26. DOE directed FERC on 23 October 2025 to accelerate large-load interconnection and permit joint co-located filings; after its 18 June 2026 meeting FERC directed six RTOs to write region-specific large-load rules. The direction was toward faster studies and firmer financial commitments, but the eventual division of federal, regional and state responsibilities remained unsettled. Check the current serving utility's process rather than treating the 2025 shorthand as a permanent rule.

The six steps and their durations

Drawing on RMI's six-step framing and utility documents (RMI; AEP Ohio tariff; EPC practice), apply each step to a dated ramp: roughly 14 MW at the utility connection for the first roughly 10 MW IT hall, increasing toward roughly 56 MW of planned full-campus demand. The chapter plans a roughly 60 MW full-site service contract; neither that contract capacity nor a queue position supplies later phases automatically.

  1. Application or load-study request: the developer submits initial and later demand, dates and assumptions through the serving utility. The utility coordinates with the transmission owner and regional operator where applicable. AEP Ohio charges tiered study fees of $10,000, $50,000 and $100,000 by request size.
  2. Load or feasibility study: tests whether existing facilities can serve the proposed first delivery and ramp.
  3. System impact study: models voltage, thermal and reliability effects and identifies network upgrades. A later phase can change the required work and cost.
  4. Facilities study: engineers and prices the substation, tie lines and metering for the agreed scope.
  5. Service or construction agreement: allocates scope, costs, security, milestones and staged service dates. An agreed date remains conditional on completing the work and tests.
  6. Construction and energization: the utility builds its assigned upgrades, the customer builds its assigned substation, and the parties test, witness and energize the connection. First-phase energization is not delivery of the remaining planned demand.

Durations. Sources give twelve months to more than three years for interconnection processing; JLL and LBNL data indicate total time-to-power of 4–5+ years in constrained primary markets. The chapter's agreement near month 18 and first energization target near month 38 are scenario assumptions for this candidate, not standard study durations or guaranteed dates. A month-40 energization sometimes used as a schedule allowance is likewise an assumption, not a utility promise. LBNL's Queued Up 2025 finds that, of generation capacity requesting interconnection in 2000–2019, 13% had reached commercial operation by end-2024 and 77% withdrew (LBNL, Queued Up 2025). Those are generator outcomes, not measured completion rates for data-center loads. Treat interconnection as a day-one workstream and overlap studies with substation design and long-lead procurement (§11).

Who pays, and the substation choice

Roles and boundary. The developer pays for assigned customer-side work and utility-side work attributable to its load. The serving utility leads the request and, with the transmission owner as applicable, studies and builds assigned network work; an RTO or ISO such as PJM or ERCOT may review transmission-level impacts. Where there is no RTO, across much of the Southeast and West, the vertically integrated utility may handle those functions (research synthesis). The chapter's substation drawing shows a proposed 138 kV arrangement, not an agreed point of ownership. The service and construction contracts, not the pictured fence, establish where ownership, operation, maintenance, metering and testing responsibilities change.

Three different exposures. Contribution in aid of construction (CIAC) pays for utility-owned facilities built to serve the customer, such as taps, feeders, metering or a utility-owned substation. The customer-owned substation's transformers, switchgear, installation and testing are owner construction costs, not CIAC. Posted security or collateral backs contractual obligations and can tie up cash or credit without being spent on construction. CIAC is generally non-refundable, although some tariffs provide conditional refunds. Indicative utility-work charges: $75k–400k for 1–3 MW distribution service; $400k–2.5M for 5–20 MW with a line extension; $3M–30M+ where a dedicated substation or transmission tap is needed; eight or nine figures for hyperscale loads (Terrapin CG, 2026). Classic tariffs may refund part of an extension contribution if others later connect, for example 30% within three years; large-load versions may condition refunds on minimum load. Texas SB 6's proposed standard includes a $50,000/MW non-refundable interconnection fee, customer payment of 100% of direct interconnection costs without a usage credit, and retention of 80% of posted security on withdrawal (Texas SB 6, 2025). These are not Phoenix quotes or tariff terms.

Service level by load. Below about 20 MW a distribution feeder, typically at primary voltage of 35 kV or less, may serve. Most facilities of 20–50 MW and above need a dedicated substation; 100 MW-plus campuses almost always need transmission-level service at 115–345 kV with dual feeds (American Power Engineers, 2026). The course's rule of thumb uses a feeder under roughly 15 MW, a dedicated 69–138 kV substation at roughly 15–80 MW, and transmission-level arrangements above that. The roughly 56 MW campus proposes a customer-owned 138 kV substation and two incoming lines. These are design and request assumptions, not confirmed service. Even two lines may share a substation, corridor or other upstream failure; their independence requires study.

Ownership choice. A customer-owned substation gives the developer control over procurement and construction, along with transformer and switchgear risk. A utility-owned substation puts that build in the utility's queue and procurement programme, funded under the applicable tariff or contribution arrangement. Neither arrangement creates upstream capacity. In 2025–26 schedule-pressed developers increasingly self-built and self-procured to avoid utility equipment backlogs (research synthesis). Substation internals are Topic 5; their procurement is §10.

Bridging and behind-the-meter power

Cleanview found 92% of the behind-the-meter (BTM) projects it identified, about 82 GW, were announced since the start of 2025; AI labs had signed leases for more than 10 GW of BTM power (Cleanview, 2026). Estimates compare grid connections taking 4–7 years with gas plants that may take 18–30 months to permit and build (Jefferies; Green Gas Turbines). Neither timeline makes a gas bridge available on demand. The chapter's pictured engines beside an unenergized substation are unapproved contingency scope, not part of the Phoenix candidate's committed service or capital budget. They need separately secured gas supply, an air permit and deliverable engines, as well as an operating and reliability design.

  • Technologies. Simple-cycle and aeroderivative gas turbines include GE Vernova's planned 29 aeroderivative turbines, about 1 GW, for Project Stargate. Modular reciprocating gas engines include VoltaGrid units proposed up to 200 MW "under a minor source air permit"; that example does not grant this site a permit. Fuel cells are another option (Bloom Energy; cost and lead time not sourced in the research, verify). Batteries can support a short transfer, including the cited 30–120 seconds between a grid event and engine start. A battery is not long-term generation for months of missing grid service.
  • Costs, indicative. A 50 MW simple-cycle aeroderivative installation is estimated at about $40–60M including balance of plant; broader estimates run $0.7–1.4M/MW depending on cycle, balance of plant and gas interconnection (research synthesis). The first hall needs about 14 MW of facility power, not just 10 MW of IT power; bridge sizing and reliability need their own design.
  • Permitting gate. BTM generation still requires applicable air authorization. Microsoft/Nebius's proposed 400 MW on-site gas plant in New Jersey struggled to obtain its permit, jeopardising tranches of a $17.4B deal. Tier 4 emissions packages can add time; staying under minor-source thresholds may offer a faster route, subject to actual emissions and local review.
  • Gas-supply and engine-delivery gates. The candidate has no secured gas service in the chapter. Cummins was "taking orders for its QSK95 generator solution into the second half of 2028," with customers considering 78, 60 and 50 litre units (Cummins, Jonas Arens, Feb 2026); Cummins targets 55 GW of high-horsepower capacity by 2030. Caterpillar reported a $63B enterprise backlog, up 79% year on year, with orders "well into 2028" (Caterpillar Q1 2026 earnings call, 30 April 2026). BTM can exchange a grid queue for gas, permit and equipment dependencies.
  • Ceiling. Cleanview models 5 GW versus 13 GW of BTM actually deployed by end-2027 depending on permit and engine delays.

PPAs and renewable matching, as siting factors only. Developers may lock PPAs before finalising land (CBRE H1 2025); 24/7 matching and additionality commitments (Google, Microsoft) favor grids where new clean generation can be added. Georgia's Customer Identified Resource tariff, approved April 2026 and capped at 3 GW through 2035, and Nevada's clean-transition tariffs allow large customers to fund generation in support of service. Sustainability accounting is out of scope.

Water as a site and permitting matter

Where the water goes. An indoor sealed liquid-cooling loop moves heat but does not determine outdoor water use: the final heat rejection may use wet towers, dry coolers or air-cooled chillers. Direct-to-chip liquid cooling likewise leaves heat to reject. Uptime Institute benchmarks a conventional evaporative system at "millions of gallons of water per year for each megawatt of IT load"; about 70–80% of its withdrawn water evaporates and 20–30% leaves as blowdown (Uptime Institute). A sealed loop paired with dry rejection can use much less on-site cooling water, with an energy penalty in hot weather; pairing it with wet rejection still consumes water. Vantage's Wisconsin closed-loop campus reports peak use near 22,000 gallons a day, about 65 homes, against about 5,000,000 for its comparable evaporative example (Vantage). That site comparison is not a universal closed-loop performance factor. Air-cooled chillers use almost no cooling water at an energy penalty; Microsoft's August 2024 zero-water design eliminates evaporative cooling and reports savings of about 33 million gallons per facility per year, with deployment in Phoenix (Microsoft).

WUE and the candidate target. Water usage effectiveness (WUE) expresses reported on-site water use in liters per kilowatt-hour of IT energy. Secondary compilations give 0.09–1.80 L/kWh for water-efficient designs and 1.99–4.00 L/kWh for traditional evaporative designs; AWS reports 0.15 L/kWh for its reporting population (arXiv survey; Introl; Avanza; AWS). These populations and reporting boundaries differ. The course's at-most-0.2 L/kWh Phoenix figure is an unmeasured design target, not an operating result, allocation or demonstrated comparison with another operator. At a constant 40 MW IT for 24 hours it would correspond arithmetically to 192,000 liters, roughly 51,000 US gallons, of on-site use per day. That daily calculation is not a peak supply requirement. A universal industry-average WUE cannot be inferred from these comparisons.

Four distinct water questions. Withdrawal is the amount taken from a source; consumption is the portion not returned locally, chiefly evaporation in a wet system. Peak supply is the volume and rate a source and its infrastructure can deliver on a demanding day, not an annual WUE result. Discharge is the remaining water sent to sewer or a permitted receiving water, subject to capacity and quality rules. For scale, one reported Google Council Bluffs example withdrew about 3.9 million gallons a day and consumed about 2.8 million (Google). Do not treat that site's quantities, or the compiled WUE ranges, as Phoenix measurements.

Sourcing. Public water systems supplied about 97% of US data centers' on-site water as of 2025 (CRS-cited analysis); potable supply is straightforward to treat but politically exposed. Reclaimed or "purple pipe" water requires a separate non-potable connection and may have competing users. Groundwater and surface withdrawals may require rights. Western prior appropriation assigns priority by date independently of land ownership, so junior rights can be curtailed first in a shortage; eastern riparian rights are tied to reasonable use of waterfront land, and California and others are hybrids. Southwest diligence therefore runs "aquifer-specific modeling, sustainable yield analysis… priority, transferability, place-of-use limitations and curtailment history" (Gravel2Gavel). Texas groundwater law is not covered by the research. The mapped reclaimed-water main at the chapter's candidate is not a supply commitment.

Discharge and sewer. Tower blowdown can go to sewer if capacity and terms allow, or to surface water under an NPDES permit with temperature and chemistry limits for thermal pollution, biocides and anti-scalants. One facility under construction was allocated 8 MGD of water and 4 MGD of wastewater, a smaller one 2 MGD and 0.57 MGD (arXiv). Available supply without sewer headroom can still stop a wet-cooled project.

Community response. The Ypsilanti Community Utilities Authority imposed a 12-month moratorium on supplying water to data centers in April 2026; Data Center Watch catalogues rezoning denials citing water. Microsoft, Vantage, AWS and Oracle have described low-water or closed-loop approaches in response to such concerns. Avanza argues that the frontier design problem is solvable; applying a design and making credible commitments before opposition forms remain separate tasks.

WUE glossary entry. WUE is reported on-site water use divided by IT energy, commonly in L/kWh. State the reporting boundary and population when comparing values; no single industry average applies to every site or cooling design.

Taxes and incentives

Exemptions and abatements are conditional, location-specific economics, usually negotiated in advance and tied to investment, jobs or wages. Sales-tax exemptions on qualifying equipment, such as servers and switchgear and sometimes electricity, are common. In FY2025 Virginia operators reported about $33.2B of exempt investment and a reported roughly $1.9B single-year benefit, against an original projection of $1.5M a year (Virginia JLARC and state reporting). Local property-tax abatements and payments in lieu of taxes (PILOTs) vary: West Virginia's 2025 law values equipment at about 5% salvage value; Nevada offers up to 75% abatements; Texas pairs a 10–15 year sales-tax exemption, subject to at least $200M investment and 20 jobs, with local Chapter 312 abatements. For an AI build, applying a 6–8% combined sales-tax rate to $25–40M/MW of qualifying equipment suggests low-single-digit millions of dollars per MW of possible relief, not a transferable project quote (verify: qualification and property terms are local and may be confidential).

The turn. Florida limited its exemption to facilities with at least 100 MW IT load on 30 June 2025; Virginia's exemption was stalling state budget negotiations; more than 300 data-center bills were filed across over 30 states in early 2026, shifting attention toward oversight. New York's Responsible Data Center Development Act had passed both chambers on 5 June 2026 but still awaited the governor's signature in the report's snapshot (verify: pending status). No Virginia, Texas or other state's benefit is an Arizona entitlement. The Phoenix candidate carries no incentive in its base-case pro forma. Even an awarded incentive supplies neither a water or air permit nor approved utility capacity or delivered power.

Permitting sequence and community risk

Overlapping approvals. Zoning checks or rezoning, a conditional or special-use hearing where required, environmental and site-plan review, water-supply and sewer decisions, building review, and air permitting for standby engines or any gas bridge can advance on overlapping tracks. Their legal order and issuing authorities vary locally. A building permit does not replace a discretionary land-use approval or an engine air permit. Noise, setbacks, drainage, discharge and operating conditions can be considered across these tracks. The common stalls are a rezoning or conditional-use hearing, water and sewer or environmental decisions, and engine air review (§6). Reported opposition-related delays average 18–24 months (Introl; Data Center Watch); that is not a promised extension for every project.

Community risk as a site screen. Data Center Watch (10a Labs) documented about $64B in value of US projects blocked or delayed by local opposition between May 2024 and March 2025, about $98–100B in projects affected in Q2 2025, and more than $130B cumulatively by its June 2026 update. These tallies describe affected project values across different reporting periods, not the chance that the Phoenix parcel will be denied. The tracker reported active opposition groups rising from 396 to 833 across 49 states in one quarter to March 2026 and cancellations rising from 2 in 2023 to 6 in 2024 and 25 in 2025 (Data Center Watch, 2025–26). Opposition spans parties, about 55% Republican and 45% Democrat among opposing politicians in the reported sample. Common arguments concern electricity rates, water, noise, property values and green space, and the low job-to-investment ratio. Documented denials include Peculiar, Missouri (September 2024); Culpeper/Brandy Station, Virginia ($12B, denied June 2024); and Tract's $14B Goodyear/Buckeye, Arizona project, withdrawn after rezoning was blocked. Developers screen for organised opposition, engage early and offer specific water, noise and PILOT commitments before filing. The candidate's pictured homes make that diligence relevant, not a prediction of its hearing outcome.

Concentration. A small group of producers supplies high-voltage transformers, including ABB, Siemens Energy, Hitachi Energy and GE Vernova; Japan, South Korea and Germany make about 70% of global GOES. Utilities' grid modernisation, renewable and storage interconnection, electrification and reshoring compete for factory capacity: "any AI campus depending on utility-supplied transformation is competing not just with other data centers, but with every utility upgrade in the country" (SAVRN). US transformer demand is projected to exceed domestic capacity by 30–40% through at least 2028, and a new transformer plant takes 3–5 years to build. Buyers "scrambling for imports and factory slots" (Reuters) also face tariff exposure on imported transformers and steel.

Ordering before design, and who carries the equipment

Three distinct milestones. A factory reservation holds a prospective production slot, subject to its terms. A purchase order places an order with specified commercial and technical obligations. Release to manufacture (RTM) authorizes fabrication after the required design information and approvals. Neither a deposit nor a PO necessarily starts the manufacturing clock. A January PO held until April RTM loses about twelve weeks before fabrication begins. Deposits of 10–30% of equipment price and freezing ratings when a one-line drawing is roughly 60% developed are indicative practices for early reservations, not requirements or promises of a completed design (Global Data Center Hub). The chapter's candidate contemplates reserving near months 3–6 and RTM near month 8 (§12).

Lead times can exceed design and permit work. Reservation agreements, slot purchases and framework or programmatic OEM agreements are available; larger buyers also pool purchases and pre-position gear (SAVRN). A smaller owner can standardise designs, make framework supply agreements directly with OEMs and free-issue equipment or nominate subcontractors (King & Spalding), qualify alternative manufacturers such as Efacec, SGB-SMIT and CG Power, whose cited quotes were 24–36 months against 48–60 at some top-tier suppliers, second-source critical categories, and consider pre-engineered modular skids quoted at 26–40 weeks. Those quotes still require project-specific confirmation.

OFE versus CFE. With owner-furnished equipment (OFE), the owner orders scarce gear and provides it to the design-build or EPC contractor for installation. The owner controls its slot but may carry supplier delay or defect risk; whether a late transformer gives the contractor schedule relief, changes damages or moves a tenant milestone is a contract allocation. Under contractor-furnished equipment (CFE), the contractor buys and installs the gear, usually pricing its schedule exposure in return for more unified responsibility. EPC can support a turnkey CFE arrangement; design-build can combine an owner-controlled long-lead order with contractor installation, as in the chapter. Lump-sum contracting is "increasingly difficult" while copper, equipment and labour prices move (Gravel2Gavel); GMP and blended arrangements are common on sub-$500M builds.

Specify OFE obligations. The contracts must allocate supplier delay and notice, factory acceptance and witness testing, delivery and installation interfaces, storage and maintenance before installation, insurance and risk of loss, defect correction, and the boundary between the OEM warranty and contractor warranty. Address schedule relief and any delay liquidated damages explicitly. Reported practice cites damages of 0.1–0.5% a day capped at 5–10% of contract value (research synthesis of contract practice); those figures are not terms for this campus. Buying the transformer directly does not by itself decide who pays for testing, insures it in storage or warrants the installed system.

Reference campus: the sequence and the money

Interconnection is the likely critical path; land, water, permits and procurement run in parallel and can themselves delay opening. "Power infrastructure is no longer a dependency of development": substations, transmission and time govern usable power even when a building finishes sooner (DataCenters.com). The course campus has about 40 MW planned IT load and 56 MW at the connection at design PUE 1.4. Hall 1 is about 10 MW IT and needs roughly 14 MW at the connection. The full-site service contract is planned near 60 MW, with later capacity and dates to be agreed, not delivered at first energization.

  • A, interconnection: apply for a roughly 14 MW first step and later ramp; complete studies, agreement, assigned utility work, testing and staged energization. Large-load total time-to-power can be 2–5+ years.
  • B, land: option, then title, geotechnical, flood, fiber and water diligence, close and any local entitlements. The roughly 120-acre Phoenix parcel remains an illustrative candidate.
  • C, water: source and peak-supply diligence, agreement or rights, and discharge and sewer capacity.
  • D, permits: zoning, hearings, environmental and site-plan work, building review and engine air review as locally required; work overlaps rather than following a universal order.
  • E, procurement: reserve slots, complete design releases, RTM, factory acceptance testing, delivery and installation.

It is not scaled into, added to or a check on this course's four-phase budget. Here the shared-work first phase is $180–230M and the complete campus is $400–500M, both excluding IT equipment. The five pre-agreement commitments are option and diligence, study fees, slot deposits, land purchase, and early design and permits. Their low endpoints sum to $22.6M ($0.5M + $0.1M + $5M + $15M + $2M); their high endpoints sum to $52.5M ($2M + $0.5M + $15M + $30M + $5M). Rounded, that is $23–53M exposed before an assumed month-18 agreement. The midpoint sum is $37.55M, about $38M. Commitment is not necessarily a permanent loss: land and some deposits may retain value or be recoverable under their terms. Utility construction payments belong to the later agreement gate, not those five items. Posted collateral secures obligations and may restrict credit, but is not spent construction capital. Virginia-style $1.5M/MW security is not a requirement or base-case cost for this Phoenix candidate (§5).

Reconcile the clocks. RTM near month 8 plus the second-quarter 2025 survey benchmark of roughly 128 weeks, about 29–30 months, suggests transformer delivery around month 37–38 if that lead time applies and the slot holds (§10). An 18-month hall build beginning month 18 reaches physical completion near month 36, before that indicated delivery. The first energization target near month 38 requires the utility's work, transformer delivery, installation and connection tests all to align; month 40 is a contingency assumption, not a standard duration or assured date. Commissioning can push tenant opening toward month 42. A delayed utility date is not cured automatically by a gas bridge: that remains separately costed, supplied and permitted contingency scope (§6). A more constrained site can take 4–5 years or longer to get power.
