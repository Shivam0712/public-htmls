What a site must provide, in 2025–26 order

The ranking inverted. For decades the checklist led with connectivity. CBRE's 2026 outlook states that "power availability and certainty are now primary site selection criteria, overshadowing traditional connectivity considerations," and frames the new bar as the ability to secure 300 MW-plus deliveries in under 36 months (CBRE, U.S. Data Center Outlook 2026). JLL calls power "the primary site-selection factor, with average grid-connection wait times in primary markets exceeding four years" (JLL, Global Data Center Outlook 2026). Bloom Energy's mid-2025 survey of 44 hyperscale and colocation developers found 84% placing power in their top three criteria; its November 2025 survey of 92 respondents found 51% (Bloom Energy, Data Center Power Report, June and November 2025). The chapter therefore cites the ranking, not the percentage. Practitioner order: power (availability, timeline, cost), fiber, land, water, hazard exposure, tax and incentive climate, workforce, community acceptance (research synthesis of CBRE, JLL, Bloom, Data Center Watch).

The 18–24 month versus 4+ year tension. CBRE reports developers "sourcing parcels within close proximity to substations and locking in power purchase agreements before finalizing land acquisition," with the most sought-after sites offering power in 18–24 months (CBRE, North America Data Center Trends H1 2025). JLL's 4+ years describes typical primary-market waits. Both are true; the first is powered land, the second the constrained market. The reference campus is written as the first kind of site, which is why its timeline (§12) is shorter than the market median.

Land. The average data center land transaction reached 224 acres in 2024, up 144% since 2022 (Cushman & Wakefield via TechTarget); weighted-average land cost was about $244,000 an acre in October 2024 (Gravel2Gavel). A 100 MW facility "might occupy roughly 20 to over 100 acres" depending on density; multi-phase campuses assemble 100–1,000+ acres; "large, unbroken tracts are far more valuable than the same acreage split by roads, easements, or separate parcels" (Passive Investments, developer practice summary). Rising rack density can shrink acres per MW (CBRE), but power, not floor area, is the binding number. Industrial zoning is the first gate only: data centers routinely need rezoning, special or conditional-use permits, height variances for cooling equipment and switchgear, noise-ordinance compliance and setbacks, and CBRE warns that development outside urban cores "will require rezoning and entitlements" (CBRE, 2025 outlook).

Hazards. FEMA floodways and 100-year Special Flood Hazard Areas are "generally disqualifying," and partial exposure "complicates insurance, financing, and tenant approval"; serious developers screen to the 500-year (0.2% annual chance) floodplain (Passive Investments). Seismic zones raise structural cost. Wildfire smoke fouls air-side economizers and forces intakes closed, degrading free cooling. Extreme heat raises cooling energy and water use when the grid is most stressed. Climate sets free-cooling hours and therefore operating cost: Phoenix measurements show water-cooled chillers achieving lower energy but higher water use and air-cooled systems the inverse (arXiv survey of 15 US climate zones). At the site stage this is the water-versus-energy decision (§7); the plant is Topic 7.

Fiber. Carrier diversity avoids monopoly pricing and single-provider outages; two physically separate entries from different directions are the bedrock requirement, and "diverse" paths that share a conduit or a bridge crossing are a hidden single point of failure. Proximity to exchanges and long-haul routes cuts latency and backhaul cost, but fiber now follows power: providers including Fiberlight, Zayo and Flo Networks are building "thousands of route miles of new fiber" in West Texas to reach power-rich sites (CBRE, North America Data Center Trends H2 2025).

Load interconnection is not generator interconnection

A data center is a large load. "Interconnection processes for new generating facilities are under FERC jurisdiction while the interconnection of large loads is conducted at the state level" (White & Case, 2025). Generator interconnection was standardised federally through FERC Order 2023 and cluster studies; load interconnection had no uniform process, and utilities handled large loads "on an ad hoc basis" (RMI; GridLab), which is what broke under the 2024–26 surge. The two overlap where a large load triggers the same transmission upgrades a generator would, and increasingly in co-located load-plus-generation filings.

Federal overlay (verify: changing fast). DOE directed FERC on 23 October 2025 to accelerate large-load interconnection and permit joint co-located filings; after its 18 June 2026 meeting FERC directed the six RTOs to write region-specific large-load rules. Direction of travel: faster studies, firmer financial commitments. Whether this federalises load interconnection or fragments it by region is an open question, and the state-versus-FERC split in the first paragraph may not survive.

The six steps and their durations

Drawing on RMI's six-step framing and utility documents (RMI; AEP Ohio tariff; EPC practice):

  1. Application or load-study request, by the developer through the local utility, which coordinates with the transmission owner and RTO. AEP Ohio charges tiered study fees of $10,000, $50,000 and $100,000 by request size.
  2. Load or feasibility study: can existing facilities serve the load?
  3. System impact study: models voltage, thermal and reliability effects and identifies network upgrades. This is where the large surprises and large bills appear.
  4. Facilities study: engineers and prices the substation, tie lines and metering.
  5. Interconnection or construction agreement: fixes scope, cost responsibility, milestones and the energization date, and triggers financial security.
  6. Construction and energization: the utility builds its upgrades, the customer builds its substation, then testing, utility witness testing and energization.

Durations. Sources converge on twelve months to more than three years for the process itself; JLL and LBNL data push total time-to-power in primary markets to 4–5+ years. LBNL's Queued Up 2025 finds that of generation capacity requesting interconnection 2000–2019, 13% had reached commercial operation by end-2024 and 77% withdrew (LBNL, Queued Up 2025) (verify: generator data, applied to loads by analogy because the wires constraint is the same). The practitioner lesson: treat interconnection as a day-one workstream and overlap the study phases with substation design and long-lead procurement (§11).

Who pays, and the substation choice

Roles. The developer builds and usually owns the on-site substation and pays for utility-side work attributable to its load. The utility or transmission owner performs the studies, owns and builds network upgrades and is lead processor. The RTO or ISO (PJM, ERCOT and others) coordinates transmission-level impacts; where there is no RTO, across much of the Southeast and West, the vertically integrated utility does everything (research synthesis).

Contribution in aid of construction (CIAC) is the non-refundable customer payment toward utility-owned facilities built to serve it: dedicated substation, transmission taps, feeders, metering. Indicative magnitudes: $75k–400k for 1–3 MW distribution service; $400k–2.5M for 5–20 MW with a line extension; $3M–30M+ where a dedicated substation or transmission tap is needed; eight or nine figures for hyperscale loads (Terrapin CG, 2026). Classic tariffs refund part of a CIAC if other customers later attach to the funded extension (for example 30% within three years); large-load versions blend non-refundable CIAC with refunds conditioned on minimum-load thresholds. Texas SB 6's proposed standard: a $50,000/MW non-refundable interconnection fee, 100% of direct interconnection costs paid by the customer with no usage credit, and 80% of posted security retained on withdrawal (Texas SB 6, 2025).

Service level by load. Below about 20 MW a distribution feeder (primary voltage typically ≤ 35 kV) may serve; most facilities of 20–50 MW and above need a dedicated substation; 100 MW+ campuses almost always need transmission-level service at 115–345 kV with dual feeds (American Power Engineers, 2026). The landing page's explorer uses the same shape: under ~15 MW at the fence a feeder, 15–80 MW a dedicated substation at 69–138 kV, above that transmission-level. The reference campus at ~56 MW at the fence sits in the middle band; the course fixes a customer-owned 138 kV substation with two incoming lines.

Ownership. A customer-owned substation gives schedule control, since the developer can procure and build faster than the utility, and puts transformer and switchgear procurement risk on the developer. A utility-owned substation shifts the build to the utility, whose queue and equipment backlog then set the date, funded by CIAC either way. In 2025–26 schedule-pressed developers increasingly self-build and self-procure to escape the utility's own equipment backlog (research synthesis). The transformers themselves are Topic 5; here they are procurement items (§10).

By September 2026 EEI's tracker showed 25 states with at least one approved large-load tariff and 7 pending; the CoBank/DELTa database counted approved tariffs rising from about 20 in 2024 to 53+ in 2025 (EEI; CoBank). Common features: minimum-take (take-or-pay) provisions, 10–20 year terms, collateral, ramp schedules and exit fees. Whether the new tariffs protect ratepayers or repel investment is unresolved; the Buckeye Institute argues they charge the most mobile customers the most (verify: contested).

Bridging and behind-the-meter power

Cleanview found 92% of the behind-the-meter (BTM) projects it identified, about 82 GW, were announced since the start of 2025; AI labs alone have signed leases for over 10 GW of BTM power (Cleanview, 2026). The logic: grid interconnection takes 4–7 years, a gas plant can be permitted and built in 18–30 months, so on-site generation bridges to grid power and increasingly becomes prime power (Jefferies; Green Gas Turbines).

  • Technologies. Simple-cycle and aeroderivative gas turbines (GE Vernova is supplying 29 aeroderivative turbines, about 1 GW, to Project Stargate); modular reciprocating gas engines (VoltaGrid's units can reach 200 MW "under a minor source air permit"); fuel cells (Bloom Energy; cost and lead time not sourced in the research, verify); batteries to bridge the 30–120 seconds between a grid event and engine start.
  • Costs (indicative). A 50 MW simple-cycle aeroderivative installation runs about $40–60M including balance of plant; broader estimates span $0.7–1.4M/MW depending on simple versus combined cycle, balance of plant and gas interconnection (research synthesis).
  • Permitting is the catch. BTM does not escape air permitting. Microsoft/Nebius's 400 MW on-site gas plant in New Jersey has struggled to obtain its permit, jeopardising tranches of a $17.4B deal. Tier 4 emissions packages add time; staying under minor-source thresholds is the fast path.
  • Engines are rationed too. Cummins is "taking orders for its QSK95 generator solution into the second half of 2028," with customers falling back to 78, 60 and 50 litre units (Cummins, Jonas Arens, Feb 2026); Cummins targets 55 GW of high-horsepower capacity by 2030. Caterpillar reported a record $63B enterprise backlog, up 79% year on year, with orders "well into 2028" (Caterpillar Q1 2026 earnings call, 30 April 2026) (verify: recent). BTM trades a grid queue for an equipment queue.
  • Ceiling. Cleanview models 5 GW versus 13 GW of BTM actually deployed by end-2027 depending on permit and engine delays.

PPAs and renewable matching, as siting factors only. Developers now lock PPAs before finalising land (CBRE H1 2025); 24/7 matching and additionality commitments (Google, Microsoft) push toward grids where new clean generation can be added; Georgia's Customer Identified Resource tariff (approved April 2026, capped at 3 GW through 2035) and Nevada's clean-transition tariffs let large customers fund their own generation to unlock service. Sustainability accounting is out of scope.

Water as a site and permitting matter

Where the water goes. Uptime Institute benchmarks a conventional evaporative system at "millions of gallons of water per year for each megawatt of IT load"; 70–80% of withdrawn water evaporates (consumptive) and 20–30% leaves as blowdown (Uptime Institute). Closed-loop systems consume 5–10% of an equivalent open-loop withdrawal: Vantage's Wisconsin closed-loop campus peaks near 22,000 gallons a day, about 65 homes, against ~5,000,000 for a comparable evaporative campus (Vantage). Air-cooled chillers use almost no water at an energy penalty; Microsoft's August 2024 zero-water design eliminates evaporative cooling and saves about 33 million gallons per facility per year, deployed in Phoenix (Microsoft). Liquid-to-chip removes heat at the chip, but the facility still rejects it, often through evaporative or dry coolers, so it does not by itself eliminate water.

WUE figures (verify: compiled from secondary sources). Hyperscale water-efficient designs 0.09–1.80 L/kWh; traditional evaporative 1.99–4.00 L/kWh; industry average about 1.8–1.9 L/kWh; AWS reports 0.15 L/kWh (arXiv survey; Introl; Avanza; AWS). The 1.8–1.9 L/kWh "industry average" is one survey's figure, repeated by secondary guides; LBNL's 2024 United States Data Center Energy Usage Report puts the US site-level average near 0.36 L/kWh (verify: sources differ by definition and population; site WUE counts only water used on site, and the survey populations differ). Topic 7's appendix carries the reconciliation. A medium evaporative site consumes about 300,000 gallons a day; large hyperscale sites in hot climates 1–5 million; Google's Council Bluffs campus withdrew about 3.9 million gallons a day and consumed about 2.8 million. Consumptive versus non-consumptive is the distinction communities care about: returned water stresses infrastructure, evaporated water is gone locally until it rains. The reference campus's OPR target of ≤ 0.2 L/kWh is a course decision at the water-efficient end of the first band.

Sourcing. Public water systems supplied about 97% of US data centers' on-site water as of 2025 (CRS-cited analysis); potable is simplest and cleanest, and the most politically exposed. Reclaimed or "purple pipe" water needs a separate non-potable line and may have competing users. Groundwater and surface withdrawals may require the developer to hold rights. Western states follow prior appropriation ("first in time, first in right"): rights carry a priority date independent of land ownership and junior holders are curtailed first in shortage, a real curtailment and financing risk. Eastern states follow the riparian doctrine (reasonable use tied to waterfront land); California and others are hybrids. Southwest diligence therefore runs "aquifer-specific modeling, sustainable yield analysis… priority, transferability, place-of-use limitations and curtailment history" (Gravel2Gavel). Texas groundwater law is not covered by the research.

Discharge and sewer. Blowdown to surface water needs an NPDES permit under the Clean Water Act with temperature and chemistry limits (thermal pollution, biocides, anti-scalants). Sewer capacity scales roughly with withdrawal: one facility under construction was allocated 8 MGD of water and 4 MGD of wastewater, a smaller one 2 MGD and 0.57 MGD (arXiv). A municipality with water but no sewer headroom can stop a project.

Community response. The Ypsilanti Community Utilities Authority imposed a 12-month moratorium on supplying water to data centers (April 2026); Data Center Watch catalogues rezoning denials citing water. Developer responses are Microsoft's zero-water design, Vantage's closed loop, AWS's 0.15 L/kWh and Oracle's closed-loop AI centers, all positioned as answers to opposition. Avanza's framing: on water "the technical problem is already solved" at the frontier, a 12× gap between average and best; the challenge is deploying it and making binding commitments before opposition forms.

Taxes and incentives

Incentives are a tiebreaker, occasionally decisive, negotiated in advance and conditioned on investment, job and wage targets. Sales-tax exemptions on equipment (servers, switchgear, sometimes electricity) are the most common tool: in FY2025 Virginia operators reported about $33.2B of exempt investment for a reported ~$1.9B single-year benefit, against an original projection of $1.5M a year (Virginia JLARC and state reporting). Property-tax abatements and PILOTs are usually local; West Virginia's 2025 law values equipment at about 5% (salvage); Nevada offers up to 75% abatements; Texas pairs a 10–15 year sales-tax exemption (≥ $200M investment, ≥ 20 jobs) with local Chapter 312 abatements. A rough read is that sales-tax relief on the $25–40M/MW of equipment in an AI build is worth low-single-digit $M/MW where combined rates approach 6–8% (verify: property terms are local and often under NDA; treat any single $/MW figure cautiously).

The turn. Florida limited its exemption to ≥ 100 MW IT-load facilities (30 June 2025); Virginia's exemption is stalling state budget negotiations; over 300 data center bills were filed across 30+ states in early 2026, shifting from incentives to oversight; New York's Responsible Data Center Development Act passed both chambers on 5 June 2026 and awaits the governor's signature, which would make it the first statewide moratorium (verify: pending). The reference campus keeps incentives out of its base-case pro forma for this reason.

Permitting sequence and community risk

Sequence. Zoning or rezoning → conditional or special-use permit → environmental review → site-plan approval → building permits → air permits for generators or BTM gas → noise and other operating permits. Projects stall at three places: the rezoning or conditional-use hearing, which is the public's veto moment and where organised opposition concentrates; air permits (§6); and environmental review with water and sewer approvals. Delays average 18–24 months where opposition forms (Introl; Data Center Watch), and cost overruns can kill project economics.

Community risk as a formal criterion. Data Center Watch (10a Labs) documented about $64B of US projects blocked or delayed by local opposition between May 2024 and March 2025, rising to $98–100B in a single quarter (Q2 2025) and about $130B+ cumulatively by its June 2026 update; active opposition groups grew from 396 to 833 across 49 states in one quarter to March 2026; cancellations went from 2 (2023) to 6 (2024) to 25 (2025) (Data Center Watch, 2025–26). Opposition is bipartisan (about 55% Republican, 45% Democrat among opposing politicians). Arguments in rough order: electricity-rate impacts, water, noise, property values and green space, and the low job-to-investment ratio. Documented denials include Peculiar, Missouri (September 2024); Culpeper/Brandy Station, Virginia ($12B, denied June 2024); and Tract's $14B Goodyear/Buckeye, Arizona project, withdrawn after rezoning was blocked. Developers now screen for organised opposition, engage early and offer binding water, noise and PILOT commitments before filing.

Topic 1's "160+ weeks in 2026" is the substation-class 2026 quote in the first row; the two are the same market a year apart, not a contradiction.

Concentration. Two or three producers dominate high-voltage transformers (ABB, Siemens Energy, Hitachi Energy, GE Vernova); Japan, South Korea and Germany make about 70% of global GOES; Vertiv, Eaton and Schneider dominate UPS, switchgear and distribution; gensets are effectively a Caterpillar/Cummins oligopoly, both booked into 2028 (§6). Utilities' grid modernisation, renewable and storage interconnection, electrification and reshoring compete for the same factories: "any AI campus depending on utility-supplied transformation is competing not just with other data centers, but with every utility upgrade in the country" (SAVRN). US transformer demand is projected to exceed domestic capacity by 30–40% through at least 2028, and a new transformer plant takes 3–5 years to build. Buyers "scrambling for imports and factory slots" (Reuters) face tariff exposure on imported transformers and steel.

Ordering before design, and who carries the equipment

Why procurement moved to the front. Lead times now exceed design-and-permit timelines. Hyperscale developers reserve factory slots with deposits of 10–30% of equipment cost at land close, 6–12 months before groundbreaking, with ratings frozen at about 60% single-line completeness (Global Data Center Hub). Instruments: reservation agreements, slot purchases, and framework or programmatic agreements with OEMs spanning multiple sites. The manufacturing clock starts at release to manufacture (RTM), which can sit a quarter behind the purchase order: a January PO with RTM held to April loses twelve weeks before fabrication begins. Hyperscalers and large colos pre-buy and even manufacture in-house (Anthropic is hiring a "Global Power Delivery Equipment Lead" to run pooled framework buys and slot reallocation; SAVRN and Giga-type players pre-position gear to compress timelines to 6–8 months). A smaller owner can standardise designs, sign framework supply agreements directly with OEMs and free-issue equipment or nominate subcontractors (King & Spalding), qualify second-tier manufacturers (Efacec, SGB-SMIT, CG Power quote 24–36 months against 48–60 at the top tier), second-source every critical category, and consider pre-engineered modular skids at 26–40 weeks.

OFE versus CFE. Under owner-furnished equipment the owner buys the long-lead gear directly and free-issues it to the design-build or EPC contractor, who installs it; the owner then carries delivery-delay and defect risk. If the transformer is late the contractor's schedule and any liquidated-damages claim are affected, but the cause sits with the owner. This is the dominant model for scarce electrical gear because owners will not cede slot control. Under contractor-furnished equipment the contractor procures everything inside a turnkey wrap: cleaner single-point responsibility, but the contractor prices the schedule risk and the owner may lose slot priority. EPC suits CFE; design-build often leaves specialised systems as OFE (Topic 1's reference choice). Lump-sum contracting is "increasingly difficult" while copper, equipment and labour prices swing (Gravel2Gavel), so GMP and blended models are common on sub-$500M builds.

Obligations that come with OFE. Factory acceptance testing, witnessed before shipment; warranty management, including the gap between the OEM's workmanship warranty and a contractor's wrap warranty; storage, insurance and maintenance of gear that arrives before the site is ready; and delay liquidated damages, commonly 0.1–0.5% a day capped at 5–10% of contract value (research synthesis of contract practice).

Reference campus: the sequence and the money

Interconnection is the critical path; land, water, permits and procurement are parallel tracks that must not fall behind it. "Power infrastructure is no longer a dependency of development": substations, transmission and time are the critical path, and buildings go up in months while the power to energize them cannot (DataCenters.com). The five tracks:

  • A, interconnection (critical path): application → studies (12–36 months) → agreement → utility build and energization; 2–5+ years in total.
  • B, land: option → diligence (title, geotech, flood, fiber, water) → close → rezoning and entitlement.
  • C, water: source diligence → supply agreement or rights → discharge and sewer capacity.
  • D, permits: rezoning and conditional use → environmental review → building and air permits.
  • E, procurement (from land close, before design is final): reserve slots → RTM → FAT → delivery → install.

Scaling from the research's example. The research's worked example is a single-phase 60 MW campus with transmission-level service and a customer-owned substation, totalling $650M–1.1B with $25–70M at risk before entitlement certainty. The course's campus is ~56 MW at the fence (40 MW IT at PUE 1.4), $400–500M of capex ex-IT (Topic 1), Hall 1 at ~10 MW first. At the construction agreement about $33–83M is committed, roughly a tenth of capex, with the site's power still contingent on the utility's build.
