Power Distribution from the UPS Output to the Rack — A Working-Knowledge Chapter

The Desktop Research report for Topic 6, run 2026-09-25 from briefs/ and published as delivered: no editing, no lint. The chapter and appendix were distilled from it; where they and this report disagree, the chapter is the course's position.

Scope: from the UPS output bus (clean power available to distribute) to the dual-corded IT load in the rack. As of 2025–2026. Every number is indicative and carries a source and date; where sources disagree, both are given with a judgment. Sections are citable as "§2.3".

TL;DR

  • The chain is short but unforgiving: UPS output bus → PDU (transformer only if you must change voltage) → optional STS → busway or cable/RPP → whip → rack PDU → dual-corded A/B IT load. The single highest-leverage decision is voltage: distributing at 415/240 V (or 400/230 V in the EU) lets the server draw 240 V line-to-neutral with no PDU transformer, cutting a conversion stage, copper, and losses; 208 V is legacy and current-bound. (Eaton, AC vs DC Power Distribution; Server Technology, 2014.)
  • Redundancy is a topology choice, not an availability number. Tier III (concurrently maintainable) is the market default; 2N strands half your capacity, distributed-redundant 3M2 strands a third, and AI/hyperscale builds are actively relaxing to N+1 on the compute blocks because GPU training tolerates checkpoint-restart. Uptime certifies the topology, not a percentage — it removed "expected downtime per year" from the Tier Standard in 2009.
  • The floor is moving under AC. 48 V in-rack (OCP ORv3) hits a copper/current wall past ~100 kW, and the industry has converged on ±400 V / 800 V DC (OCP Mt Diablo sidecar; NVIDIA 800 VDC for Kyber) with full-scale production tied to 2027 — so an owner building an AC busway hall now carries genuine stranded-asset risk in the AI pods.

1. Distribution from the UPS Output to the Rack

1.1 The full chain, in order

The canonical low-voltage (LV) distribution chain, with indicative ratings (Schneider Electric, Vertiv, Raritan white papers; Data Center Strategy, 2025):

  1. UPS output bus / output switchboard — 480/277 V or 415/240 V three-phase, in 2–3 MW blocks. Treated here as a box with an output rating.
  2. PDU (floor-standing power distribution unit) — 300–1,000 kVA typical; contains input breaker, optional step-down/isolation transformer, and two branch-circuit distribution panels. Weighs 3,000–5,000 lb each; a large hall may hold 50+ (Buildermuse, 2026).
  3. Static transfer switch (STS) — 250–4,000 A, 3- or 4-pole (LayerZero, 2025); sits upstream of, or integrated with, the PDU, or at the rack for single-corded loads.
  4. Busway (busbar trunking) OR cable-and-remote-power-panel (RPP) — the row-distribution layer.
  5. Whip / tap-off — flexible drop from busway plug-in box or RPP breaker to the rack.
  6. Rack PDU (rPDU) — 0U vertical strip; converts the branch circuit to outlets. 208 V or 415/240 V; 30–60 A three-phase common; up to ~130 kW units exist (Raritan PX4; Introl, 2025).
  7. Dual-corded IT load — server draws from A and B cords, each fed from an independent path.

1.2 Why a transformer sits in the PDU (and when it doesn't)

A PDU transformer exists to change voltage and create a separately derived system — a new neutral and ground-fault reference (isolating zero-sequence faults on both sides). The classic North American case: step 480 V delta down to 208/120 V wye so legacy servers get 120 V line-to-neutral and 208 V line-to-line (US Patent 6,967,283, Adjustable scalable rack power system). The transformer disappears entirely when you distribute at 415/240 V: the UPS feeds the busway at 415 V and the server takes 240 V line-to-neutral directly — "no intermediate transformation" (Server Technology, 415 VAC Power Distribution, 2014). A 480/277→415/240 V autotransformer is "90% smaller and less expensive than a 120/208 V PDU isolation transformer" (Silverback DC Solutions). The trade-off: removing the isolation transformer raises available fault current at the PDU — one vendor's worked figures show fault current rising from ~17,576 A (with 480→208 transformer) to ~56,144 A (415 V, transformer removed) — so a short-circuit and coordination study becomes mandatory (Vertiv/Liebert, Proper Application of 415V Systems, R1010).

1.3 The STS: where it sits, what it protects, how it fails

An STS transfers a load between two independent sources using silicon-controlled rectifiers (thyristors) in about 4 ms (sub-cycle), with no moving parts (Schneider Electric WP62; LayerZero). It exists to keep single-corded gear alive in a dual-path world, and to enable a source to be shut for maintenance without dropping the load. Failure modes: because it must monitor phase angles, SCR states, breaker states, and voltages on both sides, it is far more complex than an electromechanical ATS, and thus has more failure modes (Schneider WP62). The dominant systemic risk is the large centralized STS as a single point of failure — "big centralized STS units are a bad idea because they create single points of failure that can take down multiple racks" — so best practice pushes fault-sensing STS/ATS close to the rack (TRG Datacenters, 2025). A load fault on a shared bus can propagate to every load on that bus, single- and dual-corded, if the bus fails before the branch breaker clears (Vertiv/Emerson technical note TN-00011).

1.4 Busway vs cable-and-RPP

See §A2 (comparison table). In one line each: RPP = a breaker panel on the floor with fixed cable home-runs — cheaper on day one, familiar, best when the load is defined and stable; growth means a new home-run, a live-hall conduit pull, and a hot-work permit (aidatacenterguide.com, 2025). Busway = prefabricated overhead open-channel track (Starline-style) into which tap-off boxes twist-lock without de-energizing the bus; higher capex, dramatically lower install labor, and reconfigurable under load — the hyperscale/AI default (Semianalysis; DCD, 2025). Starline Track Busway spans 40–1,250 A with IP2X–IP54 ratings (Starline/Legrand). In a Starline cost model of a 28-rack row with 400 A A/B redundancy, adding a new bus plug box costs ~$350 and the owner installs it himself — versus a contractor mobilization for a new whip (Starline, Cost Analysis, 2019; still the reference model in 2025 materials). Cable still wins when: the room is small, densities are low and fixed, the layout won't change, or budget is day-one-constrained.

1.5 Voltage choices and what they buy

See §A3. The physics: at fixed power, raising voltage lowers current proportionally, and resistive (I²R) loss falls with the square of current. Moving 208 V → 415 V nearly doubles the power through the same conductor; a 60 A circuit carries 17.3 kW at 208 V vs 39.9 kW at 415 V — a 130% increase with no change in conductor size (asbeam.com, 2025). At the server PSU, 240 V input runs at 85.5% efficiency vs 82% at 120 V (a 3.5-point gain) and ~1.5 points better than 208 V (Mirus International, HMRP WP001). 480/277 V is impractical to the server because 277 V exceeds the 240 V rating of standard IT PSUs — it requires custom servers, so it stays a distribution voltage that is stepped down near the load (CSE, Data center power strategies). 400/230 V is the native EU scheme and yields ~89% chain efficiency with off-the-shelf gear (Eaton).

How a 120 kW rack is actually fed (indicative worked example): At 415 V three-phase, usable power per feed ≈ √3 × 415 × I × 0.8 (NEC 80% derate). A 60 A feed ≈ 415 × 60 × 1.732 × 0.8 ≈ 34.5 kW. To land 120 kW you need ≈ 4 such circuits per side, doubled for A/B = ~8 feeds, typically delivered as two rack PDUs per side (4 total) fed from A and B busway runs at ~100–160 A tap-offs. At 208 V the same rack needs roughly twice the circuits/copper — the reason AI racks abandon 208 V (Server Technology math; Introl: "at 480 V three-phase, 100 kW requires only 120 A per circuit").

1.6 A/B feeds, single-corded gear, load balancing

Dual-corded IT takes A and B cords from independent paths; each cord/feed must be sized to carry the full rack load if the other is offline — "do not split kW 50/50 for sizing unless policy and OEM allow it" (calcpanel.com, 2025). This is the cardinal load-balancing rule: run each side at ≤50% so a feed failure does not overload the survivor. Single-corded gear (legacy appliances, some network kit) needs a rack ATS/STS to give it two sources; the transfer switch is the SPOF you accept for that one device (TRG Datacenters). Branch-circuit and rack-PDU metering exist as names here — per-outlet monitoring is how you prove the A/B mapping and catch a rack running on one PSU (Obelinf, 2026).

1.7 Delivery and risk (who furnishes, who owns)

PDUs, STS and busway are frequently owner-furnished (OFE) long-lead items on design-build jobs; the contractor installs and terminates. In wholesale colocation the demarcation is often "the output breaker of the PDU," with the tenant supplying racks and contracting for whips and cable plant — though many providers now deliver "rack-ready" suites including coordinated racks, whips and cable plant (Sentinel Data Centers). Rule of thumb: owner owns to the busway/RPP and often the whip; tenant brings rack PDUs and IT in retail; in wholesale the tenant fits out the white space (racks, containment, rack PDUs) itself (Obelinf; DataCenterKnowledge, 2025).

2. Redundancy Models and What Each Actually Buys

2.1 Definitions (one-line sketches)

  • N — exactly enough capacity for the load; no spare.
  • N+1 — one extra module beyond need; survives one component failure or one unit in maintenance.
  • 2N — two fully independent systems, each 100% of load; each side runs ≤50%. Survives any single failure and concurrent maintenance.
  • 2N+1 — 2N plus a spare in each system; survives worst-case even with a unit already down.
  • Distributed redundant (3M2 / 4M3) — three (or four) independent systems share load so that if one fails the others carry it. In 3M2, each of A/B/C runs at ≤66.7% (33.4% normal load) so two can carry all three shares (CSE/Caterpillar).
  • Block redundant ("catcher"/reserve-bus) — N active blocks plus one reserve block that a failed block switches to via STS; the catcher sits unloaded normally, letting active UPS run at 75%+ (Socomec; STACK).
  • Isolated redundant — one/two UPS back up multiple units through STS.

Who uses what: enterprise → N+1; banks/critical → 2N/2N+1; colos and clouds → mix of 2N and 3M2/4M3 for concurrent maintainability at lower stranded capacity (CSE, Data center co-location electrical system design). CoreSite's VA3 (Reston, VA) runs 2N + distributed-redundant 4-to-make-3 with a catcher UPS (CoreSite).

2.2 The failure math (IEEE 493 / IEEE 3006 method)

Availability of one repairable element: A = MTBF / (MTBF + MTTR). Components in series multiply availabilities (and add failure rates); components in parallel/redundant multiply *un*availabilities (IEEE 493 "Gold Book," now Inactive-Reserved with guidance migrated to the IEEE 3006 series; formulas universal). Worked figures (indicative):

  • One path at A = 99.9% → unavailability 0.001 → ~8.76 h/yr downtime.
  • Two such paths in parallel (2N): U = 0.001 × 0.001 = 0.000001 → A = 99.9999% ("six nines"), ~31.5 s/yr. (Rule sourced; arithmetic illustrative.)
  • Redundant-pair MTBF approximation: MTBF_parallel ≈ MTBF² / (2 × MTTR) — two units at 3,000 h MTBF and 1 h MTTR ≈ 4.5 million h; let MTTR slip to 24 h and it collapses to ~187,500 h (InventiveHQ). This is the whole argument for fast repair discipline.

The caveat that matters: these percentages are industry-derived, not Uptime certification. Uptime Institute "removed all references to 'expected downtime per year'… in 2009" and "does not assign availability predictions to Tier levels" (Uptime Institute Journal). Topology, operations and human discipline — not the statistics — are what certify.

2.3 Cost and stranded capacity

See §A1. The core economics: 2N installs 200% of load and uses ≤50% per side — half the capacity is reserve. 3M2 installs 150% and uses ≤66.7% — a third is reserve, so capex per usable MW is meaningfully lower than 2N while still concurrently maintainable. Block redundant installs (N+1 blocks) and strands roughly one block. N+1 strands the least but survives the least. Each rung up demands more operational discipline (load balancing, changeover procedures, AIRs tracking).

2.4 Tier and standards mapping

Tier III = concurrently maintainable (redundant components + multiple distribution paths, one active at a time); Tier IV = fault tolerant (multiple active, physically isolated paths, survives any single event including operator error) (Uptime Institute). TIA-942 uses "Rated-1..4" (Rated-3 = concurrently maintainable, multiple independent paths) and certifies against telecom/facility criteria; BICSI 002 is a best-practices companion "intended to complement and exceed the minimum requirements in TIA-942" (TechTarget; TIA). Uptime certifies design, build and operations; TIA-942 certifies against a standard document.

AI/hyperscale relaxation (dated): "Many operators now run N or N+1 on the compute blocks and reserve 2N for the network, storage, and control plane — GPU training clusters tolerate checkpoint-restart" (ModulEdge citing Uptime Institute, 2025). AWS reports a best-case GPU-node MTTR of ~12 minutes using fail-in-place N+1 rather than hardware swap (AWS, 2025). NVIDIA's Kyber and the OCP sidecar even contemplate single-corded GPU racks where software fault tolerance replaces electrical redundancy.

2.5 Delivery and risk

The owner sets the model in the OPR (Owner's Project Requirements); the engineer renders it in the BOD (Basis of Design); the colo writes it into the SLA as an availability commitment and a redundancy descriptor. A mid-design change from, say, 2N to 3M2 ripples through UPS/PDU/STS counts, switchgear, and long-lead procurement — MV switchgear lead times now run 40–60 weeks (Buildermuse, 2026), so a late redundancy change can move the whole schedule.

3. The Shift to 48 V and High-Voltage DC for AI Racks

3.1 Inside the rack: 48 V busbar and OCP ORv3

The OCP Open Rack v3 power shelf rectifies facility AC to a 48 V DC busbar shared down the rack, removing per-server PSUs and their duplicated AC/DC stages. Google's 12 V→48 V migration achieved a ~30% efficiency improvement, per Urs Hölzle, Google SVP of technical infrastructure, who told the Open Compute Summit in San Jose that reducing conversion steps "resulted in a 30 percent improvement in energy efficiency"; Google's own blog adds it "kicked-off the development of 48V rack power distribution in 2010, as we found it was at least 30% more energy efficient," and Google contributed the 48 V design to OCP in 2016–2017 (DataCenterKnowledge; Google Cloud Blog). ORv3 HPR power shelves reach 33 kW and near-98% efficiency (Advanced Energy). The 48 V wall: as racks pass ~100 kW the current becomes brutal — moving ~2.5 kA at 48 V means a 0.1 mΩ rise in one connector dissipates ~625 W of localized heat, and per NVIDIA supplying a one-megawatt rack at 54 V requires more than 18,000 A of current, with copper busbars exceeding 200 kg per rack and up to 64 U of power shelves per 1 MW rack — leaving no room for compute (The Register; NVIDIA Technical Blog / Converge Digest, 2025).

3.2 ±400 V / 800 V DC: Mt Diablo and NVIDIA

The OCP Mt Diablo (Diablo 400) spec — co-authored by Microsoft, Meta and Google — defines a disaggregated "sidecar" power rack pushing in-rack distribution from 48 V to ±400 VDC (800 V rail-to-rail), scaling IT racks 100 kW to 1 MW; 400 V was chosen deliberately to piggyback the EV supply chain (connectors, contactors qualified at automotive volume) (OCP, 2025; v0.7.0 spec). NVIDIA's 800 VDC architecture targets 1 MW racks "starting in 2027," aligned with the Kyber (NVL576, ~600 kW) platform; per the NVIDIA Technical Blog ("NVIDIA 800 VDC Architecture Will Power the Next Generation of AI Factories," 20 May 2025, updated 31 July 2025) it delivers "up to 5% improvement in end-to-end power efficiency, up to 70% reduction in maintenance costs... reducing copper requirements by 45% compared to 415 VAC distribution," with full-scale production set to "coincide with NVIDIA Kyber rack-scale systems in 2027" and up to 30% TCO reduction (NVIDIA Technical Blog; ModulEdge, 2026). On conductor gains NVIDIA's own published figures differ and should be cited verbatim: "800 VDC enables 85% more power to be transmitted through the same conductor size" vs 415 VAC (NVIDIA Technical Blog), and separately "the same wire gauge can carry 157% more power than 415 VAC" (NVIDIA, "Building the 800 VDC Ecosystem"). What comes out of the chain: PDU transformers, rack PSUs, and multiple AC/DC stages collapse into the sidecar.

See §A4 for AC vs 48 V vs 800 V DC.

3.3 Status in 2025–26

  • Ships / imminent: Vertiv 800 VDC portfolio in H2 2026; Eaton released an 800 VDC reference architecture in October 2025; STMicro/NVIDIA validated a 12 kW 800 VDC rack-adjacent converter at OCP 2025; first HVDC shipments in small volume by Q3 2026 (Delta an early beneficiary) (szsanyi.com; BigGo, 2026).
  • Committed: Microsoft, Meta, Google (Mt Diablo); NVIDIA + ~20 silicon/power partners (ADI, Infineon, TI, onsemi, Navitas, etc.). AMD has deliberately not committed to a proprietary power path (Drybulb, 2026).
  • Standards/codes not fully ready: NEC treatment of high-voltage DC systems, UL listings, and IEC harmonization lag the hardware; DC arc behavior (arcs don't self-extinguish at zero-crossing) is a live safety gap (szsanyi.com).
  • Pre-provision now: structural allowance and floor space for sidecar power racks, higher per-rack power and liquid cooling, and conduit/pathway headroom — so an AC pod can be converted without a rebuild.

3.4 Delivery and risk

The DC power shelf/sidecar is typically an IT-vendor or hyperscaler-furnished item (OCP-spec), with interface responsibility landing at the busbar — a new owner/tenant demarcation. The stranded-asset risk is real: AC busway sized for 415/480 V in an AI pod that later goes 800 VDC may be rip-and-replace, because "the ratings you pour into the slab cap the rack power you can ever feed" (aidatacenterguide.com). Building an AC hall in 2025 for a workload that flips DC in 2027 is the defining bet of this cycle.

4. Grounding, Bonding and Power Quality

4.1 Grounding schemes

Data-center grounding is designed to exceed code minimum (which targets life safety, not sensitive electronics) (eypmcfinc.com). The core structure is a common bonding network (CBN) with a signal reference grid/structure (SRG) — a copper conductor grid on 0.6–3 m (2–10 ft) centers, ≥#6 AWG, per IEEE 1100 (Emerald Book) and TIA-942 (TIA-942 App G; IEEE 1100). TIA-607 (now -C) defines the bonding busbar hierarchy (PBB/SBB, rack bonding busbars), with 1/0 AWG to the busbar and ≥6 AWG to each cabinet, not serially bonded (ecmag.com; pducables.com). Do SRGs still matter? For modern active-PFC equipment the high-frequency noise case is weaker than in the FIPS-94 era, but SRGs remain standard practice and are often required for equipment warranty (Schneider SRG WP; eypmcfinc.com). Isolated grounds are largely deprecated for noise in favor of the equipotential mesh. TN-S (separate neutral and protective earth throughout) is the preferred earthing arrangement for IT.

4.2 Harmonics and power quality

Legacy diode-rectifier PSUs drew pulsed current and gave data-center harmonics their reputation. Modern server PSUs use active power factor correction (PFC), drawing near-sinusoidal current with THD below 5% and high power factor — "the single most impactful harmonic mitigation development of the past decade" (EmergEdge; 3dfs WP55). This has downgraded the need for K-rated transformers: K-13 suits mixed loads; K-20 is "typically overkill for most modern data centers" (green-data blog) — but an AI hall changes the calculus: thousands of identical, synchronized switching supplies with no load diversity present a concentrated spectrum that can push toward K-20 (QT&E, 2025). Modern transformerless UPS use IGBT active front ends, so input filters aren't needed for low THD (mikeholt forum, practitioners). Active harmonic filters can pull THD from 30–40% to <5% at the board where legacy gear remains (EmergEdge).

4.3 Arc-flash, NFPA 70E, selective coordination

Data centers work energized because uptime makes de-energizing impractical — so an energized electrical work permit under NFPA 70E 130.2(B) is "frequently required, not the exception" (nfpa70e.net). NFPA 70E 130.5(H) requires arc-flash labels on essentially all distribution gear; the arc-flash boundary is where incident energy = 1.2 cal/cm²; PPE Categories 1–4 map to incident energy, and energized work above 40 cal/cm² is not permitted (US Made Supply; payapress). Engineering controls — remote racking, arc-flash maintenance switches (reduced clearing time), arc-resistant switchgear, IR windows — sit above PPE and can move a worker from Cat 4 to outside the boundary entirely (US Made Supply; Bowtie). Selective coordination downstream of the UPS is the discipline that a rack-level fault must trip only its branch device, never the PDU or the UPS — so breaker time-current curves must nest, and the fuse-vs-breaker debate at the rack PDU turns on interrupting capacity: a Bussmann CUBEFuse carries 300 kA IR at 600 V, easily handling the elevated fault current of transformerless 415 V distribution (asbeam.com). Because removing the PDU transformer raises available fault current (§1.2), coordination and SCCR must be re-verified.

4.4 Delivery and risk

The arc-flash/incident-energy study and the grounding study are stamped PE deliverables modeling normal, maintenance and emergency states (Bowtie Engineering). Review at least every 5 years or after any upstream change (payapress). The live risk: a tenant's gear change (new transformer, added load, reconfigured source) invalidates the study and the labels — someone must own re-running it. NFPA 70E 2024 added an electrically-safe-work-condition program audit requirement (e-Hazard).

5. Emergency Power Off and Its Code History

5.1 NEC Article 645.10 and NFPA 75

NEC Article 645 governs IT equipment rooms; 645.10 requires a disconnecting means (EPO) that drops IT power and dedicated HVAC and closes fire/smoke dampers (NEC 2020). The trade: 645 grants relaxed wiring allowances (e.g., loose power whips under a raised-floor plenum, non-plenum cable) in exchange for the EPO. The opt-out: Article 645 is optional — if the room is built to ordinary Chapter 3 (and Article 725/770, now 722) wiring methods, 645 and its EPO do not apply (CSE; thebuildingcodeforum). This "design out" path has existed since at least the 2005 NEC. The 2011 and later editions added zoned EPO and "critical operations data systems" alternatives (645.10(B) exception, tied to Article 685) allowing EPO omission with an approved shutdown procedure, qualified staff, adequate fire protection, and AHJ approval. In the 2026 draft, 645.4 disappears but the six opt-out conditions move to 645.5 with references updated to Article 722 (thebuildingcodeforum, 2025). NFPA 75 adds fire-protection requirements and, at 8.4.2.1, that power be disconnected on activation of a gaseous total-flooding system unless Chapter 4 risk analysis warrants continuous power. NFPA 76 (telecom) has no EPO requirement.

5.2 Why owners opt out, and the accident record

Owners opt out because "the benefits to be reaped are not worth the prerequisites" and because the EPO is itself a major downtime risk (mikeholt practitioners). The record backs them:

  • NYC Transit Rail Control Center, 29 Aug 2021: A ConEd voltage dip was ridden through by the UPS ("both systems worked exactly as intended"); the outage's "most likely cause… was the 'Emergency Power Off' button being manually activated," and the button "did not have a protective cover at the time" (WSP USA investigation report, 8 Sep 2021, via governor.ny.gov).
  • Georgia Tech CCB247 datacenter: ~96-minute outage after "the EPO system was triggered accidentally as a result of power work being carried out in the datacenter" (Georgia Tech TSO notice).
  • Industry statistics: In the Ponemon Institute National Survey on Data Center Outages (sponsored by Emerson Network Power, 2010), "95 percent have had an unplanned outage in the past 24 months," with root causes including "UPS battery failure (65 percent), UPS capacity exceeded (53 percent), accidental EPO/human error (51 percent)"; one UPS vendor logged 20 EPO incidents (13% of all UPS failures) in Jan 2002–Jun 2003, another found 26% of human-error failures were EPO-caused, often involving vendors, delivery persons or cleaning crews (pducables "Ken's Korner"; DataCenterKnowledge, 2007). Uptime confirms accidental EPO is "a recurring cause of downtime" in its AIRs database and does not recommend EPO installation unless compelled by local code.

5.3 How hyperscalers and colos handle it (2025–26)

The prevailing move is to build to Chapter 3 wiring methods and omit the EPO, or to zone it tightly and cover/guard every button, because at scale the accidental-activation risk outweighs the wiring convenience. Human error remains a leading outage cause: the Uptime Institute Annual Outage Analysis 2025 found that "nearly 40% of organizations have suffered a major outage caused by human error over the past three years," and "of these incidents, 85% stem from staff failing to follow procedures or from flaws in the processes and procedures" (Uptime Institute press release, 6 May 2025) — exactly the class of risk a big red button amplifies.

5.4 Delivery and risk

The AHJ and fire marshal decide whether the opt-out flies; the conversation happens early because it dictates wiring methods and room fire-rating. The trap: an EPO designed in to satisfy a code path, then disabled or bypassed by operations later because they fear accidental activation, leaves a non-compliant, unmanaged risk whose ownership is ambiguous — the designer stamped it, but operations altered it.

6. How It Assembles: The Reference Hall's Distribution by Zone

Locked reference facility: 1,000-rack colocation campus, ~40 MW IT, hot US metro (Phoenix/Dallas). Four density zones — 300 racks @ 8 kW, 300 @ 15 kW, 200 @ 40 kW, 200 @ 120 kW (≈39 MW; AI pods = 20% of racks, 61% of load). Design PUE 1.4. Tier III concurrently maintainable standard + one optional 2N suite. Design-build, owner-furnished long-lead gear. ~10 MW phases; Phase 1 ≈10 MW IT in Hall 1, mixed profile. UPS in 2–3 MW blocks at 415/240 V or 480/277 V. 120 kW busway pre-provisioned in AI pods. 15–20% of white/gray space held in reserve. Live explorer counts N+1 blocks of ~2.5 MW. All numbers indicative.

6.0 What redundancy wraps the ~2.5 MW N+1 blocks

A real Tier III hall would wrap those ~2.5 MW N+1 UPS blocks in either a distributed-redundant 3M2/4M3 or a block-redundant (catcher) distribution scheme to achieve concurrent maintainability without full 2N. With 3M2 on 2.5 MW blocks, three blocks serve ~5 MW of usable load (each ≤66.7% = ~1.67 MW normal), so Phase 1's 10 MW needs 2 × (3M2 groups) = 6 blocks installed for ~10 MW usable — versus 8 blocks for 2N (each pair delivers one block of usable load). The block count is the redundancy tax: 6 blocks (3M2) vs 8 blocks (2N) for the same 10 MW.

6.1 Feed paths by zone (indicative)

Zone 1 — 300 racks @ 8 kW (2.4 MW): 1. UPS 415/240 V → 2. floor PDU (transformerless if 415 V) → 3. cable-and-RPP (loads low and stable) → 4. whip → 5. one 415/240 V rack PDU per rack, ~24–32 A → dual-corded. - Redundancy: Tier III via 3M2 upstream; A/B RPP feeds. ~1 rack PDU per rack per side (2 total); ~20–40 racks per RPP.

Zone 2 — 300 racks @ 15 kW (4.5 MW): 1. UPS → 2. PDU → 3. busway (starts to win), ~250–400 A A/B runs → 4. tap-off → 5. one 415/240 V, 30–60 A rack PDU per side → dual-corded. - ~10–15 racks per busway run; 2 rack PDUs per rack (A/B).

Zone 3 — 200 racks @ 40 kW (8 MW): 1. UPS → 2. PDU → 3. overhead busway 400–600 A A/B → 4. tap-off 60–100 A → 5. two 415/240 V, 60 A rack PDUs per side → dual-corded, liquid-cooling interface. - ~6–8 racks per busway run; 2–4 rack PDUs per rack.

Zone 4 — 200 racks @ 120 kW (24 MW) — AI pods, in full detail: 1. UPS output 415/240 V (2–3 MW block, 3M2 for Tier III; the optional suite is 2N). 2. Floor PDU / switchboard, transformerless at 415 V, feeding the pod. 3. 120 kW busway, pre-provisioned, dual A + B runs, sized ~800–1,000 A each (headroom to 1,250 A Starline class) so either run can carry the rack on loss of the other. 4. Tap-off boxes ~160–250 A per rack per side, twist-locked live. 5. Rack PDUs: to land 120 kW you need ≈ 4 × 415 V/60 A circuits per side (each ~34.5 kW usable), i.e. ~2 rack PDUs per side, 4 per rack, OR a shift to 48 V/DC shelves pre-provisioned for 800 VDC conversion. 6. Dual-corded GPU rack (or single-corded with software fault tolerance in the relaxed AI model). - Racks per busway run: ~2–4 at 120 kW before the run's ampacity is exhausted; each pod is its own A/B busway pair.

6.3 Growth to 40 MW

Replicate Phase 1's ~10 MW pattern across four halls/phases; AI pods (Zone 4) dominate load growth (61% of load on 20% of racks), so later phases skew toward 120 kW busway and DC-ready pods. Hold 15–20% of white/gray space in reserve for density escalation (a 40 kW pod today may become 120 kW). MV switchgear and UPS blocks are the long-lead pacing items (40–60 weeks).

6.4 Delivery and risk: owner-to-tenant boundary

  • Wholesale hall: owner delivers shell, power to the PDU output breaker or busway, and cooling; tenant fits out racks, containment, rack PDUs, whips (Sentinel; Obelinf). Schedule/cost risk on the fit-out sits with the tenant; owner carries the long-lead gear risk.
  • Retail hall: owner delivers rack-ready space including rack PDUs and whips; tenant brings IT. Owner carries more of the risk and prices it into $/kW.
  • Pre-provision (owner): busway ampacity, pathway headroom, structural allowance for sidecar DC racks, and reserve UPS blocks. Tenant brings: IT, and increasingly the DC power shelf/sidecar at the busbar demarcation — a new interface to negotiate.

Open Questions / Changing Fast in 2026

  • 800 VDC timing and standards: hardware (Vertiv H2 2026, Eaton ref arch Oct 2025, NVIDIA Kyber 2027) is ahead of NEC/UL/IEC treatment of HVDC and DC arc-flash — a code gap owners must watch.
  • Redundancy relaxation for AI: how far "N+1 is enough / three nines is enough" spreads from training clusters to inference, and whether single-corded GPU racks become normal, is unsettled.
  • EPO practice: the 2026 NEC reshuffle (645.4 → 645.5) keeps the opt-out; expect continued omission at scale, but AHJ variance remains.
  • Busway supply: high-ampacity (up to 6,300 A) busway lead times and copper/aluminum pricing are a live constraint on AI build schedules.

Glossary

  • UPS output bus — the clean-power switchboard where distribution begins (this chapter's starting boundary).
  • PDU — floor-standing power distribution unit; may hold a step-down/isolation transformer and branch breakers.
  • RPP — remote power panel; a breaker panel near the racks fed by cable home-runs.
  • Busway (busbar trunking) — prefabricated overhead conductor track with plug-in tap-off boxes.
  • Tap-off / plug-in box — the twist-lock unit that draws a branch circuit off busway, live.
  • Whip — flexible cable drop from tap-off or RPP to the rack.
  • Rack PDU (rPDU) — in-rack outlet strip converting the branch circuit to server receptacles.
  • STS — static transfer switch; thyristor-based, ~4 ms source transfer.
  • ATS — automatic (electromechanical) transfer switch; slower, simpler.
  • A/B feed — two independent power paths to a dual-corded load.
  • Dual-corded — IT gear with two PSUs on independent feeds.
  • N / N+1 / 2N / 2N+1 — capacity redundancy levels (exact / one spare / full duplicate / duplicate plus spare).
  • 3M2 / 4M3 — distributed-redundant "three-to-make-two" / "four-to-make-three."
  • Block redundant / catcher — active blocks plus a shared reserve block reached via STS.
  • Concurrently maintainable — any component can be serviced without dropping load (Tier III).
  • Fault tolerant — survives any single fault with no load impact (Tier IV).
  • MTBF / MTTR — mean time between failures / mean time to repair.
  • PUE — power usage effectiveness (facility ÷ IT power).
  • PFC — active power factor correction in modern PSUs.
  • K-rated transformer — transformer derated to tolerate harmonic heating.
  • SRG / CBN — signal reference grid / common bonding network for equipotential grounding.
  • EPO — emergency power off; the "big red button" of NEC 645.10.
  • ORv3 — OCP Open Rack v3, the 48 V power-shelf rack standard.
  • Mt Diablo / Diablo 400 — OCP disaggregated ±400 VDC "sidecar" power-rack spec.
  • Sidecar rack — a rack of power shelves feeding adjacent compute racks over DC.
  • OFE — owner-furnished equipment.
  • AHJ — authority having jurisdiction.

What Surprised the Researcher

  1. Uptime doesn't sell availability numbers. The ubiquitous "Tier III = 99.982%" table was removed from the Uptime Tier Standard in 2009 — the certification is about topology and operations, yet the percentages persist industry-wide as if they were certified. The whole industry cites a number Uptime disowns.
  2. The 48 V rack hit a physical wall shockingly fast. A one-megawatt rack on 54 V requires more than 18,000 A and up to 64U of power shelves — more than a full rack — leaving no room for compute. The move to 800 VDC isn't optimization; it's "run out of copper" necessity.
  3. The EPO button is a leading cause of the outages it's meant to prevent. Vendor data attributing 13–26% of failures to EPO and a survey showing 51% of outaged sites hit by accidental EPO/human error — plus the 2021 NYC subway control center taken down by an uncovered EPO — make the "big red button" one of the least-loved safety devices in the building. Uptime flatly does not recommend installing it unless code forces you.
  4. Removing a transformer makes the system more dangerous, not less. Going transformerless to 415 V saves copper and a conversion stage but triples available fault current at the PDU (~17.6 kA → ~56 kA in one vendor's figures), quietly shifting cost and risk into the coordination study and arc-flash labels.
  5. AI is un-learning redundancy. After decades of "dual-corded everything," the frontier is single-corded GPU racks and N+1 compute because a training job can checkpoint-restart — software fault tolerance is eating electrical fault tolerance.
