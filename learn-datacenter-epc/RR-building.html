Topic 4 — The Building and Physical Infrastructure of a New-Build Data Center (2025–2026)

The Desktop Research report for Topic 4, run 2026-09-25 from briefs/ and published as delivered: no editing, no lint. The chapter and appendix were distilled from it; where they and this report disagree, the chapter is the course's position.

A working-knowledge chapter for the reference facility: a 1,000-rack, ~40 MW colocation campus on the outskirts of a hot US metro (Phoenix/Dallas), Tier III concurrently maintainable, design-build, four halls ultimate, Hall 1 ≈ 10 MW IT on day one. Density mix: 300 racks @ 8 kW, 300 @ 15 kW, 200 @ 40 kW, 200 @ 120 kW (liquid-cooled AI pods).

How to read this chapter. The building is the cheap part and the slow part at the same time: it is only ~10–15% of construction cost but it sets the rhythm every other trade follows. Everything below is written so you can ask an engineer, developer or GC the right question and understand the answer. Every worked number for the reference facility is labeled indicative — they are planning-grade, not stamped design.

1. Shell and Core — Building Forms, Duration, Who Builds What

1.1 Structural systems in 2025–26

What's changing fast: Meta's "Prometheus" campus in New Albany, Ohio used fabric-covered tent structures (~125,000 sf each; eight standing by April 2026) to accelerate enclosure — not the full building. SemiAnalysis notes this "trades away some of the durability and long-term flexibility of a permanent concrete or steel building." AWS is moving to narrower, more repeatable structures "organized around the systems installed inside" — less building per MW, shorter spans, fewer field interfaces. The industry thread: shell modularity is increasingly about design simplification, not just prefabrication.

1.2 Building forms and column grids

  • Single-storey slab hyperscale box vs multi-storey colo in land-constrained metros. QTS opened Northern Virginia's first three-storey data center, ultimately four 2.5 MW halls per floor, with a massive freight lift and floors filled quadrant-by-quadrant because cooling relies on convection across the three storeys.
  • Column grids: standard spacings are 8.4, 9.6, 10.8 or 12 m in both directions, sized to modular rack rows (1.2 m rack depth, 1.2 m cold aisle, 1.5–1.8 m hot aisle). Hyperscalers push for 20–30 m+ column-free spans for uninterrupted rows; colo/multi-storey uses tighter 9–15 m grids to keep structural depths sane.
  • What changes at 3–5 storeys: you add freight elevators (a rigging constraint, §5), post-tensioned or composite floors instead of slab-on-grade (§2.4), fuel storage rules (NYC: all fuel on lowest floor post-9/11), and cooling has to be designed as a vertical system. A current example: Brightray/BUUU's Macao design targets ~40 MW of IT in a building of up to five storeys on ~9,000 m² using prefabricated modules, Tier III, delivery targeted Q3 2027 — illustrating the multi-storey-AI direction.

1.3 Prefab and modular

Prefabricated precast is described by GBC Engineers as "the most favorable solution for modern data center construction." One real hyperscale case (via iRecruit): over 600 exterior wall panels prefabricated off-site in two months; structural steel mobilized in six weeks; first turnovers at 11 months; all data halls under 18 months. Power and cooling skids and modular electrical rooms (eHouses) let owners overlap factory build with site work. The flexibility cost: you commit to a repeatable design and lose some field adaptability.

1.4 Duration and cost share

  • Shell's share of capex: the building shell is only 10–15% of total construction cost (Terrapin/TCG) — an inversion of normal commercial buildings. MEP is the project at 75–80% of capex (consistent with Topic 2). "The mechanical and electrical systems are the project. The building is the box they go in."
  • Shell-and-core cost/MW: JLL's 2026 Global Data Center Outlook puts global average shell-and-core at $11.3M/MW in 2026 — a 6% rise from $10.7 million in 2025 and up from $7.7 million in 2020 (~7% CAGR) — covering "the physical structure plus base power and cooling distribution"; JLL adds that tenant AI fit-out "can cost as much as $25 million per MW on top." AI-ready halls cost far more on MEP density, not the frame.
  • Cost per watt (all-in): Turner & Townsend's Data Centre Construction Cost Index 2025–2026 shows traditional air-cooled cost/watt rose 5.5% in 2025 (down from 9.0% in 2024), and — per NAM lead Paul Barry — "We have identified the 7-10% cost premium based on like-for-like analysis of traditional air-cooled and high-density, liquid-cooled data centers in the US of a similar IT capacity" (priciest markets: Tokyo $15.2/W, Singapore $14.5/W, Zurich $14.2/W). AI-optimized facilities can reach $20M/MW+ vs $10–12M/MW for standard builds.
  • What moves shell $/sf: seismic zone (adds 8–15% to structural+mechanical), soil (deep foundations if bearing is poor), storey count, span, hardening (§6), and labor market (union $140–200+/hr vs $65–95 in right-to-work states).

1.5 Sequencing and interfaces (design-build)

Indicative critical-path sequence for a 10–40 MW building: 1. Site work / earthwork — ~4–12 weeks (grading, underground utilities, subgrade to bearing spec) 2. Foundations / slab — overlaps; combined civil+foundations+shell ~12–36 weeks 3. Structural shell — steel erection can be ~2–6 weeks (15 days on one Turner project); tilt-up/precast panels within the same window 4. Dry-in / weathertight — milestone at end of shell 5. MEP rough-in — ~12–24 weeks (MEP was ~34 weeks total on one real project); "the densest, riskiest stretch of the build" 6. Equipment set — ~4–12 weeks (owner-furnished long-lead gear: transformers, switchgear, gensets, UPS) 7. Fit-out — begins in the "early access period toward the end of construction" (CBRE), overlapping rather than following 8. Commissioning / integrated systems testing (L1–L5) — 2–8 weeks minimum, more commonly 3–6 months

Totals (indicative): vertical construction ~12–24 months for a single building at this scale; total ready-for-service (construction + Cx) ~18–30 months; full lifecycle including permitting/design 3–6 years.

Where interfaces bite: embeds and penetrations (cast into the slab/panels before MEP knows final equipment), pad tolerances for owner-furnished skids, the rigging path (§5), and the design-freeze-to-OEM-data gate. Under design-build the GC carries schedule and weather risk on the shell; skid suppliers carry factory-slot risk; the owner carries long-lead procurement risk (OFE). Failures during integrated systems testing delay projects by an average of 3.8 months.

2. Structural Loading for Racks Over a Tonne

2.1 The floor-load numbers owners specify today

  • Code minimum: ASCE 7-22 specifies 100 psf distributed OR a 2,000-lb point load for "computer use — access floor systems." UFC 3-301-01 specifies 150 psf for computer/server spaces.
  • Industry reality: new data centers ask for at least 150 psf, and 250 psf where full/narrow/tall cabinets are expected (TechTarget). Intel's guidance recommended 350 psf as far back as 2012. For AI halls the specification is climbing to 250–400+ psf.
  • The discrepancy matters: there is a real gap between the ASCE minimum (100–150 psf) and industry expectation (250–400 psf). Trust the higher owner spec — the code minimum is a floor, not a target.

2.2 How a 1,500 kg rack translates to the slab

The NVIDIA GB200 NVL72 (the 120 kW pod class) is the governing case: - Weight: per the rax.ae GB200 guide, "The GB200 NVL72 rack weighs approximately 1,360 kg (3,000 lbs) before coolant is added. With coolant, the total weight can approach 1,500 kg. Most traditional raised-floor data centers are designed for 500 to 750 kg per rack position" (NVIDIA/Supermicro datasheets, 2024). - Footprint: 600 mm W × 1,068 mm D ≈ 0.64 m². - Point load: ~1,360 kg / 0.64 m² ≈ 2,125 kg/m² (~435 psf) — Introl computes the compute rack "concentrates 1,500kg in just 0.8 square meters, creating point loads of 1,875 kg/m²... Standard raised floors rated for 1,000 kg/m² require steel reinforcement plates"; ModulEdge cites "over 2,000 kg/m²." Either way, it exceeds raised floors typically rated 1,200–1,500 kg/m². - Per caster/foot: ~1,360 kg on four points ≈ 340 kg (750 lb) each, on contact areas of a few square inches — this is the concentrated load that punches through standard tiles. - Design response: purpose-built AI facilities use reinforced slab-on-grade rated for ≥1,500 kg per rack position with margin for CDUs and piping. A legacy raised-floor hall is "structurally the wrong building" (ModulEdge).

2.3 OCP and vendor rack weights (dated)

  • OCP Open Rack V3 (ORv3): frame rated to 1,400 kg (3,080 lb) max payload; 600/750 mm W × 1,068 mm D × 2,286 mm H; 48 V DC busbar to 18 kW/shelf, up to 120 kW; seismic kit offered as an accessory (Rittal, nVent, Emcor OCP listings, 2023–2025). ORv3.1 is the current base spec as of March 2026.
  • NVIDIA GB200 NVL72: ~1,360 kg / 120 kW nominal (130–132 kW observed); GB300 "Blackwell Ultra" ~135–142 kW.
  • Legacy: a standard air-cooled rack is ~300 lb empty; a typical enterprise rack position was designed for 500–750 kg.

2.4 Liquid cooling's added weight

  • CDU when flooded reaches ~3 tons, requiring ~800 kg/m² floor capacity locally (Introl). CDUs range 70 kW–2.3 MW (Vertiv CoolChip) up to 14 MW (Trane); in-row CDUs cool 5–6 racks each.
  • Manifolds, filled pipe racks, in-row heat exchangers add distributed weight along the row: a liquid-cooled row carries substantially more kg/m than an equivalent air-cooled row once you add stainless piping, coolant (often 20–35% water-glycol), CDUs and leak-detection trays.
  • Rule of thumb: liquid buys back white space (70–85% less per MW) but "busway, stainless piping, leak detection, and slab reinforcement consume the saving" (Global Data Center Hub).

2.5 Slab, soil and deflection

  • Slab-on-grade: a conventional office slab is 4–6" with light rebar; a data hall typically requires a 12–18" slab with heavy rebar at 6–8" centers both ways, with supplemental reinforcement under equipment (Buildermuse). A single hyperscale hall uses 8,000–15,000 cubic yards of structural concrete.
  • Soil/geotechnical: slab capacity depends on subgrade stiffness (k-value) more than bearing strength; poor soils force piles/drilled shafts. Battery rooms create loads approaching 1,000 psf.
  • Multi-storey: upper floors use post-tensioned or composite floors designed for both flexure and vibration; differential settlement limits are tight (raised-floor pedestals tolerate only 5–10 mm between adjacent pedestals).
  • Deflection/vibration: floors near their rating creep — a 2 mm sag over 12–24 months binds doors and misaligns trays; excessive vibration is linked to a 15–25% increase in drive failures (per a Google reliability study cited by Huiya).

2.6 Seismic bracing and anchorage

  • Framework: ASCE 7-22 Chapter 13 governs nonstructural anchorage. Data centers are commonly assigned Risk Category IV (essential facility) under IBC, giving an importance factor Ip = 1.5 → ~50% higher design forces than ordinary buildings.
  • Where required: engineered anchorage is required in SDC C and above. A site's SDS drives this: Reno ≈ 1.3g vs Dallas ≈ 0.1g — so the reference facility (Phoenix or Dallas) is a low-seismic site where anchorage is comparatively light, but Risk Category IV still applies.
  • Base isolation vs bracing: base isolation can cut seismic forces by up to ~70% but is reserved for high-seismic regions (California/Nevada); in Dallas/Phoenix conventional bracing and anchorage suffice.
  • Who supplies what: the rack maker supplies the seismic-rated cabinet and shake-table (AC156) certification and an optional seismic kit; the building must provide the anchorage design, embeds, and PE-stamped calculations for racks, pipes, busway, UPS and generators.

The shift: slab with overhead distribution is now "the standard in large hyperscale deployments" (Data Centre Magazine). Liquid cooling reduces the need for underfloor air, further favoring slab. Raised floor persists in retail colo and where tenants still want underfloor flexibility. For the reference AI pods (§2), slab is the correct choice.

3.2 Raised-floor engineering (where RAF is still used)

  • Pedestal heights: 12–18" for smaller sites; 24–36" for high-density underfloor air.
  • Tile ratings (CISCA): concentrated, uniform and rolling loads; standard uniform 250–600 psf, high-density up to 2,500 psf; the pedestal/stringer understructure is usually the limiting factor, not the panel.
  • What a 1,500 kg rack does to a standard tile: per Spheron's GB200 guide, "Standard raised-floor tiles are rated for 250 kg per tile. A fully loaded GB200 NVL72 rack requires reinforced flooring or a dedicated slab with verified load capacity before installation." This is the single clearest reason legacy RAF halls can't host Blackwell.
  • Leak detection and drainage run under the floor where liquid or CRAC condensate is present.

3.3 Overhead vs underfloor as a whole-building choice

Overhead (power busway, cable tray, cooling pipe) gives shorter air paths, frees floor for revenue equipment, and is easier to maintain live — but concentrates leak and fire exposure above the racks and needs clear height (§4.4). Underfloor keeps cabling out of sight but restricts airflow and buries leaks. The 2025–26 default for new high-density build: overhead everything on a slab.

4. Room Topology and the Space Program

4.1 The rooms

  • White space: the data hall (raised floor or slab) where racks live.
  • Gray space: electrical rooms, UPS rooms, battery rooms, mechanical rooms, chiller/pump yards, MEP galleries.
  • CDU rooms / pipe galleries for liquid distribution.
  • Telecom entrance rooms and meet-me rooms (space only — Topic 9 covers the network).
  • NOC, security, dock and staging, offices and support.

4.2 Ratios and rules of thumb

  • White-to-gray ratio historically ~1:1 for data centers (vs offices, which need far less support space). As density rose the ratio moved against white space: each square foot of white now demands more gray behind it. Liquid cooling and 2N redundancy push gray-to-white above 1:1.
  • White space share: on average ~65% of total in older/air designs; falls with density.
  • Treat any published ratio as a planning figure, not a design rule (Anvilfield) — size gray space from actual load and redundancy.

4.3 Indicative area program for a 10 MW hall (reference Hall 1)

All figures indicative, planning-grade. - Hall 1 IT load ≈ 10 MW. Mixed density; assume white space sized around the AI pods that dominate area at lower density and the dense pods that compress it. - White space: ~30,000–45,000 sf indicative for 10 MW at this mixed density (dense AI pods shrink footprint per MW; air-cooled zones expand it). - Gray space (electrical/UPS/battery/mechanical): roughly 1:1 with white at Tier III with liquid → ~30,000–45,000 sf. - CDU/pipe gallery: allocate ≥50% extra mechanical space beyond initial buildout for cooling growth. - Dock/staging + support + NOC/security: ~10–15% of gross. - Gross-to-white ratio: plan ~2.0–2.5× white for a liquid-cooled Tier III hall → gross ~75,000–110,000 sf for the Hall 1 block. Label every number indicative and confirm against the OPR.

4.4 Code separations as building matters

  • NFPA 855 (lithium-ion batteries): the binding edition depends on the IFC your AHJ adopted (IFC 2021 → NFPA 855-2020; IFC 2024 → NFPA 855-2023; a 2026 edition released Sept 2025). Key building constraints: lithium-ion limited to 600 kWh per fire-enclosure/battery room (spacing exceptions via UL 9540A); 3 ft spacing between cabinets/walls; 1- or 2-hour fire-rated barriers; not permitted below grade; rooftop only with AHJ approval; not above the reach of fire-department ladders. Ventilation: continuous ≥1 CFM/sf or intermittent triggered at 25% LFL; explosion control (deflagration venting) where UL 9540A shows a hazard; 2 hours standby power for safety systems. Sprinklers commonly 0.3 gpm/ft² over 2,500 ft² as a baseline. A Hazard Mitigation Analysis (HMA) is step one.
  • NFPA 75/76: fire-rated separation of the computer room from other occupancies.
  • IBC: occupancy classification and egress travel-distance limits.
  • Generator/fuel setbacks: local; fuel storage rules can force ground-floor tanks in multi-storey.

4.5 Vertical and horizontal topology

  • Clear/floor-to-floor height: hyperscale AI halls now specify 5–6 m floor-to-ceiling (hot-aisle containment, direct-to-chip lines and dense cable trays consume overhead space); colo runs 3.8–4.5 m, "a structural barrier for any tenant arriving with liquid-cooled AI equipment." This is why 6–9 m floor-to-floor is now common in new AI build.
  • Gallery widths for pipe and busway are set by maintainability and rigging.
  • Hall-to-hall separation for tenants and fire uses rated walls; the pod is the repeatable unit (the reference facility's 120 kW AI pods with pre-provisioned manifolds and busway).

5. Loading Docks and Equipment Logistics

5.1 Dock sizing

  • Dock doors: standardize on 9′ W × 10′ H for modern 102″ trailers; use 10′×10′ flex bays for oversized loads. Doors on 12′ centers minimum (14′ recommended) to reduce congestion and allow staging between docks.
  • Grade-level (drive-in) doors: ground-level for vans/forklifts and rolling heavy gear straight in; grade doors range 7′×7′ to 12′×14′.
  • Levelers: hydraulic, sized for dynamic (not static) load; DC gear argues for 40,000–45,000 lb+ capacity given 3,000 lb racks plus rigging. Standard leveler 6–6.5′ wide × 8′ long.
  • Staging/laydown: a dedicated staging/unboxing room outside the white space (contamination isolation — the canonical rule from Snevely's Enterprise Data Center Design) sized to hold multiple truckloads of racks; ~510 sf per 60′ trailer position as a warehouse proxy. Truck court depth 120′+.
  • Colo vs hyperscale: colo provides isolated white space + shared plant and secure caged storage per tenant; hyperscale builds numerous loading areas for repeated deliveries. Secure unboxing rooms with caging/security are standard.

5.2 The rigging path

The route often governs the slab more than the final rack position: corridor widths, door sizes, turning radii, and slab/wheel loads along the path must all carry the heaviest single load. In multi-storey buildings, freight-elevator capacity is a hard constraint (QTS built a "massive freight lift"). Because the equipment list is rarely final at design, the path is designed to a governing envelope (heaviest transformer, longest genset) rather than a known list — embeds and knock-out panels are provided for later gear.

5.3 Heavy gear: highway to pad

  • A 100–200-tonne transformer or a 3 MW generator moves on multi-axle lowboys, dual-lane trailers, or SPMTs for the heaviest urban sets. Medium-power transformers up to ~90,000 lb move on multi-axle trailers.
  • On site: lift planning evaluates ground bearing, crane pads/mats, temporary roads, and load paths before anything moves; the crane, trailer and load all impose heavy point loads on the ground.
  • Crane sizing: mid-range all-terrains (~90 t) pick chillers/gensets; 190- and 275-ton cranes run the largest transformer and gear placements. When "the asset on the hook took years to build and cannot be quickly replaced," a single engineered, on-time set is the goal.
  • Set sequence: transformers and switchgear are often set before the shell is closed (or through knock-out panels), which is why the rigging path and dry-in milestone are coupled.

5.4 Fit-out logistics

Fit-out overlaps late construction (CBRE "early access period"). Rack rollout is now industrial-scale — "the old days of deploying a rack at a time are long gone" (Data Center Frontier). A Meta/AnyLogic study modeled rack receiving throughput over a one-week window: baseline 40% of target at 3.7 days per rack cycle; optimized to 92% at 2.2 days — the dock → staging → hall → elevator chain is the bottleneck, not the hall itself. Busway tap-offs cut per-rack deployment "from days to hours" vs conduit/cable pulls, which is why the reference facility pre-provisions 120 kW busway in the AI pods. Rack rollout rate and tenant move-ins must be scheduled against dock capacity.

6. Hardening, Roof and Envelope, and Noise at the Fence

6.1 Seismic

As §2.6: Risk Category III or IV under IBC/ASCE 7-22; Category IV → Ip = 1.5, ~50% higher design forces and higher wind design speeds. Engineered anchorage required at SDC C+. The reference site (Dallas SDS ≈ 0.1g; Phoenix similar low-moderate) is low-seismic — conventional bracing/anchorage, not base isolation. ASCE 7 Ch. 13 governs all nonstructural anchorage (racks, pipe, busway, UPS, gensets). Base isolation (up to ~70% force reduction) is a California/Nevada tool.

FM Global's role: FM data sheets frequently "become deal terms" — insurers shape the design more than code in many cases. FM 1-28 wind pressures run ~15% above ASCE 7; FM prefers structural concrete roof decks because loss experience with steel decks is worse in wind/tornado. Dallas sits in a severe-hail/tornado corridor (the 1995 Mayfest hailstorm), so hail and wind-borne debris drive envelope and rooftop-equipment protection even though seismic is minimal.

6.3 Roof and envelope

  • Rooftop dead loads: chillers, dry coolers and air handlers impose large concentrated loads requiring dunnage (steel support frames) and screens; PV adds further dead load. (Cooling plant sizing itself is Topic 7 — here it's a roof-load and anchorage question.)
  • Rooftop anchorage: ASCE 7 Ch. 13 and FM 1-28 govern anchorage of roof-mounted equipment against uplift.
  • Membrane in a hot climate: high-albedo/reflective single-ply (TPO) reduces heat gain; must meet FM uplift + hail ratings.
  • Insulation, air-tightness, vapor control: a building cooled 24/7 in Phoenix/Dallas needs a continuous air/vapor barrier to prevent condensation and cooling loss — governed by ASHRAE 90.4 (data-center-specific), 90.1, and the IECC envelope provisions.

6.4 Noise as a permitting constraint

This is now a top-three community-opposition issue and a real schedule risk. - Sources at the fence: continuous cooling fans/towers, transformers and switchgear typically 60–80 dBA at the property line; generator load tests 80–100 dBA (usually exempt as emergency-equipment testing). - Typical ordinance limits: day (7am–10pm) often 55–65 dBA; night (10pm–7am) often 45–55 dBA at the property line, measured in dBA with Leq/Lmax/L10/L90; low-frequency (<100 Hz) analyzed separately because it travels farther. - Documented disputes 2023–2026: Prince William County, VA supervisors voted 7-1 in 2023 to remove the nighttime HVAC exemption from county code 14-4 (residential limits 60 dBA daytime / 55 dBA night), and the revised ordinance adds steady-tonal-noise enforcement — "a whine, hum, rumble, or buzz" — with first-time violations as Class 4 Misdemeanors effective May 1, 2026. Chandler, AZ adopted Zoning Code Amendment PLH22-0053 / Ordinance No. 5033 unanimously on Dec. 5, 2022 (effective Jan. 5, 2023) after roughly a decade of Brittany Heights complaints (the data center arrived late 2014), and on Dec. 11, 2025 the City Council voted unanimously to reject a New York developer's rezoning for a 43,000-sq-ft data center despite lobbying by former Sen. Kyrsten Sinema. A wave of nuisance class actions hit facilities in 2026 (Vineland NJ, Dowagiac MI, North Tonawanda NY; Hood County TX crypto). Nearly one-third of Virginia's data centers sit within 200 ft of residentially zoned land. - Mitigation and its penalty: acoustic enclosures, sound barriers/walls, low-noise fans, and setbacks — each adds cost and footprint. Generators enclosed to ~70 dBA at 23 ft appear in draft ordinances (Albemarle County). The building consequence: larger setbacks eat developable land, and enclosures add capex and maintenance access constraints.

8. Open Questions and What's Changing Fast in 2026

  • 2,000 kg racks: GB300 and Rubin-class ("Kyber" ~600 kW/rack in H2 2027, NVIDIA's 1 MW rack announced at OCP 2025) will push point loads and CDU weights well past today's specs — floor and slab specs are a moving target.
  • Multi-storey AI halls: convection-coupled cooling, freight-lift rigging limits, and post-tensioned floors are being worked out live (QTS, Brightray Macao).
  • Prefab share: precast/modular and even fabric "tents" (Meta Prometheus) are rising, trading durability/flexibility for schedule — the durable share of new build is an open question.
  • Noise disputes: ordinances are tightening toward 24-hour limits and nuisance litigation is proliferating (2026 class actions) — setback/enclosure requirements may reshape site layouts.
  • Data on 10–40 MW phase durations is thin in tier-1 published sources; most week-level numbers come from GC blogs and one 2011 Turner schedule. Treat all durations as planning-grade.

9. Glossary

  • Shell and core: the structure, envelope and base building — the "box" before MEP fit-out.
  • Tilt-up: concrete wall panels cast flat on the slab and craned upright.
  • Precast: structural/enclosure concrete cast in a factory and shipped to site.
  • IMP: insulated metal panel — steel-framed envelope cladding.
  • Column grid / bay: the spacing of structural columns; sets rack-row layout (8.4–12 m typical).
  • Slab-on-grade: concrete floor poured directly on prepared ground (12–18" in data halls).
  • Post-tensioned floor: slab pre-compressed with steel tendons; used on upper storeys.
  • Raised access floor (RAF): elevated tile floor on pedestals over a plenum.
  • Point load: concentrated weight on a small area (a rack caster), vs distributed psf.
  • psf / kN/m² / kg/m²: units of floor loading (1 kN/m² ≈ 20.9 psf).
  • CDU: coolant distribution unit — isolates and pumps the IT liquid loop (heavy when filled).
  • Manifold: the pipe header distributing coolant to racks in a row.
  • White space / gray space: IT hall vs support-infrastructure space.
  • Meet-me room: carrier-neutral interconnection space (allocated here as area only).
  • Dunnage: steel support frames for rooftop equipment.
  • Freeboard: design height added above a flood elevation.
  • Risk Category (III/IV): IBC/ASCE 7 occupancy class setting importance factor and design forces.
  • SDC: Seismic Design Category (A–F) combining site shaking and Risk Category.
  • Ip: component importance factor for nonstructural anchorage (1.5 for RC IV).
  • AC156: shake-table test protocol certifying equipment survives seismic forces.
  • FM Global data sheet: insurer loss-prevention standard (1-28 wind, 1-34 hail, etc.).
  • NFPA 855: standard for stationary energy-storage (battery-room) installation.
  • RFS: ready-for-service — the date the facility is energized, commissioned and handed over.
  • SPMT: self-propelled modular transporter for the heaviest loads.
  • Rigging path: the route heavy equipment travels from dock to final position.

10. What Surprised the Researcher

  1. The building is only 10–15% of cost but sets the whole schedule — the shell is cheap yet its pace governs every trade behind it.
  2. The rigging path can govern the slab more than the rack does — you design the floor for the heaviest thing that ever rolls across it, not where it ends up.
  3. A single GB200 rack (~1,360 kg on 0.64 m²) exceeds the rating of most existing raised floors — legacy halls are "structurally the wrong building," not just under-powered.
  4. Insurers (FM Global) often set the design harder than code — FM 1-28 wind runs ~15% above ASCE 7 and FM effectively bans steel roof decks in tornado country.
  5. Noise is now a project-killing permitting risk — 2026 saw a wave of nuisance class actions and 24-hour dBA limits, with Chandler AZ rejecting a data center outright after a decade of complaints.
  6. Liquid cooling saves white space but the weight and reinforcement claw much of it back — the slab, stainless pipe, CDUs and leak detection eat the footprint win.
