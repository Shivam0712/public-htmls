Appendix: Building and Physical Infrastructure

Sources and fine detail behind the chapter. Sections are numbered so the chapter can point at them.

The detail behind the Topic 4 chapter: shell cost and duration, the four structural systems and multi-storey, floor loads for racks over a tonne and what liquid adds, seismic anchorage, raised floor against slab, the space program and the separations that size rooms, docks and the rigging path, the hardened envelope, noise at the fence, and reference Hall 1 worked through. The chapter cites sections by number (APX-building §n). Sources are in parentheses as the research gave them; "(verify)" marks figures flagged as thin, secondary, 2026-dated or borrowed by analogy. Every reference-facility number is indicative. Delivery models are Topic 1's appendix, the OPR Topic 2's, the site Topic 3's; the machines in these rooms are Topics 5 to 9.

Cost and duration evidence

The shell's share. The shell is about 10–15% of total construction cost, an inversion of ordinary commercial buildings (Terrapin/TCG, 2026). Topic 1's teardown has core, shell and architectural at 14% of an air-cooled US build and 9% of a liquid-cooled one (Turner & Townsend, Data Centre Construction Cost Index 2025–26); liquid moves spend toward mechanical while MEP stays at 75–80%. "The building is the box they go in."

Why JLL's number differs. JLL puts global shell-and-core at $11.3M/MW in 2026, up from $10.7M in 2025 and $7.7M in 2020, with AI fit-out "as much as $25 million per MW on top" (JLL, Global Data Center Outlook 2026). Its scope is "the physical structure plus base power and cooling distribution," a powered shell, not the 10–15% "shell" above (verify: not comparable; the chapter uses the T&T share). T&T's index shows air-cooled cost per watt up 5.5% in 2025 after 9.0% in 2024, and a "7–10% cost premium based on like-for-like analysis of traditional air-cooled and high-density, liquid-cooled data centers in the US" (Paul Barry, Turner & Townsend). Tokyo ($15.2/W), Singapore ($14.5) and Zurich ($14.2) lead; AI-optimised facilities reach $20M/MW against $10–12M/MW standard, the difference being MEP density, not the frame.

What moves the shell's $/sf. Seismic zone (8–15% on structure and mechanical in a high-seismic region), poor soil forcing deep foundations, storey count and span, hardening (§10), and labour at $140–200+ an hour in union markets against $65–95 in right-to-work states (research synthesis). None is a rack question.

Duration. Indicative critical path for a 10–40 MW building: earthwork 4–12 weeks; civil, foundations and shell together 12–36 weeks; shell erection 2–6 weeks (15 days on one Turner project); dry-in ends the shell; MEP rough-in 12–24 weeks, "the densest, riskiest stretch of the build"; equipment set 4–12 weeks; fit-out from the "early access period toward the end of construction" (CBRE); commissioning L1–L5 commonly three to six months. Vertical construction 12–24 months, ready-for-service 18–30. Integrated systems test failures delay projects 3.8 months on average (research synthesis). Week-level data for 10–40 MW phases is thin, mostly general-contractor blogs and one 2011 Turner schedule (verify: planning-grade).

Shell systems in detail

Four systems dominate; the choice is regional and owner-driven more than technical (research synthesis, 2025–26).

Concrete tilt-up. Panels cast flat on the slab and craned upright. Fast for a single-storey box, lowest $/sf on flat campuses, 8–12 m grids, high thermal mass, good wind and ballistic mass. Sunbelt hyperscale and colocation.

Precast concrete. Factory-cast panels and structure (Clark Pacific AccelCore, High Concrete). The fastest enclosure: about 48 hours' cure in the plant against up to 28 days cast-in-place; 8–12 m grids; high mass; FM prefers concrete decks for wind and tornado; a moderate premium over tilt-up. GBC Engineers call it "the most favorable solution for modern data center construction." One hyperscale case: over 600 exterior panels prefabricated in two months, first turnovers at 11 months, all halls under 18 (iRecruit case).

Structural steel with insulated metal panel. Off-site frame erected in 2–6 weeks; 12 m and longer spans; flexible for multi-storey; low mass; steel decks need heavier design for tornado under FM 1-28; volatile with steel pricing. Multi-storey colocation in land-constrained metros.

Hybrid, modular and prefabricated. Precast shell with power and cooling skids and eHouses, or factory-built halls. Highest schedule certainty because factory and site run in parallel; a premium per unit recovered in schedule; the price is a repeatable design and lost field adaptability. Hyperscalers with reference designs.

The 2026 extremes (verify: recent). Meta's Prometheus campus in New Albany, Ohio used fabric tents of about 125,000 sf each, eight standing by April 2026, to accelerate enclosure; this "trades away some of the durability and long-term flexibility of a permanent concrete or steel building" (SemiAnalysis, April 2026). AWS is moving to narrower structures "organized around the systems installed inside," with fewer field interfaces. Modularity is increasingly design simplification, not only prefabrication.

Reference choice. Tilt-up, one building per 10 MW hall, 12 m grid with column-free centre bays for the AI pods, concrete roof deck. Precast would be taken only to buy weeks, and §12's schedule does not need them.

Forms, grids and multi-storey

Grids. Standard column spacings are 8.4, 9.6, 10.8 or 12 m both ways, sized to rows of 1.2 m rack depth, 1.2 m cold aisle and 1.5–1.8 m hot aisle (research synthesis). A row with its two aisles is about 3.9–4.2 m, so a 12 m bay takes three rows and 8.4 m takes two. Hyperscalers push for 20–30 m column-free spans; colocation and multi-storey use 9–15 m. The reference hall takes 12 m and drops columns from the centre bays, where a 120 kW pod's rows, CDUs and manifolds tolerate a column least.

Multi-storey. QTS opened Northern Virginia's first three-storey data center, ultimately four 2.5 MW halls per floor, with a "massive freight lift" and floors filled quadrant by quadrant because cooling relies on convection across the storeys (QTS). Three to five storeys add freight elevators as the rigging constraint (§9), post-tensioned or composite upper floors (§5), fuel rules that can force all fuel to the lowest floor, as New York City required after 9/11, and cooling designed vertically. Brightray/BUUU's Macao design targets about 40 MW in up to five storeys on about 9,000 m², prefabricated, Tier III, delivery Q3 2027 (verify: announced, not built). At 120 acres the reference campus has no reason to pay for any of it.

Floor loads and rack weights

What owners specify. ASCE 7-22 requires 100 psf distributed or a 2,000 lb concentrated load for "computer use, access floor systems"; UFC 3-301-01 requires 150 psf for server spaces. New data centers ask for at least 150 psf and 250 psf where full, narrow or tall cabinets are expected (TechTarget); Intel recommended 350 psf in 2012; AI halls are climbing to 250–400+ psf (research synthesis). The code minimum is a floor, not a target.

The governing rack. "The GB200 NVL72 rack weighs approximately 1,360 kg (3,000 lbs) before coolant is added. With coolant, the total weight can approach 1,500 kg. Most traditional raised-floor data centers are designed for 500 to 750 kg per rack position" (rax.ae GB200 guide, citing NVIDIA and Supermicro datasheets, 2024). Footprint 600 by 1,068 mm, about 0.64 m². Arithmetic: 1,360 ÷ 0.64 = 2,125 kg/m², at 0.2048 psf per kg/m² about 435 psf; wet, 1,500 ÷ 0.64 = 2,344 kg/m², about 480 psf; per caster 1,360 ÷ 4 = 340 kg (750 lb) on a few square inches. Other estimates bracket it: Introl's 1,500 kg on 0.8 m² gives 1,875 kg/m² against "standard raised floors rated for 1,000 kg/m²" (Introl); ModulEdge cites "over 2,000 kg/m²." All exceed the 1,200–1,500 kg/m² raised floors are typically rated for; purpose-built AI facilities use reinforced slab-on-grade rated for at least 1,500 kg per position, and a legacy raised-floor hall is "structurally the wrong building" (ModulEdge).

Dated vendor weights. OCP Open Rack V3: 1,400 kg (3,080 lb) maximum payload, 600 or 750 by 1,068 by 2,286 mm, 48 V busbar to 120 kW, seismic kit as an accessory (Rittal, nVent, Emcor OCP listings, 2023–25); ORv3.1 is the base specification as of March 2026 (verify: recent). GB200 NVL72 about 1,360 kg at 120 kW nominal, 130–132 kW observed; GB300 135–142 kW (NVIDIA, 2025). A standard rack is about 300 lb empty; an enterprise position was designed for 500–750 kg.

Reconciling Topic 2's "1,500 lb" (issue #3). APX-requirements §8 and §12 carried "fully populated AI racks near 1,500 lb" from WWT's AI readiness glossary, and the OPR locked "floor-loading headroom for 1,500 lb racks." 1,500 lb is 680 kg, a legacy position's weight; the GB200 NVL72 is about 1,360 kg (3,000 lb) dry and 1,500 kg wet, 2.2 times more. The chapter resolves the unit: the headroom is re-read as 1,500 kg and the AI pods and reserve strip are designed to 400 psf (§12). The Topic 2 pages should be corrected to "~1,500 kg (3,000+ lb)" with a pointer here.

Conversions. 1 kN/m² ≈ 20.9 psf ≈ 102 kg/m²; 1 psf ≈ 4.88 kg/m². 250 psf ≈ 12 kN/m²; 400 psf ≈ 19 kN/m²; 1,000 psf ≈ 48 kN/m².

Liquid weight, slab, soil and deflection

What liquid adds. A flooded CDU reaches about 3 tonnes and needs roughly 800 kg/m² locally (Introl); on about 1 m² the reference treats it as a 3 t point load on a reinforced pad. CDUs run from 70 kW to 2.3 MW (Vertiv CoolChip) and to 14 MW (Trane); in-row units cool 5–6 racks. Stainless piping, coolant (often 20–35% water-glycol), manifolds and leak trays make a liquid row materially heavier per metre than an air row. Liquid buys back white space, 70–85% less per MW, but "busway, stainless piping, leak detection, and slab reinforcement consume the saving" (Global Data Center Hub). The CDU room is Topic 7.

Slab. An office slab is 4–6 in with light rebar; a data hall typically needs 12–18 in with heavy rebar at 6–8 in centres both ways and supplemental reinforcement under equipment (Buildermuse). A hyperscale hall uses 8,000–15,000 cubic yards of structural concrete. The reference takes 14–16 in, thickened pads under CDUs and electrical gear, and a battery room near 1,000 psf.

Soil. Slab-on-grade capacity depends on subgrade stiffness, the k-value, more than on bearing strength; poor soils force piles or drilled shafts. The slab's rating is a property of the ground as much as the concrete, which makes Topic 3's geotechnical work a Topic 4 input.

Deflection and vibration. Upper floors are post-tensioned or composite, designed for flexure and vibration; raised-floor pedestals tolerate only 5–10 mm of differential settlement. Floors near their rating creep: a 2 mm sag over 12–24 months binds doors and misaligns trays. Vibration is linked to a 15–25% increase in drive failures (verify: a Google reliability study cited second-hand by Huiya). Do not run a floor at its rating.

Seismic

ASCE 7-22 Chapter 13 governs nonstructural anchorage: racks, pipe, busway, UPS and generators. Data centers are commonly Risk Category IV under the IBC, which gives a component importance factor Ip of 1.5, design forces about 50% above an ordinary building's, and higher wind design speeds (ASCE 7-22; IBC). Engineered anchorage is required from Seismic Design Category C, and the category follows the site's short-period acceleration SDS: Reno about 1.3 g, Dallas about 0.1 g (USGS design maps). Phoenix is described as similarly low, but the dump gives no SDS (verify: read it from the USGS/ASCE hazard tool before the structural basis of design). Base isolation cuts forces up to about 70% but is a California and Nevada tool; in Dallas or Phoenix conventional anchorage suffices. The rack maker supplies the seismic-rated cabinet, AC156 certification and an optional kit; the building supplies the anchorage design, cast-in embeds and PE-stamped calculations (research synthesis). Reference stance: RC IV, Ip 1.5, conventional anchorage, embeds cast to the ORv3 footprint in the AI pods.

Raised floor versus slab

The matrix. Raised access floor (RAF) suits legacy and retail colocation, underfloor air and a cable plenum; slab suits hyperscale, high density and AI. RAF supplies air from a plenum with power and cable under the floor; slab floods the room from fan walls with busway and tray overhead. RAF's density ceiling is moderate and it needs understructure bracing and costs more; slab carries 1,500 kg racks directly, anchors to concrete, costs less and saves height. RAF can carry underfloor liquid pipe but leak-exposed; slab takes pipe overhead or at slab level with trays (research synthesis). Slab with overhead distribution is "the standard in large hyperscale deployments" (Data Centre Magazine); raised floor persists in retail colocation and where tenants want underfloor flexibility.

Where RAF survives. Pedestals run 12–18 in for smaller sites and 24–36 in for high-density underfloor air. CISCA rates tiles for concentrated, uniform and rolling loads, standard uniform 250–600 psf and high-density panels to 2,500 psf, with the understructure usually the limit (CISCA). "Standard raised-floor tiles are rated for 250 kg per tile. A fully loaded GB200 NVL72 rack requires reinforced flooring or a dedicated slab with verified load capacity before installation" (Spheron GB200 guide) (verify: reads low against CISCA). Leak detection runs under the floor wherever liquid is present.

Overhead against underfloor. Overhead busway, tray and pipe give shorter air paths, free the floor for revenue equipment and are easier to maintain live, at the cost of leak and fire exposure above the racks and the clear height they need (§8). Topic 9 fixes the stack order: busway highest, fiber raceway and copper tray below, liquid manifolds lowest so a leak falls on nothing electrical.

Reference decision. Slab throughout, including the 8 kW retail zone. Raised floor there alone was rejected: two floor systems, two seismic details and two air paths in one hall, for the tenants least likely to need a plenum.

Space program and separations

Rooms. White space is the hall where racks live; gray space is the electrical, UPS, battery and mechanical rooms and MEP galleries; CDU rooms and pipe galleries carry liquid; telecom entrance and meet-me rooms are area only (Topic 9); NOC, security, dock and staging complete the list.

Ratios. White to gray has run about 1:1; liquid plus 2N pushes gray above 1:1. White space averages about 65% of total in older air-cooled designs and falls with density. "Treat any published ratio as a planning figure, not a design rule" (Anvilfield).

Hall 1 arithmetic. The dump gives white 30,000–45,000 sf for 10 MW at the mixed density, gray about 1:1 at Tier III with liquid, a CDU gallery with at least 50% growth room, dock and support at 10–15% of gross, gross 2.0–2.5 times white. The course takes white 32,000, gray 32,000, CDU gallery 8,000, dock and support 8,000: gross 80,000 sf. Reconciliation with APX-requirements §12, which sized white space at 17,000–18,000 ft² for all 1,000 racks from ft²-per-rack rules: that is net rack-plus-aisle area for the campus; this chapter's 32,000 sf is gross white space for Hall 1's roughly 250 racks including containment, CDU rows, network racks, circulation and reserve, about 128 ft² per rack gross against 15–20 net (verify: plausible for a liquid-cooled hall but not sourced; APX-requirements §12 should carry one line saying the two measure different things).

NFPA 855 as a building matter. The binding edition follows the International Fire Code the AHJ adopted: IFC 2021 references NFPA 855-2020, IFC 2024 references NFPA 855-2023, and a 2026 edition was released in September 2025 (NFPA; ICC). The constraints that size the room: lithium-ion limited to 600 kWh per fire enclosure, exceptions through UL 9540A; 3 ft between cabinets and to walls; one- or two-hour barriers; not below grade; ventilation continuous at 1 CFM/sf or intermittent at 25% of the lower flammable limit; deflagration venting where UL 9540A shows a hazard; two hours of standby power for safety systems; sprinklers commonly 0.3 gpm/ft² over 2,500 ft²; a Hazard Mitigation Analysis first (NFPA 855-2023). A 10 MW Tier III hall therefore has several 600 kWh enclosures, each with its own barriers and venting. The batteries are Topic 5.

Other separations. NFPA 75 and 76 require rated separation of the computer room from other occupancies; the IBC sets occupancy and egress travel distances; generator and fuel setbacks are local (§3).

Heights. Hyperscale AI halls specify 5–6 m floor to ceiling because containment, direct-to-chip lines and dense trays consume overhead space; colocation runs 3.8–4.5 m, "a structural barrier for any tenant arriving with liquid-cooled AI equipment"; 6–9 m floor to floor is now common (research synthesis). The reference takes 6 m clear and about 8–9 m to the deck.

Docks, rigging and heavy haul

Docks. Dock doors 9 ft wide by 10 ft high for 102 in trailers, 10 by 10 ft flex bays for oversized loads, on 12 ft centres minimum and 14 ft recommended. Grade doors, 7 by 7 up to 12 by 14 ft, let forklifts and rolling heavy gear come straight in. Hydraulic levelers are sized for dynamic load, 40,000–45,000 lb or more given 3,000 lb racks plus rigging. A staging and unboxing room outside the white space is the canonical contamination rule (Snevely, Enterprise Data Center Design), about 510 sf per 60 ft trailer position; truck court 120 ft or more (research synthesis).

The rigging path. "The route often governs the slab more than the final rack position": corridor widths, door sizes, turning radii and slab and wheel loads along the path must all carry the heaviest single load; in multi-storey buildings the freight elevator is the limit. Because the equipment list is rarely final at design, the path is designed to a governing envelope, the heaviest transformer and longest genset, with embeds and knock-out panels left for later gear (research synthesis).

Highway to pad. A 100–200 t transformer or 3 MW generator moves on multi-axle lowboys, dual-lane trailers or self-propelled modular transporters (SPMTs); medium-power transformers to about 90,000 lb move on multi-axle trailers. Lift planning checks ground bearing, crane mats, temporary roads and load paths first. All-terrain cranes of about 90 t pick chillers and gensets; 190 and 275 t cranes set the largest transformers. When "the asset on the hook took years to build and cannot be quickly replaced," one engineered set is the goal. Transformers and switchgear are often set before the shell closes, or through knock-out panels, which couples the rigging path to dry-in (rigging-contractor practice). The reference's substation-class transformers stay in Topic 3's yard; the hall's envelope is the largest unit-substation transformer or a 3 MW genset.

Fit-out throughput. "The old days of deploying a rack at a time are long gone" (Data Center Frontier). A Meta and AnyLogic simulation of rack receiving ran at 40% of target with a 3.7-day cycle per rack at baseline and 92% at 2.2 days once optimised; the dock, staging, hall and lift chain is the bottleneck (Meta/AnyLogic). Busway tap-offs cut deployment "from days to hours" against conduit pulls, hence the 120 kW busway pre-provisioned in the AI pods.

The insurer's role. FM Global's data sheets frequently "become deal terms"; the insurer often shapes the design more than code. FM 1-28 wind pressures run about 15% above ASCE 7, and FM prefers structural concrete roof decks because loss experience with steel decks is worse in wind and tornado (FM Global Data Sheet 1-28). Dallas sits in a severe hail and tornado corridor (the 1995 Mayfest hailstorm), so hail and wind-borne debris drive envelope and rooftop protection there. The reference assumes FM insurance and takes the concrete deck and a hail-rated membrane in Phoenix too, both small premiums (verify: confirm the FM-insured assumption with the owner's risk manager).

Roof and envelope. Chillers, dry coolers and air handlers impose concentrated roof loads that need dunnage, steel support frames, and screens; PV adds dead load (plant sizing is Topic 7). ASCE 7 Chapter 13 and FM 1-28 govern anchorage of roof-mounted equipment against uplift. A high-albedo single-ply membrane, typically TPO, reduces heat gain and must meet FM uplift and hail ratings. A building cooled around the clock in Phoenix needs a continuous air and vapour barrier under ASHRAE 90.4, 90.1 and the IECC, and dust filtration.

Noise

Sources and levels. Cooling fans, transformers and switchgear typically produce 60–80 dBA at the property line unmitigated; generator load tests 80–100 dBA, usually exempt as emergency testing. Typical ordinances allow 55–65 dBA by day (7 am to 10 pm) and 45–55 at night, measured with Leq, Lmax, L10 and L90; low-frequency content below 100 Hz is analysed separately because it travels farther (research synthesis).

Disputes, 2023–26. Prince William County, Virginia supervisors voted 7 to 1 in 2023 to remove the night-time HVAC exemption from county code 14-4 (residential limits 60 dBA day, 55 night); the revised ordinance adds steady-tonal enforcement, "a whine, hum, rumble, or buzz," with first violations as Class 4 misdemeanours from 1 May 2026 (Prince William County) (verify: recent). Chandler, Arizona adopted Ordinance 5033 (PLH22-0053) unanimously on 5 December 2022 after a decade of Brittany Heights complaints, and on 11 December 2025 its council unanimously rejected a rezoning for a 43,000 sf data center despite lobbying by former Senator Kyrsten Sinema (City of Chandler). Nuisance class actions reached facilities in 2026 in Vineland NJ, Dowagiac MI, North Tonawanda NY and Hood County TX (verify: 2026 filings). Nearly one-third of Virginia's data centers sit within 200 ft of residentially zoned land. Chandler is a Phoenix suburb; it is the local precedent for the hearing Topic 3 warned about.

Mitigation and its penalty. Acoustic enclosures, barrier walls, low-noise fans and setbacks each add cost and footprint; generators enclosed to about 70 dBA at 23 ft appear in draft ordinances (Albemarle County). Setbacks eat developable land; enclosures add capex. Reference stance: 55 dBA by day and 45 at night at the property line, generator tests in daylight only, a berm and enclosures on the south edge where Topic 3 placed the homes. Prince William's 60/55 is the fallback if the acoustic model shows 45 cannot be met.

Reference Hall 1 worked example

All numbers indicative. The facility: 1,000 racks, about 40 MW IT, Phoenix-leaning site, Tier III, design-build with OFE, 300 racks at 8 kW, 300 at 15, 200 at 40, 200 at 120; Hall 1 about 10 MW at T+42 (Topics 1 to 3).

Arithmetic for the AI pod: 1,360 ÷ 0.64 = 2,125 kg/m² ≈ 435 psf; wet, 1,500 ÷ 0.64 = 2,344 kg/m² ≈ 480 psf; per caster 1,360 ÷ 4 = 340 kg (750 lb). Add a filled CDU of about 3 t on about 1 m² per five or six racks, plus manifolds, pipe and trays, and the pod zone as a whole is designed to a blended 400 psf, about 19 kN/m², with reinforced pads under each CDU; the blend is below the footprint figure because the aisles carry nothing. The reserve strip takes the same 400 psf because an AI pod could land anywhere in it (Topic 2). The rest of the white space is 250 psf; battery rooms near 1,000 psf; electrical rooms about 250 psf plus pads.

Row weights. Ten 15 kW air-cooled racks weigh about 7–9 t. Ten GB200 racks weigh 13.6–15 t, plus two CDUs at about 3 t each, plus filled manifolds and pipe: roughly 20–22 t, two and a half to three times the air row (indicative, from §4 and §5).

Program. White space about 32,000 sf: four pod zones, the network racks Topic 9 needs in the AI pods (about one air-cooled 20–30 kW network rack per two GPU racks, because copper reach is short), and the reserve strip; gray about 32,000 sf; CDU room and pipe gallery about 8,000 sf with at least 50% growth room; dock, staging, NOC, security and support about 8,000 sf; gross about 80,000 sf (§8 for the reconciliation with Topic 2). Halls 2 to 4 repeat the plan.

Docks and rigging. Two 9 by 10 ft dock doors on 14 ft centres and one 12 by 14 ft grade door per hall, 45,000 lb levelers, a 120 ft truck court, staging outside the white space, and a rigging path designed to the largest per-hall unit-substation transformer or a 3 MW genset (§9).

Timeline on Topic 3's clock. Groundbreaking T+18; site work and foundations T+18–22, slab, pads and embeds cast to the envelope; shell and dry-in T+21–26, knock-outs left open; MEP rough-in T+24–31; equipment set T+30–34 through the knock-outs; fit-out and early access T+32–38; substation transformer and energization T+36–38 (Topic 3); commissioning T+38–42. Ready-for-service at T+42 is about 24 months from groundbreaking, inside the dump's 18–30; vertical construction about 16 months, inside its 12–24.

What is changing fast. GB300 and Rubin-class racks head toward 2,000 kg; NVIDIA's Kyber at about 600 kW per rack is slated for late 2027 and a 1 MW rack was announced at OCP 2025, so the 400 psf pods are a 2026 number. Multi-storey AI halls, the prefab share and 24-hour noise limits are all moving; duration data for 10–40 MW phases stays thin (verify: all 2026-dated).
