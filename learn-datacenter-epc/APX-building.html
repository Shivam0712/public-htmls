Cost and duration evidence

The structure-and-envelope share. The shell's structure and envelope account for about 10–15% of total construction cost (Terrapin/TCG, 2026). Topic 1's teardown puts core, shell and architectural work at 14% of an air-cooled US build and 9% of a liquid-cooled one (Turner & Townsend, Data Centre Construction Cost Index 2025–26); liquid moves spend toward mechanical work while MEP remains about 75–80%. These percentages do not price a powered shell. For this course, the $180–230 million first phase, excluding customer IT, includes shared site work and shell work for all four halls, not just Hall 1. The $400–500 million completed-campus construction scope also excludes customer IT. Both dollar ranges are course planning budgets, not prices for structure and envelope alone.

Why JLL's number differs. JLL puts global shell-and-core at $11.3M/MW in 2026, up from $10.7M in 2025 and $7.7M in 2020, with AI fit-out costing "as much as $25 million per MW on top" (JLL, Global Data Center Outlook 2026). Its scope includes "the physical structure plus base power and cooling distribution," so it cannot be applied to the 10–15% structure-and-envelope share (verify: not comparable; the chapter uses the T&T share). T&T's index shows air-cooled cost per watt up 5.5% in 2025 after 9.0% in 2024, and a "7–10% cost premium based on like-for-like analysis of traditional air-cooled and high-density, liquid-cooled data centers in the US" (Paul Barry, Turner & Townsend). Tokyo ($15.2/W), Singapore ($14.5) and Zurich ($14.2) lead; AI-optimised facilities reach $20M/MW against $10–12M/MW standard, chiefly because of MEP density rather than the frame.

What moves the shell's $/sf. Seismic zone (8–15% on structure and mechanical in a high-seismic region), poor soil forcing deep foundations, storey count and span, hardening (§10), and labour at $140–200+ an hour in union markets against $65–95 in right-to-work states affect the price (research synthesis). None is determined by rack count alone.

Duration and overlaps. Indicative activities for a 10–40 MW building are earthwork at 4–12 weeks; civil, foundations and shell together at 12–36 weeks; shell erection at 2–6 weeks (15 days on one Turner project); MEP rough-in at 12–24 weeks, "the densest, riskiest stretch of the build"; equipment set at 4–12 weeks; and commissioning L1–L5 commonly at three to six months. Dry-in is an enclosure milestone, not a requirement that all protected interior installation wait. MEP can start during shell work, equipment can be set before closure or through removable openings, and fit-out begins in the "early access period toward the end of construction" (CBRE). These durations overlap and must not be summed. Vertical construction is indicatively 12–24 months and ready-for-service 18–30 months from construction start. Integrated systems test failures delay projects 3.8 months on average (research synthesis). Week-level data for 10–40 MW phases is thin, mostly general-contractor blogs and one 2011 Turner schedule (verify: planning-grade).

Shell systems in detail

Four systems dominate; the choice is regional and owner-driven more than technical (research synthesis, 2025–26).

Concrete tilt-up. Panels are cast flat on the slab and craned upright. This is the course's choice for a flat, single-storey campus: fast for a large box, lowest $/sf on large flat sites, commonly using 8–12 m grids, with high thermal and wind-resistant mass. It suits Sunbelt hyperscale and colocation builds; its suitability still depends on the site and engineered enclosure.

Precast concrete. Factory-cast panels and structure (Clark Pacific AccelCore, High Concrete) can bring earlier enclosure at a moderate premium over tilt-up. The reported comparison of about 48 hours' plant cure against up to 28 days cast-in-place describes particular processes, not a guaranteed cure or delivery time for every supplier. Typical grids are 8–12 m. Concrete decks may appeal to an insurer in wind or tornado exposure, subject to the specific roof and site review. GBC Engineers call precast "the most favorable solution for modern data center construction." In one hyperscale case, over 600 exterior panels were prefabricated in two months, first turnovers came at 11 months and all halls turned over under 18 months (iRecruit case).

Structural steel with insulated metal panel. An off-site frame can be erected in 2–6 weeks and span 12 m or more. It offers flexibility for multi-storey colocation in land-constrained metros, with lower thermal mass and exposure to steel pricing. Steel roof decks may need stronger site-specific wind and tornado detailing under FM 1-28; they are not categorically prohibited.

Hybrid, modular and prefabricated. Precast shells can pair with power and cooling skids or eHouses; repeated halls can also be factory-built. Factory and site work can run in parallel, improving schedule certainty when dimensions and connections are frozen early. A per-unit premium and reduced field adaptability are the trade-offs. The course uses selected factory-built plant rooms, not a factory-built Hall 1 shell.

The 2026 extremes (verify: recent). Meta's Prometheus campus in New Albany, Ohio used fabric tents of about 125,000 sf each, eight standing by April 2026, to accelerate enclosure; this "trades away some of the durability and long-term flexibility of a permanent concrete or steel building" (SemiAnalysis, April 2026). AWS is moving to narrower structures "organized around the systems installed inside," with fewer field interfaces. Modularity can simplify the design as well as move work off-site.

Reference choice. One-storey tilt-up blocks serve the roughly 10 MW halls on the course's flat site, with a planning grid of 12 m, column-free centre bays for AI pods and a concrete roof deck. The grid and deck need layout, load and insurer checks (§3; §10). Precast remains an option if its actual enclosure schedule justifies the premium; steel suits longer spans or a constrained multi-storey site.

Forms, grids and multi-storey

Grids. Standard column spacings are 8.4, 9.6, 10.8 or 12 m both ways, laid out against nominal 1.2 m rack depth, 1.2 m cold aisle and 1.5–1.8 m hot aisle (research synthesis). A row and its two aisles occupy roughly 3.9–4.2 m, suggesting three such modules in a 12 m bay or two in an 8.4 m bay before allowances for columns, clearance and services. Hyperscalers push for 20–30 m column-free spans; colocation and multi-storey schemes use 9–15 m. The reference's 12 m grid and column-free centre bays are layout assumptions, not proof of fit. Check actual rack rows, containment, CDUs, manifolds, circulation, delivery turns and heavy-equipment openings before freezing columns or panel openings.

Multi-storey land use. Building upward can save scarce land. QTS opened Northern Virginia's first three-storey data center, ultimately four 2.5 MW halls per floor, with a "massive freight lift" and floors filled quadrant by quadrant because cooling relies on convection across the storeys (QTS). Brightray/BUUU's Macao design targets about 40 MW in up to five storeys on about 9,000 m², prefabricated and Tier III, with delivery targeted for Q3 2027 (verify: announced, not built).

Multi-storey penalties. Three to five storeys replace the ground-supported slab for upper halls with post-tensioned or composite floors requiring load, flexure and vibration checks (§5). Freight-elevator dimensions and capacity constrain rigging (§9); vertical power, cooling and piping routes need space and maintenance access. Fuel rules can constrain floors, as New York City required fuel on the lowest floor after 9/11. The reference campus is about 120 acres, a course fact from Topic 3. Its available land supports the course's one-storey choice without paying these upper-floor, lift and vertical-service penalties.

Floor loads and rack weights

Separate the checks. ASCE 7-22 specifies 100 psf distributed or a 2,000 lb concentrated load for "computer use, access floor systems"; UFC 3-301-01 specifies 150 psf for server spaces. These are distinct code load categories and checks, not an AI rack design specification. New data centers ask for at least 150 psf and 250 psf where full, narrow or tall cabinets are expected (TechTarget); Intel recommended 350 psf in 2012; AI halls are climbing toward 250–400+ psf (research synthesis). The owner's distributed-area allowance, rack footprint average, each foot's concentrated load and rolling loads on the delivery route must each be checked independently. A code minimum is not the owner's target.

The governing rack. "The GB200 NVL72 rack weighs approximately 1,360 kg (3,000 lbs) before coolant is added. With coolant, the total weight can approach 1,500 kg. Most traditional raised-floor data centers are designed for 500 to 750 kg per rack position" (rax.ae GB200 guide, citing NVIDIA and Supermicro datasheets, 2024). Its 600 by 1,068 mm footprint is about 0.64 m². Dry: 1,360 ÷ 0.64 = 2,125 kg/m², about 435 psf averaged across the footprint. Filled: 1,500 ÷ 0.64 ≈ 2,344 kg/m², about 480 psf. With four equally loaded feet, the static loads are 340 kg (750 lb) dry and 375 kg (about 827 lb) filled per foot, on much smaller contact areas; movement and uneven loading require further checks. Introl's different 1,500 kg on 0.8 m² yields 1,875 kg/m² against "standard raised floors rated for 1,000 kg/m²" (Introl); ModulEdge cites "over 2,000 kg/m²." These footprint figures cannot by themselves establish whether a particular raised-floor assembly passes its tile, pedestal or rolling ratings. Typical 1,200–1,500 kg/m² raised-floor figures warrant verification against the specified assembly; purpose-built AI halls commonly favor reinforced slab-on-grade with at least 1,500 kg per position and local equipment checks. A legacy raised-floor hall can be "structurally the wrong building" for this rack (ModulEdge), but an engineered raised floor is not automatically excluded.

Dated vendor weights. OCP Open Rack V3: 1,400 kg (3,080 lb) maximum payload, 600 or 750 by 1,068 by 2,286 mm, 48 V busbar to 120 kW, seismic kit as an accessory (Rittal, nVent, Emcor OCP listings, 2023–25); ORv3.1 is the base specification as of March 2026 (verify: recent). GB200 NVL72 about 1,360 kg at 120 kW nominal, 130–132 kW observed; GB300 135–142 kW (NVIDIA, 2025). A standard rack is about 300 lb empty; an enterprise position was designed for 500–750 kg. Verify vendor configuration, payload versus filled installed weight, and foot geometry before structural sign-off.

Reconciling Topic 2's "1,500 lb" (issue #3). APX-requirements §8 and §12 carried "fully populated AI racks near 1,500 lb" from WWT's AI readiness glossary, and the OPR locked "floor-loading headroom for 1,500 lb racks." 1,500 lb is 680 kg, a legacy position's weight; the GB200 NVL72 is about 1,360 kg (3,000 lb) dry and 1,500 kg wet, 2.2 times more. The chapter resolves the unit: the headroom is re-read as 1,500 kg and the AI pods and reserve strip are designed to 400 psf (§12). The Topic 2 pages should be corrected to "~1,500 kg (3,000+ lb)" with a pointer here.

Conversions. 1 kN/m² ≈ 20.9 psf ≈ 102 kg/m²; 1 psf ≈ 4.88 kg/m². 250 psf ≈ 12 kN/m²; 400 psf ≈ 19 kN/m²; 1,000 psf ≈ 48 kN/m².

Liquid weight, slab, soil and deflection

What liquid adds. A roughly 3-tonne filled CDU is a worked equipment case, not a standard weight for every coolant unit. Introl reports a flooded unit at about 3 tonnes and a local requirement of roughly 800 kg/m²; the reference separately tests a roughly 3 t unit on an approximately 1 m² reinforced pad. These are different equipment and area assumptions, so confirm the chosen unit's filled weight, bearing points and pad design. CDUs range from 70 kW to 2.3 MW (Vertiv CoolChip) and up to 14 MW (Trane); an in-row unit can cool 5–6 racks. Stainless piping, filled pipe racks, coolant (often 20–35% water-glycol), manifolds and leak trays make a liquid row materially heavier per metre than an air row. Liquid can buy back white space, reported as 70–85% less per MW, but "busway, stainless piping, leak detection, and slab reinforcement consume the saving" (Global Data Center Hub). The CDU room is Topic 7.

Slab and local details. An office slab is commonly 4–6 in with light rebar; a data hall is reported at 12–18 in with heavy rebar at 6–8 in centres both ways and supplemental reinforcement under equipment (Buildermuse). A hyperscale hall uses 8,000–15,000 cubic yards of structural concrete. The reference plans 14–16 in, thickened local pads under CDUs and electrical gear, and a battery room allowance near 1,000 psf. These dimensions do not certify capacity: thickness, reinforcement, joint location and detailing, pad bearing and anchors, settlement, soil stiffness, filled piping and moving loads all affect the engineered result.

Soil. Slab-on-grade behavior depends on subgrade stiffness, the k-value, as well as soil bearing and settlement; poor soils can require piles or drilled shafts. Topic 3's geotechnical evidence therefore feeds the slab and heavy-route design, not just the building foundations.

Deflection and vibration. Upper floors are post-tensioned or composite and must be checked for flexure and vibration. Raised-floor pedestals tolerate only about 5–10 mm of differential settlement. A reported 2 mm sag over 12–24 months can bind doors and misalign trays. Vibration is linked to a 15–25% increase in drive failures (verify: a Google reliability study cited second-hand by Huiya). Avoid treating a nominal load rating as spare capacity.

Seismic

ASCE 7-22 Chapter 13 addresses nonstructural anchorage for racks, pipes, busway, UPS equipment and generators. The adopted code, actual occupancy and use determine the building's Risk Category; site hazard and Risk Category determine its Seismic Design Category (SDC). Data centers are not automatically Risk Category IV because they are data centers or Tier III-class. The applicable component importance factor, Ip, depends on the component and code criteria, rather than being uniformly 1.5. Risk Category IV and Ip 1.5 can increase applicable design demands, but neither should be presumed for every item (ASCE 7-22; IBC).

Engineered anchorage needs assessment against the actual SDC and component requirements; SDC C and above is an important threshold in the report. Illustrative site short-period accelerations, SDS, are Reno about 1.3 g and Dallas about 0.1 g (USGS design maps). Phoenix is described as similarly low, but the dump gives no SDS (verify: read it from the USGS/ASCE hazard tool before the structural basis of design). Base isolation can cut forces by up to about 70% in some high-seismic applications; conventional bracing and anchorage are the reference planning choice for a Phoenix- or Dallas-leaning site, subject to its actual hazard and code classification. The rack maker supplies the seismic-rated cabinet, AC156 certification where specified and an optional kit; the building team designs anchorage, cast-in embeds and PE-stamped calculations (research synthesis). Reference stance: reserve embeds and check the ORv3 footprint in the AI pods, but establish Risk Category, SDC and each component's Ip from the actual site, use and adopted code before fixing the design.

Raised floor versus slab

The matrix. Raised access floor (RAF) suits legacy and retail colocation, underfloor air and a cable plenum; a ground-supported slab suits hyperscale, high-density and AI uses. RAF can supply air through the plenum with power and cable below; the reference slab supplies the room from fan walls with overhead busway and tray. RAF adds floor, ramps and understructure bracing; a slab simplifies anchorage and saves height. Underfloor liquid pipe is possible but needs leak detection and drainage; slab-based rooms can route pipe overhead or at slab level (research synthesis). Slab with overhead distribution is "the standard in large hyperscale deployments" (Data Centre Magazine); RAF persists where retail tenants value underfloor flexibility. Neither system's nominal distributed rating alone certifies a filled rack.

Where RAF survives. Pedestals commonly run 12–18 in for smaller sites and 24–36 in for high-density underfloor air. CISCA distinguishes tile concentrated, uniform and rolling ratings; standard uniform values are 250–600 psf and high-density panels can reach 2,500 psf, while pedestals and stringers can govern the assembled floor (CISCA). Assess the rack foot against the tile's concentrated rating, the understructure against its loads, and loaded wheels against rolling ratings and joints. "Standard raised-floor tiles are rated for 250 kg per tile. A fully loaded GB200 NVL72 rack requires reinforced flooring or a dedicated slab with verified load capacity before installation" (Spheron GB200 guide) (verify: reads low against CISCA). This warning about a standard tile is not a claim that no purpose-engineered RAF can support the rack. Leak detection runs under the floor wherever liquid is present.

Overhead against underfloor. Overhead busway, tray and pipe give shorter air paths, free floor area for equipment and are easier to maintain live, but require clear height and leak and fire controls above racks (§8). Topic 9 fixes the reference stack order: busway highest, fiber raceway and copper tray below, liquid manifolds lowest so a leak falls on nothing electrical.

Reference decision. Use slab with overhead distribution throughout Hall 1, including its 8 kW retail zone. Raised floor there alone was rejected because it would create two floor systems, two seismic details and two air paths in one hall. The slab still needs separate area, foot, joint, pad, soil and rolling-route checks (§4; §5).

Space program and separations

Rooms. White space contains racks, circulation and its reserved rack area. Gray space contains electrical, UPS, battery and mechanical rooms and MEP galleries; the separate CDU room and pipe gallery also count as gray space. Telecom entrance and meet-me rooms are allocated space here but designed in Topic 9. NOC, security, dock and staging complete the support program.

Ratios. White to gray has historically been about 1:1; liquid distribution and 2N support can push gray above white. Older air-cooled designs can average about 65% white space overall, but that figure does not describe this liquid-cooled hall. "Treat any published ratio as a planning figure, not a design rule" (Anvilfield).

Hall 1 arithmetic. The report gives indicative white space of 30,000–45,000 sf for a mixed-density 10 MW hall, gray near 1:1 before extra liquid space, a CDU gallery with at least 50% growth room, and dock and support at roughly 10–15% of gross. The course's 80,000 sf example allocates 32,000 sf to white space, including all white-space reserve; 32,000 sf to electrical and mechanical rooms, including UPS and batteries; 8,000 sf to additional liquid gray space, the CDU room and pipe gallery; and 8,000 sf to dock, staging, NOC, security and other support. Thus gray totals 40,000 sf and white is 40% of gross. Reconciliation with APX-requirements §12, which sized white space at 17,000–18,000 ft² for all 1,000 racks from ft²-per-rack rules: that is net rack-plus-aisle area for the campus; this chapter's 32,000 sf is gross white space for Hall 1's roughly 250 racks including containment, CDU rows, network racks, circulation and reserve, about 128 ft² per rack gross against 15–20 net (verify: plausible for a liquid-cooled hall but not sourced; APX-requirements §12 should carry one line saying the two measure different things).

NFPA 855 as a building matter. The binding edition depends on the locally adopted fire code and authority having jurisdiction (AHJ): the report associates IFC 2021 with NFPA 855-2020 and IFC 2024 with NFPA 855-2023; a 2026 edition was released in September 2025 (NFPA; ICC). The report lists, for particular lithium-ion configurations, a 600 kWh per-fire-enclosure limit with potential UL 9540A-based exceptions; 3 ft spacing between cabinets and to walls; one- or two-hour barriers; below-grade restrictions; continuous ventilation at 1 CFM/sf or intermittent control at 25% of the lower flammable limit; deflagration venting where testing identifies a hazard; two hours of standby power for safety systems; and sprinklers commonly at 0.3 gpm/ft² over 2,500 ft² (NFPA 855-2023). These are reported review items, not universal requirements for every battery or room. Start with a Hazard Mitigation Analysis, then confirm applicable limits, spacing, ventilation, separation and exceptions against battery chemistry and configuration, test evidence, adopted edition and AHJ. Do not assume a 10 MW hall necessarily needs several identical 600 kWh enclosures. Battery-system design is Topic 5.

Other separations. Review NFPA 75 and 76 for applicable computer-room fire protection and separation; the adopted IBC governs occupancy and egress, while generator and fuel setbacks are local (§3). Ratings and distances require the actual room use and fire-authority review.

Heights. Hyperscale AI halls specify about 5–6 m clear because containment, direct-to-chip lines and dense trays use overhead space; colocation runs 3.8–4.5 m, potentially limiting tenants bringing liquid-cooled AI equipment. Floor-to-floor heights of 6–9 m are reported in new AI builds (research synthesis). The reference plans 6 m clear and about 8–9 m to the deck, subject to the final service section.

Docks, rigging and heavy haul

Ordinary rack docks. Dock doors are commonly 9 ft wide by 10 ft high for 102 in trailers, with 10 by 10 ft flex bays for oversized loads, on 12 ft centres minimum and 14 ft recommended. Grade doors range from 7 by 7 to 12 by 14 ft. Hydraulic levelers need dynamic-load ratings, around 40,000–45,000 lb or more for racks plus handling equipment, but that rating does not qualify them for a transformer move. Keep staging and unboxing outside white space for contamination control (Snevely, Enterprise Data Center Design); the report offers about 510 sf per 60 ft trailer position as a warehouse proxy and a 120 ft or deeper truck court (research synthesis). The course uses two 9 by 10 ft dock-high doors and one 12 by 14 ft grade door per hall for ordinary receiving.

Separate heavy route. The route can govern the slab more than the installed rack position. Check corridor width and height, door clearances, turns, moving roller or wheel loads, slab joints, local ground bearing and final pad anchors; for multi-storey buildings, also check the freight lift. Agree an early governing size and weight envelope for the largest in-hall unit-substation transformer or a 3 MW genset, then check actual confirmed supplier dimensions, weights and connection details before fixing openings, embeds and anchors (research synthesis). The course's in-hall transformer enters through a removable wall panel and bypasses the ordinary rack dock. The much larger utility-scale or substation-class transformer remains in Topic 3's outdoor yard, with its own highway, lift and pad route.

Highway to pad. A 100–200 t utility-scale transformer or 3 MW generator can require multi-axle lowboys, dual-lane trailers or self-propelled modular transporters (SPMTs); medium-power transformers up to about 90,000 lb move on multi-axle trailers. Lift planning checks ground bearing, crane mats, temporary roads, turning geometry and load paths before the move. All-terrain cranes around 90 t can pick chillers and gensets; 190 and 275 t cranes can set the largest transformers. When "the asset on the hook took years to build and cannot be quickly replaced," one engineered set is the goal. Transformers and switchgear are often set before shell closure or through removable panels, linking the route to dry-in (rigging-contractor practice). Neither an outdoor-yard lift nor an in-hall roller move is certified by the rack dock specification.

Fit-out throughput. "The old days of deploying a rack at a time are long gone" (Data Center Frontier). A Meta and AnyLogic rack-receiving simulation ran at 40% of target with a 3.7-day cycle per rack at baseline and 92% at 2.2 days when optimised; dock, staging, hall and lift form a throughput chain (Meta/AnyLogic). Busway tap-offs can cut deployment "from days to hours" against conduit pulls, supporting the course's pre-provisioned 120 kW AI-pod busway.

Flood target and insurer role. The 500-year elevation plus freeboard is an owner planning target, not a known floor elevation or universal code minimum. Confirm it with the actual flood map, local survey, site evidence and adopted requirements before grading. FM Global data sheets frequently "become deal terms" and may be more demanding than code. The report cites about 15% higher FM 1-28 wind uplift than ASCE 7 as an illustrative comparison, not a multiplier to apply to every site or roof assembly. Concrete roof decks can be favored for wind or tornado loss control, but there is no general FM ban on steel decks: compare the actual deck, attachment, membrane, wind exposure and insurer criteria (FM Global Data Sheet 1-28). Dallas's severe hail and tornado exposure, including the 1995 Mayfest hailstorm, warrants envelope and rooftop-equipment review. The course plans a concrete deck and hail-rated membrane for the Phoenix-leaning reference (verify: confirm the FM-insured assumption with the owner's risk manager).

Roof and envelope. Chillers, dry coolers and air handlers impose concentrated loads requiring engineered dunnage, support frames and access for maintenance; PV adds dead load (plant sizing is Topic 7). Check equipment and screen weights, support load paths, anchorage, uplift and safe access together under the applicable structural and insurer review, including ASCE 7 Chapter 13 and FM 1-28 where relevant. A high-albedo single-ply membrane, often TPO, reduces heat gain and must meet the chosen assembly's uplift and hail criteria. A building cooled around the clock in Phoenix needs a continuous air and vapour barrier and dust filtration; review ASHRAE 90.4, 90.1 and the IECC for applicable envelope provisions.

Noise

Sources, measurements and targets. The report gives illustrative unmitigated 60–80 dBA for cooling fans, transformers and switchgear at a property line and 80–100 dBA for generator load tests. Neither range is a guaranteed reading: distance, operating state, simultaneous sources, barriers and measurement point change the result (research synthesis). Reported daytime ordinance ranges are about 55–65 dBA and night ranges about 45–55 dBA, using measures such as Leq, Lmax, L10 and L90; low-frequency content below 100 Hz merits separate review. The reference's 55 dBA day and 45 dBA night at the property boundary are course design targets, not a stated local ordinance. Confirm the jurisdiction's measurement location, periods, tonal rules and any generator-test exemption rather than presuming emergency-equipment tests are exempt.

Disputes, 2023–26. Prince William County, Virginia supervisors voted 7 to 1 in 2023 to remove the night-time HVAC exemption from county code 14-4 (residential limits 60 dBA day, 55 night); the revised ordinance adds steady-tonal enforcement, "a whine, hum, rumble, or buzz," with first violations as Class 4 misdemeanours from 1 May 2026 (Prince William County) (verify: recent). Chandler, Arizona adopted Ordinance 5033 (PLH22-0053) unanimously on 5 December 2022 after a decade of Brittany Heights complaints, and on 11 December 2025 its council unanimously rejected a rezoning for a 43,000 sf data center despite lobbying by former Senator Kyrsten Sinema (City of Chandler). Nuisance class actions reached facilities in 2026 in Vineland NJ, Dowagiac MI, North Tonawanda NY and Hood County TX (verify: 2026 filings). Nearly one-third of Virginia's data centers sit within 200 ft of residentially zoned land. Chandler is a Phoenix suburb and a local precedent for the hearing Topic 3 warned about.

Mitigation and its penalty. Acoustic enclosures, barrier walls, low-noise fans and setbacks add cost, footprint and maintenance-access constraints; generators enclosed to about 70 dBA at 23 ft appear in draft ordinances (Albemarle County). The reference models all operating equipment against its 55/45 targets, schedules generator tests in daylight, and plans a berm and enclosures on the south edge where Topic 3 placed the homes. Prince William's 60/55 figures are an example of a different locality, not an automatic fallback ordinance for this site. Review local tonal-noise provisions, actual generator-testing exemptions and the acoustic model before changing the course target.

Row weights. Ten 15 kW air-cooled racks weigh about 7–9 t. Ten worked GB200 racks weigh 13.6–15 t; add two approximately 3 t filled CDUs and filled manifolds and pipe for roughly 20–22 t, about two and a half to three times the air row (indicative, from §4 and §5).

Program. Of the 80,000 sf Hall 1 block, 32,000 sf is white space, including four pod zones, network racks and the reserve strip. Topic 9's AI-pod planning ratio is about one air-cooled 20–30 kW network rack per two GPU racks because copper reach is short. Electrical and mechanical rooms take 32,000 sf; the additional liquid gray space, a CDU room and pipe gallery with at least 50% growth room, takes 8,000 sf; dock, staging, NOC, security and other support take 8,000 sf. Gray therefore totals 40,000 sf, and the white-space reserve is not added again (§8 for the reconciliation with Topic 2). Halls 2 to 4 repeat the planning approach.

Separate delivery routes. Ordinary racks use two 9 by 10 ft dock doors on 14 ft centres, a 12 by 14 ft grade door, 45,000 lb levelers and staging outside white space; the truck court is about 120 ft deep. The governing in-hall unit-substation transformer or a 3 MW genset has a separately engineered route through a removable wall panel, with ground bearing, lift mats, turns, moving roller loads, slab joints, final pad and anchors checked against confirmed supplier dimensions. Topic 3's utility-scale substation transformer remains in the outdoor yard, not on the in-hall or rack-dock route (§9).

First-phase scope and timeline. The first phase is budgeted at $180–230 million excluding customer IT and buys shared site work and four-hall shell work as well as the first roughly 10 MW hall's fit-out; the completed-campus budget is $400–500 million on the same customer-IT exclusion (§1). On Topic 3's clock, groundbreaking is T+18; site work and foundations run T+18–22, with slab, pads and preliminary embeds; shell and dry-in run T+21–26, retaining the heavy opening; MEP rough-in runs T+24–31; equipment set runs T+30–34; fit-out and early access run T+32–38; substation transformer work and energization occur T+36–38 (Topic 3); commissioning runs T+38–42. These windows overlap rather than add together, and final anchors depend on signed supplier data. Hall 1 ready for service about T+42 is about 24 months after groundbreaking, within the report's indicative 18–30 months; vertical construction is about 16 months, within its 12–24 months.

GB300 and Rubin-class racks head toward 2,000 kg; NVIDIA's Kyber at about 600 kW per rack is slated for late 2027 and a 1 MW rack was announced at OCP 2025, so the 400 psf pod allowance is a course planning value, not future-proof certification.
