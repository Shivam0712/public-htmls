Current chapter.

A rack is a cabinet for computing equipment. IT load is the power drawn by servers, storage and network equipment; it is how a data center's size is usually stated. In colocation, a provider owns the facility and rents space, power and cooling to tenants who bring their own computers. Our example is a 1,000-rack campus outside a hot US city, mixing retail customers who rent racks with wholesale tenants who lease halls. Follow power in and heat out, then follow the same project through delays, contracts and bills.

Start with one campus

A phase is one portion built and opened before the whole campus is finished. Gross cost means bills before tenants reimburse the owner. Power usage effectiveness (PUE) is total facility power divided by IT power. Read these anchors as a completed design with all 40 MW of computing power in use.

The grid connection must supply the computers and their supporting equipment. These costs are course planning assumptions for one hot-climate site, not guaranteed bids.

An AI pod is a group of high-power racks; its 120 kW racks need liquid cooling, which carries heat away in a circulating liquid. Compare the rack groups by power, not just by count.

Just 200 of the 1,000 racks account for 24 MW, so an all-air-cooled quote cannot automatically price this site. It is planned as Tier 3-class and concurrently maintainable: key power and cooling equipment can be serviced without taking the racks offline.

Rounding and phasing

300 × 8 + 300 × 15 + 200 × 40 + 200 × 120 = 38,900 kW. The course rounds this to 40 MW for site sizing and financial examples. Four roughly 10 MW halls are built in stages, while common site routes are planned for all four. APX-foundations §1 and APX-foundations §2.

See where power and heat go

At the site edge, a substation takes grid power and changes its voltage. Switchgear routes electricity, and an uninterruptible power supply (UPS) bridges short outages until backup generators start. The rack floor is white space; support rooms are gray space. The cooling plant carries computer heat outdoors.

Trace the equipment from power entry through the racks to the heat-out arrow.

In the design case, 40 MW reaches the computers while about 16 MW runs cooling and other supporting equipment or is lost in power conversion. Almost all computer power becomes heat; the cooling plant removes that heat before the racks overheat.

What the protected path does

The UPS bridges short interruptions; generators can cover longer outages if designed and fueled to do so. Facility power also includes lighting and controls, so PUE is not a measure of cooling alone. The drawing does not mean every watt passes through the UPS. APX-foundations §2.

Understand who uses the building

An enterprise site belongs to and serves one company. Retail colocation sells racks with power; wholesale colocation leases dedicated halls. A powered shell supplies a building and utility capacity, but not the tenant's finished interior. Hyperscale describes very large cloud facilities, often at least 100 MW, whether owned or leased. Use the table to separate the landlord from the computer owner.

Our provider serves retail customers and wholesale halls, so it must accommodate different tenants and power densities. A hyperscaler can itself lease a wholesale hall: scale and ownership are different questions.

These dated demand signals come from Synergy Research Group, the property firm CBRE and Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory; read their dates as well as their values.

Tenants can commit before a hall opens, and ready space was scarce in those surveys. Strong demand still cannot give an unconnected site utility power (APX-foundations §3).

Categories and limits

Enterprise, retail and wholesale describe use and sales arrangements; hyperscale describes scale. Edge facilities sit near users. Factory-built modules are a construction method, not a customer type. Definitions: APX-foundations §1. The market measurements have different dates and geographic scopes: APX-foundations §3.

Plan the site before building a hall

Grid interconnection is the utility's approval and physical work to connect a site to the power network. A large power transformer changes voltage for the campus; its lead time is the wait from order to delivery. The following stages overlap, so do not add their durations to calculate an opening date.

The course plans on roughly 128 weeks for its transformer, not the report's 160-plus-week market quote. At 160 weeks from month 8, delivery moves toward month 45; faster building cannot make the transformer or grid connection arrive sooner (APX-foundations §4).

Look for the fixed power and fiber routes around the one built hall.

Hall 1 can open as a roughly 10 MW phase, but the substation location and two fiber routes serve all four halls. Phasing defers some equipment purchases; it does not make the first phase exactly one quarter of total site cost.

Site timing is not construction timing

Utility work can run longer than site purchase, and a completed hall can wait dark for power. The 128-week assumption begins with an order around month 8; 160 weeks is a longer-lead scenario, not the course baseline. APX-foundations §4.

Give each party a job and a risk

The owner secures grid power and sets requirements. A design-builder is one contractor responsible for design and construction; trade crews install systems. Commissioning means testing against the requirements, with an independent commissioning agent reporting results to the owner. Follow the handovers to the facility operator, the team that runs the site after opening.

The builder hands over a test-ready system, not merely a shell. An owner-directed tester can expose faults before the operating team takes control.

Long-lead equipment takes months or years to arrive. The owner can supply owner-furnished equipment (OFE), buying gear for the builder to install. A strict turnkey contract for engineering, procurement and construction (EPC) normally makes the contractor the buyer. Design-bid-build keeps an owner-hired designer separate; a construction manager at risk also leaves design with the owner. A guaranteed maximum price (GMP) caps agreed construction spending. Compare the owner's remaining exposure.

Here the owner orders the transformer while one design-builder coordinates its installation and the rest of the build. The builder owns its agreed execution scope, but grid timing and late owner gear do not transfer simply because the contract says design-build.

A missing transformer can strand a finished hall

Specify who receives, stores and tests owner-bought gear, and who bears delays or defects. A design-build or turnkey label cannot transfer an unpromised grid connection.

What the contract comparison cannot promise

Under design-bid-build, separate design and building contracts leave the owner exposed to drawing gaps. Under a manager-at-risk arrangement, a price ceiling still has scope exclusions. Historical cross-sector comparisons found design-build generally faster end to end, but they do not predict a data center's utility connection or owner-gear delivery. Roles: APX-foundations §5; contracts: APX-foundations §6; evidence: APX-foundations §7.

Check what a build price includes

Capital expenditure (capex) is the one-off build cost. Prices per MW usually mean per MW of IT load and exclude tenant computers. Shell and core means a building with specified base works, not necessarily a finished hall; build-to-suit means built to a customer's specification. Read the scope and year before comparing prices.

Our $400–500M campus figure is a course assumption for a partly liquid-cooled facility, not 40 times an air-cooled Phoenix quote. The spread reflects different products, scopes and years, not four bids for the same site.

To see what a build budget buys, compare the published shares for an air-cooled facility.

Electrical and mechanical systems total 76%; core, shell and architectural work is 14% in this benchmark. These exact shares are not a measured breakdown of our mixed-cooling campus.

The first phase is not a quarter of the budget

A provisional, unverified phase-1 estimate is $180–230M for about 10 MW, including shared shell and site work planned for all four halls. Verify its scope before treating it as a funding commitment. In a liquid-cooled benchmark, electrical work is 48% and mechanical work 33%. Source scopes and dates: APX-foundations §8; system shares: APX-foundations §9.

Keep the yearly bill distinct

Operating expenditure (opex) is annual running cost. Full load means all 40 MW of IT power is being drawn; part load means less. The design ratio, expected full-load ratio and first-hall meter estimate describe different conditions. Read each PUE value with its condition; use $0.07 per kilowatt-hour for the modeled electricity bill.

The same equipment can use proportionally more overhead power when halls are partly filled. The $34.3M bill uses design PUE at full load, not either forecast operating ratio.

Now split the approximately $65M full-load gross annual bill into its component costs.

Electricity is roughly half the gross bill, and colocation leases often reimburse some or all of it. About 55 staff is within the course's 50–58-person range for the complete 40 MW campus.

Assumptions behind the annual numbers

The staff line rounds 55 × $130,000 to $7.2M. The report's 60 staff and $7.8M are a high-end ceiling, not the central course case. The $6.8M other-cost allowance reconciles indicative lines; it is not a supplier quote. At full design load, 56,000 kW × 8,760 × $0.07/kWh = $34.339M. The first phase draws less IT power even if its metered PUE is higher. APX-foundations §2, APX-foundations §10 and APX-foundations §12.

Add costs across the facility life

Total cost of ownership (TCO) adds building, operating and replacement spending. Discounting converts future bills to present value, their equivalent today; owner-borne cost means gross bills less actual tenant reimbursements. This model assumes all 40 MW operate from year one, not a forecast of how quickly halls lease. Add the lines before comparing the totals.

Gross cash cost reaches about $1.48 billion; at an 8% discount rate, those same assumed outlays are worth about $1.03 billion today. Full electricity reimbursement would lower owner-borne cost, but would not reduce electricity use and is not guaranteed by every lease.

What this model leaves out

The arithmetic assumes 40 MW runs all 8,760 hours every year, constant prices and one $54M replacement around year 12. Actual phased occupancy will differ. Wholesale tenants may pay base rent for committed capacity and reimburse power separately. At full occupancy, a hypothetical base rent of $160 per kW each month across 40,000 kW is $76.8M a year if electricity is extra. Do not compare that rent with gross operating cost without including the corresponding power receipts. APX-foundations §11 and APX-foundations §12.

  • Use the rack mix and retained cutaway to trace electricity, protected power, cooling and heat for that same campus.
  • Distinguish facility users and owners, then introduce a small number of dated market signals.

  • Separate build-price scope, annual gross bills and 15-year gross versus reimbursed costs. Keep source detail and caveats in the appendix or collapsed depth.

Glossary additions (31)
Rack
A cabinet that holds computing, storage and network equipment.
Rack power density
The power drawn by one rack, used to size its electrical supply and cooling.
Power usage effectiveness (PUE)
Total facility power divided by IT power; a design value of 1.4 turns 40 MW IT into 56 MW total.
AI pod
A group of high-power racks used for AI computing, often needing liquid cooling.
Liquid cooling
Moving computer heat through circulating liquid instead of relying on room air alone.
Tier 3-class facility
A facility designed for concurrent maintenance of key power and cooling systems, without implying formal certification.
Concurrent maintainability
The ability to service a major power or cooling component while computers remain online.
Phased construction
Opening portions of a planned campus in stages while reserving shared infrastructure for the whole site.
Substation
The site's connection point for high-voltage grid power and equipment that changes or routes its voltage.
Switchgear
Electrical equipment that routes power and isolates circuits during faults or maintenance.
Uninterruptible power supply (UPS)
Short-term backup that keeps protected equipment powered during a grid interruption.
Backup generator
On-site equipment that can produce electricity during a longer grid outage.
Cooling plant
Facility equipment that carries computer heat away and rejects it outdoors.
Grid interconnection
Utility approval and physical work needed to connect a site's agreed power demand to the grid.
Power transformer
A large device that changes electrical voltage between the grid and the campus.
Long-lead equipment
Gear ordered far ahead because manufacturing and delivery take months or years.
Commissioning
Planned testing that checks whether finished systems meet the owner's requirements.
Commissioning agent
The independent specialist who witnesses and reports the results of facility tests.
Facility operator
The team that runs, monitors and maintains the site after it opens.
Design-builder
One contractor responsible for both design and construction under a design-build contract.
Design-build
A delivery arrangement putting design and construction under one contractor.
Design-bid-build
A delivery arrangement in which the owner hires a designer and then separately hires a builder.
Engineering, procurement and construction (EPC)
A contract scope combining design, specified equipment purchasing and construction, subject to written exclusions.
Shell and core
A building and specified base works that may not include a finished interior ready for computers.
Full load and part load
Full load uses the modeled IT capacity; part load uses less than that capacity.
Electricity pass-through
A lease arrangement in which tenants reimburse the operator for some or all electricity it purchases.
Base rent
Payment for leased space and committed capacity before any separately billed electricity.
Discounting
Converting future payments to equivalent amounts today using an assumed annual rate.
Present value
The today-dollar equivalent of future payments after discounting.
Gross and owner-borne cost
Gross includes all site bills; owner-borne cost subtracts amounts actually reimbursed by tenants.
Handover
Transfer of the tested facility and its records from the building team to the operating team.
Dropped figures

None: every existing figure is kept.
