Data centers: from the rack to the lifetime bill

Preview of the rewritten Topic 1. This is the chapter-rewrite pipeline's result, verified and with its appendix and glossary edits applied, shown in the new page design for review. It is not yet the course text: the current chapter is at L3-foundations.html; deep detail and sources are in the appendix.

Imagine a building where customers bring computers but the landlord must keep them powered, cooled and connected. That is colocation: a provider rents space and services to customers who own the computers. In any data center, almost every watt used by the computers becomes heat. Computing gear is information technology (IT); IT load is its electrical draw, not the building's total. A megawatt (MW) is 1,000 kilowatts (kW), and a rack is a cabinet of computing gear. Power usage effectiveness (PUE) is total facility power divided by IT power. Follow one campus from rack heat to grid access, contracts and lifetime bills.

Meet the reference campus

Our example has 1,000 racks near Phoenix or Dallas and serves small and large tenants. Two hundred racks belong to artificial intelligence (AI) pods rated at 120 kW per rack. These need liquid cooling, which carries heat in fluid near the processors. Its Tier III-class goal is concurrent maintenance: essential equipment can be serviced without stopping tenants. This is a design intent, not a claimed certification.

Takeaway: A cabinet count conceals where most electricity and heat arise.

Takeaway: The densest 20% of racks carry about 62% of rack power. Air cooling moves heat through room air; this campus also needs liquid. The 40 MW and 56 MW figures are rounded design points, not what the first hall will meter. See the foundations appendix, APX-foundations §2.

Rounding and resilience

The rack arithmetic is 300 × 8 + 300 × 15 + 200 × 40 + 200 × 120 = 38,900 kW, or 38.9 MW. Applying PUE 1.4 to that exact sum gives about 54.5 MW. The course instead uses rounded 40 MW IT and 56 MW total for campus planning.

In reliability shorthand, N means just enough units to carry the required load; N+1 adds one spare unit, while 2N duplicates a complete path. These choices do not themselves establish formal Tier certification. Design choices are covered in APX-foundations §4.

See power enter and heat leave

A substation changes the voltage of incoming grid power; switchgear routes it. An uninterruptible power supply (UPS) keeps protected equipment running through brief interruptions, while backup generators cover longer ones. The rack floor is called white space; power and cooling rooms are gray space. Follow power through the cutaway, then find where heat exits.

Takeaway: At full design load, the racks shed nearly their 40 MW electrical input as heat. The roughly 16 MW beyond IT runs cooling, power conversion and other services; it is not a measurement of cooling alone.

Takeaway: The 8 kW example primarily uses air. The 120 kW pod needs liquid near its processors as well as some air. Both paths ultimately reject heat outdoors (APX-foundations §2).

What PUE does not tell you

PUE shows total support electricity, not the separate draw of each fan, pump or transformer. The course's 1.4 is a design-sizing value. Later operating illustrations may meter about 1.55–1.65 in a partly loaded first phase or about 1.25 at full load. Those are different operating conditions, not competing definitions. The 40 kW racks need cooling selected for their actual equipment, not an automatic air-only assignment.

Tell facility types apart by ownership

Facility types are mainly about who owns the building and who brings the computers. An enterprise site is one company's own facility. Retail colocation sells cabinets to smaller customers; wholesale colocation leases dedicated rooms and larger power blocks. The shell is the roof, walls and structure. A powered shell adds utility power capacity but leaves interior power and cooling installation to the customer. Hyperscale describes a very large, standardized cloud operation, whether owned or leased.

Takeaway: Our campus combines retail and wholesale space, so its provider must accommodate customers with different machines. Prefabricated modules describe how to build, not who buys the space (APX-foundations §1).

When the labels blur

An edge site places relatively small computing capacity near users to reduce communication delay. Cloud operators may lease wholesale space instead of building their own sites. A dedicated AI factory describes a workload; prefabrication describes a construction method. Neither is automatically a separate ownership model.

Understand why capacity is claimed early

A kilowatt-hour (kWh) is the energy from drawing 1 kW for one hour; a terawatt-hour (TWh) is a billion kWh. Vacancy is capacity available but unleased. Precommitted capacity has been reserved by a customer before opening. The snapshots below cover different markets and years, not slices of one pie.

Takeaway: Demand helps explain early reservations, but national statistics cannot deliver electricity to this site. Grid interconnection is the utility's approval and physical work to supply an agreed load; in constrained regions it can outlast building construction (APX-foundations §3).

Read the schedule as overlapping clocks

The owner needs land, utility service and long-lead equipment, gear with unusually long manufacturing times. Factory release means approving a stable specification so the maker can begin the production lead time. Commissioning means testing installed systems against the owner's requirements before handover. Phasing is not four equal cheques

The first phase pays for some shared access, land preparation and utility arrangements; later phases add their own power and cooling equipment. Its $180–230 million cost is a provisional course assumption, not a contractor bid. Grid waits of four years or more describe constrained locations, not a guaranteed wait at every Phoenix or Dallas site.

Track the people behind each handoff

The owner funds the campus and specifies what it must do; the electric utility supplies its grid connection. The owner's engineer checks the design. Architects and mechanical, electrical and plumbing (MEP) engineers design the building and its services; builders and specialist trades install them. An independent commissioning agent witnesses tests and reports faults to the owner before the operations team accepts the hall.

Takeaway: These arrows are handoffs, not a stack of subcontracts. Depending on the delivery model, designers may work for the owner or the builder. The tester should remain independent (APX-foundations §5).

The written yardsticks

The owner's project requirements (OPR) state measurable needs, including capacity, maintainability and tests. The basis of design (BOD) records how the designers intend to meet them. The owner's representative tracks budget and schedule. Independent testing does not remove the designer's or builder's responsibility for faults.

Choose a contract without losing sight of the grid

In design-bid-build, the owner hires a designer and then a separate builder. Design-build gives both jobs to one contractor. Engineering, procurement and construction (EPC) is often a fixed-price turnkey arrangement, intended to deliver a complete working facility, although its scope varies. A construction manager at risk joins during design and offers a guaranteed maximum price (GMP), a ceiling for agreed work. Owner-furnished equipment (OFE) is gear the owner buys for the builder to install.

Takeaway: Design-build combines responsibility for drawings and construction, but it cannot make the utility or an owner-bought transformer arrive sooner. Even turnkey protection applies to defined work and contract exceptions (APX-foundations §6).

What the owner cannot transfer away

For this campus, choose design-build with OFE so the owner can book slow-to-make gear while design progresses. One design-builder coordinates installation, but the owner must secure utility power and deliver its purchases as promised. Specify who stores equipment, corrects defects, pays for late delivery and gets an extension if owner gear or grid service is late. A turnkey label alone settles none of this.

Why the model alone cannot promise a date

A guaranteed maximum price covers agreed scope; owner changes and exclusions can alter it. Engineering, procurement and construction terminology comes from other industries and does not always describe an all-inclusive data-center contract. Historical, cross-sector evidence favors overlapping design and construction for speed, but its percentages cannot predict this campus's utility wait (APX-foundations §7). Contract provisions are detailed in APX-foundations §6.

Read a build quote before trusting it

Capital expenditure (capex) is the upfront building cost. Quotes are commonly per MW of IT capacity and exclude customer computers. Shell and core is a narrower base-building scope; all-in aims to include a fitted facility, but land, site power and fees can still be treated differently. These markers describe different dates and products, not bids for one site.

Turn capacity into a yearly bill

Operating expenditure (opex) is the recurring cost of running the campus. Gross means before tenants reimburse a bill. For a teaching year, suppose all 40 MW of IT runs every hour at design PUE 1.4, and purchased electricity costs $0.07 per kWh. This is a full-capacity model, not a first-phase meter reading.

Reproduce the annual amounts

56,000 kW × 8,760 hours = 490.56 million kWh. At $0.07 per kWh, that costs about $34.34 million. At the $450 million build midpoint, 3% maintenance is $13.5 million and 0.7% insurance is about $3.2 million. Sixty staff at an illustrative $130,000 each cost $7.8 million. The remaining modeled property, water and other costs are $6 million. A real bill depends on load, tariff and lease terms.

See what a lease and a lifetime total mean

Total cost of ownership (TCO) adds construction, running costs and later equipment renewal. This teaching model puts the $450 million build midpoint up front, operates all halls at design load for 15 years and renews $54 million of equipment in year eight. Gross is before tenant reimbursements; owner-borne subtracts modeled reimbursements. Discounting converts future costs into present value, their equivalent today, here using 8% a year.

Takeaway: Full operation from year one is deliberately unrealistic; the figure isolates the arithmetic. Full recovery of electricity is also an assumption, not a promise about every lease (APX-foundations §11).

Base rent pays for reserved space and power capacity. An electricity pass-through repays some or all of the utility bill under the lease. For illustration only, suppose all 40,000 kW leases at $160 per kW per month.

Takeaway: Do not add reimbursements to rent and call the sum profit. Halls fill over time, and financing and renewals still need funding (APX-foundations §12).

Check the lifetime arithmetic

The undiscounted gross total is $450 million + 15 × $65 million + $54 million = $1.479 billion. At 8%, fifteen equal $65 million payments have a present value of about $556 million; a $54 million payment in year eight is worth about $29 million today. With the initial build, that is about $1.04 billion. If all modeled power is reimbursed, owner outlays before rent are about $450 million + 15 × $30.5 million + $54 million = $0.96 billion. The $160 rent is a hypothetical blended rate, not an observed price for every retail and wholesale tenant.
