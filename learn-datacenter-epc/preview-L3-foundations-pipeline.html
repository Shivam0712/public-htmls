How a data center gets built and paid for

Preview of the rewritten Topic 1, Iteration 2. This is the chapter-rewrite pipeline's result, verified and with its appendix and glossary edits applied, shown in the new page design for review. It is not yet the course text: the current chapter is at L3-foundations.html; deep detail and sources are in the appendix.

Imagine planning a site outside Phoenix or Dallas. A data center supplies computers with steady electricity, carries away their heat and connects them to networks. In colocation, the provider owns the facility and rents capacity to customers, called tenants, who bring their own equipment. A rack is a cabinet of those computers.

Servers and networking equipment are information technology (IT); the power they draw is IT load. Power is measured in megawatts (MW), each equal to 1,000 kilowatts (kW). Power usage effectiveness (PUE) is total facility power divided by IT power. These six numbers describe the site we will follow.

Follow power in and heat out

Before a tenant can run a rack, the electric utility, which supplies grid power, must connect the site. A substation receives that power and changes its voltage; switchgear routes it and isolates faults. An uninterruptible power supply (UPS) bridges brief interruptions, while backup generators cover longer outages. The rack room is called white space, and the cooling plant takes its heat outdoors. The cutaway shows how these parts fit together.

Takeaway: The total draw at the fence is facility load: at this design load, 40 MW serves IT and another 16 MW supports cooling, electrical losses and other services. Nearly all electricity used by IT becomes heat. Design PUE 1.4 sizes the project; it is not a promise of the meter reading when only part of the site is occupied. See who owns the computers

Who brings the servers changes what the owner must build and how it is paid. Retail colocation rents a few racks with power to a small customer; wholesale colocation rents larger reserved blocks or whole halls. Our owner uses both, but tenants bring their own computers.

An enterprise data center is built for its owner's own computers. A hyperscale operator is a very large cloud company that may own a site or lease wholesale space. A powered shell provides a structure and grid access, but the tenant installs its interior power and cooling systems. The table shows which party brings which part.

Takeaway: Our provider builds the power and cooling backbone; tenants buy their servers. That split determines which construction costs belong in the owner's budget. Vacancy is the share of rentable capacity still unleased; preleasing means a customer commits before a building opens. The next figures show strong demand, not a guarantee that a particular site can open on time.

Takeaway: A signed lease is not a grid connection, so power availability still governs opening. Market definitions and sources are in APX-foundations §1 and APX-foundations §3.

Other types and market details

An edge data center is a small site near users to cut network delay. An AI factory serves one dense computing workload; modular construction means factory-built pieces assembled on site, not a class of tenant. One terawatt-hour (TWh) is a billion kilowatt-hours of energy; United States data centers used 176 TWh in 2023, or 4.4% of national electricity. The vacancy, preleasing and capacity figures come from different surveys and dates. Sources: APX-foundations §3 and APX-foundations §13.

Plan all four halls before opening one

The first roughly 10 MW hall will serve tenants while three more are still planned. Moving the substation or cooling yard afterward could disrupt the live hall. Fiber-optic cables carry data to the outside network, so two separate entry routes need space from day one. The drawing sets out the whole footprint before the first opening.

Takeaway: Phase 1, the first hall, costs about $180–230 million because it includes up-front shell and site work shared with later halls. The entire 40 MW site costs $400–500 million, including Phase 1 and excluding tenants' computers. It is Tier III class: key power and cooling equipment can be serviced while computers keep running, an ability called concurrent maintenance, without claiming formal certification. Phasing details are in APX-foundations §4.

One spare unit is not a full duplicate

Critical load is the computing gear and essential cooling supplied by protected power. N means exactly enough units to carry the load, N+1 adds one spare, and 2N provides an independent full second system. Those configurations specify spare capacity, not proof that the installed equipment works. See APX-foundations §2.

Watch the clocks that govern opening

A built hall can sit empty if grid power or a transformer is late. Grid interconnection means utility approval and physical work to deliver the site's planned power. A transformer changes voltage; it is long-lead equipment, gear that takes unusually long to make.

Factory release is approval of a transformer's final specification, which starts its maker's production clock. Commissioning is planned testing against the owner's requirements; handover passes the tested hall to the operator, the team that runs it. The overlapping stages below show when choices become hard to reverse, not times to add together.

Takeaway: The 128-week transformer time is this course's baseline, not a promise for every order. A 160-week quote shifts delivery by roughly eight months. Building faster cannot make the utility supply power early. Durations and overlaps are in APX-foundations §4.

Why two transformer numbers appear

The course releases its transformer specification about eight months after project start. At 128 weeks from release, arrival is around months 35–38; a 160-week order lands around month 45, prompting a plan for temporary on-site power from gas generators. Some 2026 market quotes exceed 160 weeks, while the shorter figure remains the course baseline. Utility connection can also exceed four years in constrained markets; these clocks overlap rather than add. See APX-foundations §4.

See who does the work and who checks it

With the utility and equipment maker on separate clocks, who delivers and checks the building? The owner writes measurable requirements and pays for the work. A design-builder is one contractor responsible for both drawing and constructing the facility; electricians and cooling specialists do much of the installation. An independent commissioning agent plans and witnesses tests, reporting findings directly to the owner. The owner's operating team runs the accepted site. These arrows show handoffs, not a chain of contracts.

Takeaway: The tester must be free to report failed tests even when they delay opening. Involving the operator and commissioning agent during design can also reveal impractical requirements before they are built. More roles are explained in APX-foundations §5.

The specialists behind the boxes

The owner's representative manages the budget, schedule and handoffs. The owner's engineer reviews technical drawings against the owner's standards. Architects design the building layout and address building rules, while power and cooling engineers lay out its working systems. Equipment makers supply long-lead gear, and trade contractors install it. See APX-foundations §5.

Choose a contract that matches the risks

Now that the people are visible, a contract decides who pays when their work fails. Design-bid-build hires a designer, then a separate builder, leaving design errors with the owner. Design-build puts design and construction under one contractor. Our owner uses a reference design, a repeatable plan for its halls.

Engineering, procurement and construction (EPC) turnkey promises a complete facility for a defined price and date, with included risks priced into that promise. A construction manager at risk joins early and offers a guaranteed maximum price (GMP), a ceiling for agreed work while the owner retains its designer. Owner-furnished equipment (OFE) is gear the owner buys and hands over for installation so its production can start early. The table distinguishes building work from external supplies.

Takeaway: Separate design and building contracts take longer to hand off; design-build lets that work overlap. OFE lets this owner book equipment production early, but leaves it responsible for its orders. See APX-foundations §6 for the other contract choices.

The date a contractor cannot promise away

The utility controls grid availability. The contract must say who receives, stores and tests the owner's gear, and whether late or defective delivery extends the builder's deadline. Otherwise a fixed-price completion date can hide an impossible opening date.

What other models buy

Integrated project delivery puts the owner, designer and builder in one shared-risk agreement. Cross-industry evidence favors design-build for speed, but its percentage gains are not a forecast for a particular data center in 2026 (APX-foundations §7). Liquidated damages are pre-agreed payments for late completion of agreed work; they do not make utility power appear.

Price the whole facility, not only its shell

The contract allocates overruns, but it does not change which systems are expensive. Capital expenditure (capex) is the upfront cost to build the site. Mechanical, electrical and plumbing (MEP) is shorthand for power, cooling and related pipework. What can move the price

One study quotes a 7–10% premium for liquid-cooled versus comparable air-cooled construction at equal IT power. That does not price this mixed campus by itself. Redundant equipment, hot-weather cooling, skilled labor and speed also change costs. See APX-foundations §8 and APX-foundations §9.

Separate the annual bill from what tenants repay

Building cost is only the first bill. Operating expenditure (opex) is the recurring cost of running the site. A kilowatt-hour (kWh) is the energy used by one kilowatt of power for one hour. At full planned load, 56,000 kW for 8,760 hours at $0.07 per kWh costs about $34.3 million in electricity a year. This uses design PUE for a full-load illustration, not a predicted meter reading for the partly occupied first phase. Here are the full-site bills before tenant reimbursements.

Takeaway: The pieces sum to about $65 million, with about $30.5 million unrelated to electricity. Colocation leases commonly make tenants reimburse most power use; that is an electricity pass-through, not extra rent. Separately, tenants pay for reserved space and power capacity. See APX-foundations §10 and APX-foundations §12.

Rent is not the same as power reimbursement

At a fully committed 40,000 kW, charging $160–196 per kW of reserved capacity each month gives about $77–94 million in yearly base rent, before separately billed electricity. This illustrates full leasing; it does not forecast profit. Either compare base rent with non-power costs, or add electricity repayments to rent and include the full electricity expense. Actual bills vary with occupancy, lease terms and measured PUE. See APX-foundations §12.

Count the cost of the whole life

Total cost of ownership (TCO) adds the build, yearly bills and major equipment replacement. Gross means before tenant repayments; undiscounted means adding each payment without adjusting for when it occurs. Present value turns future bills into today's dollars using a chosen yearly rate, 8% in this example. At a $450 million build midpoint, these are three ways to count the same 15 years.

Takeaway: The $0.96 billion is not profit; it merely omits electricity tenants repay. The arithmetic is in APX-foundations §11.

How the totals are made

Gross: 450 + 15 × 65 + 54 = 1,479 million, rounded to $1.48 billion. Non-power bills are about $30.5 million a year, so 450 + 15 × 30.5 + 54 = 961.5 million, rounded to $0.96 billion. At an 8% yearly discount rate, 15 level $65 million bills are worth roughly $556 million today; putting the $54 million refresh in year 12 makes it worth roughly $21 million today. Add those to the initial $450 million to get about $1.03 billion. These are planning costs before tenant computers or rent, not an investment return.
