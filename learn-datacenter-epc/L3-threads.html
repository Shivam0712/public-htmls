How one data center fits together

Deep detail and sources: the appendix for this topic. Short version: sixty words. Learn it as cards: 77 cards. Earlier version: archived 2026-09-26.

Picture 1,000 racks, cabinets for computers, near Phoenix or Dallas. In colocation, a provider rents rack space, electricity and cooling to tenants, who bring most of the computing gear. That gear is information technology (IT); its draw is the IT load. One kilowatt (kW) equals 1,000 watts (W), and one megawatt (MW) equals 1,000 kW. The rack plan adds to 38.9 MW, rounded to 40 MW. Power usage effectiveness (PUE) is whole-site power divided by IT power at the same load. Design PUE 1.4 therefore calls for about 56 MW at full build, not a reading from the first occupied hall. Four roughly 10 MW phases open over time. The Tier III-class aim is to service essential equipment without stopping customers' machines. We will trace what makes that possible.

A rack's power rating is an allowance for equipment a tenant may install, not today's meter reading. The most demanding positions sit in artificial intelligence (AI) pods, groups of high-power racks working together. Their place in the bar explains why the owner cannot build every row identically:

Follow electricity into the rack

Both an 8 kW rack and a 120 kW rack need a supply that survives planned electrical work. The utility delivers electricity at 138 kilovolts (kV); one kilovolt is 1,000 volts (V). A substation reduces that voltage, while switchgear protects and routes circuits along a shared conductor called a power bus. An uninterruptible power supply (UPS) uses batteries to bridge a brief supply interruption for the computers. A floor power distribution unit (PDU) divides the protected supply into smaller circuits; overhead busway carries them along rack rows. The figure follows one route. A separate route serves the computer's second power cord.

Takeaway: The first roughly 10 MW of IT load needs about 14 MW for the hall once cooling and losses are included. The UPS protects the computer feed immediately; standby generators, engines that make electricity on site, must cover the full 14 MW after starting. Each rack's A and B routes must be able to carry its entire load when the other is unavailable. The 56 MW completed campus also needs upstream expansion beyond the opening arrangement. See APX-threads §1.

What counts in the IT allowance

The appendix also sketches AI network cabinets outside the listed 1,000 racks. Their electricity is not automatically in the 38.9 MW sum. Allocate it within an agreed IT allowance or revise the rack plan and roughly 56 MW utility request; do not silently add another load. See APX-threads §1 and APX-threads §2.

Follow a connection from street to server

A powered rack still needs an outside network. A carrier, an outside network supplier, brings fiber-optic cable, thin glass that carries data as light, from an off-site carrier hotel where providers connect. Its cables travel in two duct banks, separate buried groups of conduits, to an entrance room (ER) on each side. The hotel's 10-kilometer (km) distance is illustrative. In a meet-me room (MMR), a cross-connect joins carrier and tenant connections.

From there the main distribution area (MDA), the hall's fiber hub, serves each horizontal distribution area (HDA), a pod patching point. The tenant's network rack switches traffic toward a graphics processing unit (GPU) rack, whose chips perform parallel computing. Follow this physical route:

Do not count rooms as switch hops

The plan labels one of two entrance rooms and one of several pod patching points. Its carrier hotel is not a campus room. The number of rooms along a cable route does not tell you how many switches a tenant's data crosses. See APX-threads §2.

Follow the rack's heat to outdoor air

Nearly all rack electricity becomes heat. For a 120 kW rack, cold plates on chips carry about 90 kW into liquid; 30 kW remains in room air. A coolant distribution unit (CDU) transfers that heat to separate facility water without mixing the fluids. In degrees Celsius (°C), rack liquid runs 40 °C in and 48 °C out; facility water runs 36 °C back from the roof and 46 °C toward it. Dry coolers use outdoor fans; yard chillers use compressors. Heat in the opening phase

About 4.5 MW of the first phase's pod heat takes the warmer liquid route. The chilled route carries about 6.2 MW, including roughly 0.7 MW of in-hall electrical losses. Those are heat-removal duties, not another 10.7 MW to add to the IT electricity bill. See APX-threads §3.

The illustrated 15-second pickup also cannot prove a ten-second rule for any emergency loads subject to one. See APX-threads §4.

A separate emergency-power clock

National Fire Protection Association (NFPA) 110 Type 10 requires specified emergency loads to receive power at their transfer-switch terminals within ten seconds. A hall transfer illustrated at fifteen seconds does not establish compliance for those loads. A dead utility feeder cannot participate in a closed transition between two live sources; transfer back after grid recovery is a separate operation. UPS capacity sized for 10 MW of IT must also be revisited if pumps or fans are added to its protected load.

Check the promises with one part unavailable

Equipment drawn twice is not necessarily a working spare. The Tier III-class goal requires planned maintenance of covered equipment without stopping the computers. The owner must approve a seventh set, larger site-rated sets or a revised and proven load requirement; the stated six-set yard is not N+1 for 14 MW. The opening transformer pair likewise cannot stand in for a full-campus expansion plan. A commissioning test can reveal missing capacity, not create it. See APX-threads §5 and APX-threads §7.

Why the UPS blocks do not fix the engine yard

The six UPS blocks use a three-to-make-two (3M2) layout for 10 MW of IT: two groups each serve 5 MW, with two of each group's three blocks able to carry that group. Their A and B distribution still needs a witnessed test. This rack-power arrangement does not make the generators N+1 or complete the full-campus substation. See APX-threads §1 and APX-threads §5.

Connect delivery risks to the bill

Who pays if one of those links needs a redesign? Under design-build, one firm designs and constructs the agreed site, but the owner buys some early-order gear as owner-furnished equipment (OFE). Commissioning is planned testing of installed systems; an independent agent hired by the owner witnesses it. The first roughly 10 MW phase costs $180–230 million including shared site and shell work, within the $400–500 million full-facility build. Tenants' computers are outside both figures. Takeaway: The four amounts total about $1.48 billion. Tenant reimbursement of electricity, called a power pass-through, does not make that gross bill disappear or turn it into rent. This is a full-campus cost illustration, not the opening hall's cash forecast. See APX-threads §6.

Delivery dates have different owners

The utility controls its connection; the owner controls orders for its purchased gear; the design-builder controls agreed installation. Name who receives, stores and tests owner-furnished equipment and what happens when it is late. Price and approve the generator correction before promising maintainable service. The independent commissioning agent must be able to report a failed test even if it delays tenant opening.

Costs without double counting

Early land and equipment payments are components of the phase budget, not additions to its $180–230 million estimate. Utility collateral, money or credit pledged against a commitment, is not automatically construction spending. The model assumes full-campus operation for all fifteen years. If all electricity is repaid, the owner-borne cost is about $0.96 billion before rent. At an 8% annual discount rate, which reduces the weight of later bills, gross present value is about $1.03 billion in today's dollars. Neither number is profit. See APX-threads §6.
