Proving, running and retiring a data center

Deep detail and sources: the appendix for this topic. Short version: sixty words. The research report this chapter was distilled from. Earlier version: archived 2026-09-26.

The course's 1,000-rack campus sits outside a hot metro such as Phoenix or Dallas. It is a colocation site: one operator sells powered, cooled space to multiple tenants, from whole halls to individual racks. Its mix totals 38.9 megawatts (MW) of information technology (IT) load, rounded to 40 MW for design; one megawatt is 1,000 kilowatts (kW). The site opens in roughly 10 MW phases and aims for Tier III class, meaning critical equipment can be removed for planned maintenance without interrupting customer service. This chapter follows the first phase from proof to everyday operation and a tenant move-out, then tests a second phase beside live customers.

What the first hall has to prove

Power usage effectiveness (PUE) is total facility power divided by IT power. At its design value of 1.4, roughly 40 MW for servers requires about 56 MW at the utility fence, the site's incoming-power boundary, including cooling and electrical overhead. Design load means this intended full-load condition, not the load tenants happen to use today.

Takeaway: The rack count hides where the heat is concentrated. Artificial intelligence (AI) pods pack the greatest demand into just 200 racks. The mix shows why liquid cooling deserves its own tests.

Takeaway: One fifth of the racks carry about three fifths of the IT load. A failure in their liquid loop would therefore affect a large share of the campus, not a niche corner. The plan has to prove that loop as well as the electrical supply (APX-commissioning §2).

Budget context

Phase 1 costs about $180–230 million because its build includes shared site and shell work needed by later halls. The whole 40 MW campus costs $400–500 million excluding tenants' IT equipment; at full build, the $65 million annual operating figure includes about $34 million for electricity. These are the course's planning figures, not universal unit costs (APX-commissioning §3).

Prove each part before the whole hall

A switchboard can pass a factory check and still be wired wrongly on site. Commissioning is the planned proof that installed equipment meets the owner's written requirements under normal operation and failures. The owner directly hires an independent commissioning agent (CxA) to agree test scripts, meaning the steps and pass criteria, witness key tests and log what must be fixed. The boundary of each successive test gets wider.

Takeaway: Factory and receiving checks overlap construction; the field period for Levels 3–5 is about 12–16 weeks including preparation and corrections. A successful component test never substitutes for a successful whole-system test. The level numbers are an industry convention, while actual pass conditions come from the project test plan (APX-commissioning §1; APX-commissioning §3).

What happens before energization

These levels are not the commissioning phases described by the American Society of Heating, Refrigerating and Air-Conditioning Engineers (ASHRAE). Electrical acceptance before energization follows the 2025 specifications of the InterNational Electrical Testing Association (NETA) and is performed by a qualified testing firm. On the course clock, on-site tests span roughly months 38–42 after the site option, with Phase 1 targeted to open around month 42 (APX-commissioning §1; APX-commissioning §2).

Give an empty hall a real test load

No customer computers are installed yet, so the team needs something else to draw power and heat the room. A load bank is temporary equipment that consumes controlled electricity instead of doing computing. Phase 1 needs about 10 MW of test load, built up in steps; a coolant distribution unit (CDU) transfers heat from liquid-cooled rack loops to the site cooling plant. Not every load bank can test every part of that chain.

Takeaway: Before heating the liquid loop, the crew flushes it to its agreed cleanliness, checks flow and pressure, and checks for leaks. Outdoor trailers cannot prove rack inlet temperatures or that clean coolant reaches an AI pod. The operations team should learn these checks while it watches the tests (APX-commissioning §2).

What the test equipment costs

Rack-level load banks help locate hot spots; larger trailers can also test generators under different electrical conditions. A liquid-cooled unit's roughly 1 MW rating is a unit size, not a claim that 1 MW proves every liquid-cooled rack. Load-bank rental, test energy, fuel and vendor standby add to commissioning costs; the broad planning allowance for commissioning is often 0.5–2% of construction cost (APX-commissioning §2; APX-commissioning §3).

Cut the utility feed on purpose

A Level 5 integrated systems test (IST) deliberately simulates losing utility power at full artificial load. An uninterruptible power supply (UPS) bridges the time until generators start and transfer controls take over. Its batteries hold the critical bus, the conductors supplying the IT load, while cooling rides through the interruption. The electrical power monitoring system (EPMS) records bus voltage alongside room-temperature readings.

Takeaway: A generator starting is not a pass on its own. The critical bus must stay energized, temperatures must remain in the agreed band, and transfers must happen in the specified order. The team records those checks through both loss and restoration of utility (APX-commissioning §2).

Takeaway: The open breaker is only the start of the picture. The uninterrupted bus trace and in-range temperature at the monitoring wall are the evidence the future tenant needs. A failed step is repaired and retested, not signed away (APX-commissioning §4).

What goes into the script

The approved script specifies generator and transfer timings, a hold on backup power, cooling recovery and a clean return to utility. For liquid-cooled pods it also watches coolant supply and leaks. The owner agrees load steps, temperature limits and failure criteria before test day (APX-commissioning §2).

Make proof independent and assign failure costs

If the builder checks its own work, who tells the owner that a failure matters? The builder and equipment makers perform tests, but the owner's agent must be free to document defects even when they delay opening. That is why the witness has a different contract.

Takeaway: An optional second check is the Uptime Institute's Tier Certification of Constructed Facility (TCCF). After integrated testing, its consultants inspect the built plant and witness operators repeat key failure and maintenance demonstrations. This tests the Tier III claim, not a particular annual uptime percentage (APX-commissioning §5; APX-commissioning §6).

Who pays after a failed test

Our owner procures certain generators and switchgear as owner-furnished equipment (OFE), while the design-builder installs them. If a test fails, record whether the installation, supplied equipment, script or utility restriction caused it. The contracts determine who pays for re-tests, idle load banks and delays; a manufacturer's warranty on early-delivered gear may already be running. Tenant losses can exceed the builder's agreed delay compensation, leaving the owner with the gap (APX-commissioning §4).

Why the two checks differ

Commissioning works through findings against this owner's requirements and test plan. Uptime certification is an optional, separate verdict against its Tier topology standard, based on the installed facility and live demonstrations rather than acceptance of the commissioning report. A site can be designed Tier III class without purchasing that certificate (APX-commissioning §6).

Turn test results into an operable handover

Substantial completion is when the first hall is contractually usable, not when every small defect is gone. Make required integrated tests and safe operation explicit conditions. Price allowed remaining work on a punch list; final completion follows when that work and close-out are done.

Takeaway: Retainage is money withheld from construction progress payments, often 5–10%. The contract controls its release and the start of the workmanship warranty; equipment bought early may already be on its manufacturer's warranty. Acceptance cannot replace a usable set of records (APX-commissioning §7).

Operations and maintenance (O&M) manuals explain the plant; corrected record drawings show what was built. The commissioning (Cx) report records the evidence, and the building management system (BMS) point list maps each sensor or alarm to a screen. The crew also needs breaker settings, training sign-offs, critical spares and keys.

Takeaway: The day-one operator should have witnessed the system and integrated tests, not received documents cold. A missing point list or outdated drawing can turn the first fault into a blind search. Have the operator check the table before signing the handover (APX-commissioning §7).

Make performance checks contract-specific

A design PUE of 1.4 can be checked at agreed loads and conditions; it is not automatically a year's measured result. The contract must say which performance checks gate substantial completion, when delay compensation ends and how remaining retainage is released. An equipment maker's warranty and the builder's workmanship warranty can start on different dates (APX-commissioning §7).

Plan every live change and emergency response

Once tenants arrive, routine maintenance can cause the outage the design was meant to prevent. The Tier III hall has redundant A and B power paths: if A is taken out for service, B must remain healthy and untouched. Routine rounds follow a standard operating procedure (SOP), planned changes a method of procedure (MOP) with checks and rollback, and unexpected faults an emergency operating procedure (EOP). Maintenance bypass is a route around a UPS block that permits service but can temporarily reduce protection.

Takeaway: A planned bypass is not general permission to work on both paths. One technician reads each step while another acts, and the network operations center (NOC), the team monitoring alarms, watches the power trace. The example electrical procedure MOP-EL-014 freezes B-side work until A is back to normal.

Takeaway: The tag on B is as important as the hand on A's breaker. Uptime's 2025 analysis found nearly 40% of organizations had a major human-error outage in three years; 85% of those incidents involved ignored or flawed procedures. Stop and roll back if the observed result does not match the step (APX-commissioning §8).

What follows an alarm or a failed check

The example MOP requires approval, a healthy alternate supply, a live bypass source, two-person checks and a rollback point. A change advisory board (CAB) approves changes to critical-system procedures. After an incident, a review establishes its cause, procedures are corrected and any tenant credit is recorded in the monthly report (APX-commissioning §8).

Maintain the site and staff every shift

Commissioning proves the new hall only once. Generators, batteries, electrical gear and cooling loops must be maintained as they age, yet every service action can also create a fault. Preventive maintenance means planned inspections and work before failure; safe isolation and a checked procedure protect the live load.

Takeaway: The staffing plan must cover every hour of every day, including holidays. Remote hands are on-site technicians who handle tenants' physical requests. Phase 1 needs 25–32 people at opening, with four or five shift crews for continuous coverage, growing to about 50–58 people at 40 MW (APX-commissioning §10).

Takeaway: At full 40 MW, annual labor is about $6–9 million within roughly $65 million of operating costs, including about $34 million for electricity. A service-level agreement (SLA) promises specified tenant power availability and environmental limits, often with credits when breached. A 99.999% availability target allows about 5.3 minutes of annual unavailability, but Tier III describes maintainability, not a guaranteed uptime percentage (APX-commissioning §10).

Maintenance rules and staffing constraints

Where adopted, the National Fire Protection Association (NFPA) 110 emergency-power standard includes weekly inspection and a monthly generator exercise with specified duration and load or exhaust-temperature conditions. The applicable edition, supplemental tests and local diesel air permit must be reconciled; electrical gear also has maintenance requirements. Stock long-lead critical spares or contract for their availability. Four or five trained shift crews are hard to sustain when operators face a staffing shortage (APX-commissioning §9; APX-commissioning §10).

Retire a tenant's devices without losing custody

A 50-rack tenant is leaving while its neighbor remains live. Before anything is unplugged, agree who may touch the tenant's devices and inventory their serial numbers. Information technology asset disposition (ITAD) is secure retirement for resale or recycling; chain of custody is the signed record of each hand-off.

Takeaway: A magnetic hard disk drive (HDD) can be cleared by one verified overwrite under the National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) 2025 guidance. A solid-state drive (SSD) stores data in flash; overwriting it can miss hidden cells. Purge is a stronger erase intended for reuse: cryptographic erase, if the whole drive was encrypted, destroys its key. When erasure cannot be demonstrated, destroy the media.

Takeaway: An erase command is only part of the proof. The tenant, operator and disposition vendor must reconcile every serial against the custody log and final certificates. A missed scan or undisclosed subcontractor breaks that link.

Takeaway: Sanitize or destroy media before unsupervised trades enter the space. The neighboring cage remains powered throughout removal. The move-out ends when records are delivered and the space is restored under the lease (APX-commissioning §11).

The drive and the building have different retirement rules

The September 2025 Revision 2 of NIST publication 800-88 distinguishes checking that a process ran from validating that it achieved the required sanitization outcome. Device-specific commands must be suitable for the drive; degaussing does not erase flash. The tenant agreement must assign custody and require serialized records, including downstream processors. Later facility work also requires proper handling of refrigerants, fuel, batteries and coolant (APX-commissioning §11).

Test the next phase beside a live hall

Phase 2 is not another empty campus: Hall 1 now serves paying tenants while the new hall may share upstream power and cooling. A failure rehearsal that was safe before opening could now interrupt customers. Operators must approve isolation points and a procedure before a tie-in or test that affects shared equipment.

Takeaway: Hall 1 also becomes the best source of improvements for Hall 2. A failed transfer, dirty filter or alarm shown on the wrong screen is evidence that a drawing, procedure or purchase specification should change. The next phase should inherit the correction, not the defect.

Takeaway: The cycle continues through phases 3 and 4. A denser tenant may later prompt liquid-cooling work beside occupied racks, which needs the same controlled boundaries. Proof at handover becomes useful only when its findings change how the live campus is built and run (APX-commissioning §12).

When a new hall shares power and cooling infrastructure with an occupied hall, its test boundary must protect the customers already inside.

Takeaway: The approved breakers and valve isolate Hall 2's test branches without interrupting Hall 1. The first empty phase could simulate 10 MW without tenants, but the new hall's test load must stay within protected capacity while Hall 1 remains in service.

Working near live tenants

Plan temporary barriers, dust control and rollback for any shared-system tie-in. Where technically suitable, flame-free pipe joining avoids introducing welding beside live racks. New-phase failure tests must respect the capacity and isolation limits of equipment already serving Hall 1 (APX-commissioning §12).

What are the first hall's IT test load and the campus's design power at the utility fence? Which rack group supplies most IT load?

About 10 MW in Phase 1, about 56 MW at the campus fence, and the 200 racks at 120 kW carry 24 of the 38.9 MW rack mix.

What do Levels 1 and 5 prove, and how long do the on-site tests for Phase 1 take?

Level 1 tests major units at the factory; Level 5 tests the whole hall through faults at design load. The on-site window is about 12–16 weeks.

When the utility feed opens in the planned test, what immediately protects the critical bus, and which two recorded conditions must stay in range?

UPS batteries protect the bus until generators take over; bus voltage must stay continuous and room temperature must remain inside the agreed band.

Who supplies independent test evidence, and which completion gate allows tenant use before final construction payment?

The owner-contracted commissioning agent supplies the independent evidence; substantial completion allows tenant use before final completion and final payment.

Which procedure handles a planned UPS bypass, which handles a sudden coolant leak, and what happens to work on the B power path?

A MOP handles the bypass and an EOP handles the leak; B stays healthy and work on it is frozen.

For a flash drive, which reusable methods replace an unreliable overwrite, and what closes the 50-rack move-out?

Verified cryptographic erase or device sanitize; serials are reconciled to certificates and downstream records, then the space is restored.
