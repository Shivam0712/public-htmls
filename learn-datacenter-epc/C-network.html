What are north–south and east–west data-center traffic, and why have modern workloads made east–west traffic more important?

North–south traffic runs between clients and servers; east–west traffic runs between servers. Why does a legacy access–aggregation–core network handle cross-rack traffic inefficiently, and how does Spanning Tree affect its redundant links?

A legacy three-tier network was built mainly for traffic between clients and servers. Traffic between servers in different racks must climb from access to aggregation or core and back down, adding hops, latency, and risk of buffering or dropped packets. Spanning Tree prevents loops by blocking redundant links, so available connections can sit idle rather than carry traffic.

Card 3 of 97concept

How does a spine-leaf Clos connect leaves and spines to provide predictable cross-leaf paths, and what does adding leaves versus spines accomplish?

In a spine-leaf Clos, every leaf switch connects to every spine switch. Traffic between servers on different leaves follows a predictable two-hop switch path: leaf → spine → leaf. Multiple spines provide parallel paths rather than a single bottleneck. Adding leaves increases the number of server ports; adding spines increases the bandwidth available between leaves.

Card 4 of 97mechanism

What is equal-cost multipath (ECMP), and how does it let a Clos fabric use multiple paths without blocking redundant links?

Equal-cost multipath (ECMP) spreads traffic across routes of equal length. In a spine-leaf Clos, every leaf connects to every spine, so traffic between two leaves can travel through different spines. ECMP assigns flows across those available paths, using all the uplinks rather than blocking redundant links as Spanning Tree did in legacy networks.

Card 5 of 97foundation

What is an all-reduce collective in distributed AI training, and why can its slowest network path delay the entire operation?

An all-reduce collective combines results from many GPUs—such as summing training gradients—so the participating GPUs can use the combined result. It is synchronized: thousands of GPUs may exchange data at once, and the operation cannot finish until the needed contributions arrive. A congested or under-provisioned network path can therefore hold up the whole group, not just the GPUs using that path.

Card 6 of 97concept

What does leaf-switch oversubscription measure, and why can bursty web traffic tolerate a different ratio from AI training traffic?

Leaf-switch oversubscription is the ratio of server-facing bandwidth to uplink bandwidth toward the spines. A 3:1 ratio gives servers three times the bandwidth available upstream; bursty web traffic can tolerate that shared capacity. AI training instead uses synchronized GPU operations, such as all-reduce, that can stall on the slowest link. Its back-end fabric therefore typically uses 1:1, or non-blocking, bandwidth.

Card 12 of 97concept

How do top-of-rack and end- or middle-of-row switch placement differ in cable length, stranded ports, switch count, and failure domain?

Top-of-rack (ToR) puts a leaf switch in every rack: short in-rack server cables, but more switches and potentially unused (“stranded”) ports in sparsely populated racks. A switch failure affects one rack. End- or middle-of-row (EoR/MoR) consolidates switches and ports, but requires longer, denser cables from servers to the shared switch; its failure can affect a larger part of the row.

Card 13 of 97mechanism

Why does the report favor a Layer-3-to-the-rack eBGP underlay over a large Layer-2 domain for a Clos fabric?

The report favors routing at each top-of-rack switch rather than extending one large Layer-2 domain across the fabric. RFC 7938 describes eBGP (external Border Gateway Protocol) as the single routing protocol for a large Clos underlay. This approach is chosen for scalability and stability and limits the reach of failures—the “blast radius”—compared with a large shared Layer-2 domain.
