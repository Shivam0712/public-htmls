Appendix: Foundations, Delivery Models and Project Economics

Sources and fine detail behind the chapter. Sections are numbered so the chapter can point at them.

The detail behind the Topic 1 chapter: definitions, market numbers, lifecycle durations, contract mechanics, cost benchmarks and the full arithmetic of the reference facility's cost model. The chapter cites sections by number (APX-foundations §n). Non-obvious claims carry their source in parentheses; where sources disagree, the text says which to lean on and why. All dollar figures are indicative 2025–26 benchmarks.

Facility types and definitions

The industry sorts data centers by who owns the building versus the servers inside it, and by scale. Definitions drift between sources; this is the course's working taxonomy.

Enterprise fell from 56% of global capacity in 2018 to about 32% at end-2025 (19% forecast for 2031); about 60% of hyperscale capacity is own-built, 40% leased; the neocloud market is forecast to approach $400B by 2031 (Synergy Research Group, Q4 2025). Powered land and shell is roughly 15–20% of finished cost (trade sources, unattributed in the dump). Modular or prefabricated build is a method, not a type; its claimed 20–30% capex saving is a modelled estimate. Boundaries blur: hyperscalers lease wholesale to move faster, developers build single-tenant "build-to-suit" campuses, and master-planned campuses target 100–500 MW (datacenters.com, 2025). "AI factory" is arguably a workload rather than an ownership model; the course keeps it because its density changes the cost structure (§9).

Where the electricity goes, and PUE

Essentially all energy drawn by IT leaves as heat that must be removed continuously, so the cooling plant is sized by the same number as the compute. In a typical facility IT takes 60–65% of total power and cooling 30–40%; a teardown of a 100 MW facility at PUE 1.54 gave about 65% to IT, around 4 MW to UPS and distribution losses, around 4 MW to lighting, security, BMS and fire, and the rest to cooling (DC Atlas, 2025). Respondent average PUE was 1.54–1.56 (Uptime Institute, Global Data Center Survey, 2024 and 2025); Google reports a 2025 fleet-wide PUE of 1.09, "83% less overhead energy than the industry average" (Google, 2025 Environmental Report).

PUE, defined once: total facility power divided by IT power. 1.0 is the ideal; 1.5 means 50% overhead. Topic 7 owns efficiency metrics.

Units. IT load is the power drawn by servers, storage and network, and how size is quoted; the reference facility is "40 MW IT". Facility load adds cooling, losses and ancillary: at PUE 1.4, 40 MW IT draws about 56 MW. Critical load is the portion on protected, redundant (UPS and generator-backed) power. Capacity, cost ($/MW), leases ($/kW) and interconnection are all denominated in power, not floor area. A rack unit is 1.75 in of device height; a standard rack has about 42U. kW per rack is the density measure: traditional colo ran 3–10 kW, AI runs 20–130 kW and forces liquid cooling. White space is the IT floor, gray space the support area (UPS, switchgear, generators, cooling plant), roughly 1:1 and rising with redundancy and density.

Market context 2025–26

US data centers used 176 TWh in 2023, 4.4% of US electricity, up from 58 TWh in 2014; 2028 projections run 325–580 TWh (6.7–12.0%); compound growth rose from about 7% (2014–18) to about 18% (2018–23), chiefly from accelerated servers (LBNL/DOE, 2024 United States Data Center Energy Usage Report, December 2024). Globally about 415 TWh in 2024, roughly 1.5% of world electricity (IEA, 2024).

Hyperscale operators ran 1,360 large data centers at end-Q4 2025 and "now account for 48% of the worldwide capacity of all data centers"; Amazon, Microsoft and Google hold 58% of hyperscale capacity; the US holds about 55%. With enterprise at about 32%, other colocation is the remaining roughly 20% (Synergy Research Group, Q4 2025).

Primary-market vacancy "fell to a record low 1.4% at year-end" 2025 with record net absorption of 2,497.6 MW, Northern Virginia leading at 1,102.0 MW; Dallas-Fort Worth passed 1 GW at 2.4% vacancy with about 700 MW under construction 94.5% preleased (CBRE Research, North America Data Center Trends H2 2025). "74.3% of all under-construction capacity already committed" (CBRE, H1 2025, 8 September 2025); "77% of the construction pipeline pre-committed to tenants" (JLL, 2026 Global Data Center Outlook, January 2026). The global pipeline is about $2.3 trillion, $492 billion under construction (Cushman & Wakefield, 2026 Development Cost Guide). Community opposition has reportedly blocked or delayed $64B-plus of US projects since 2023 (trade reporting, 2026; unverified), pushing developers toward power-advantaged metros such as Dallas and Phoenix.

Lifecycle stage durations

A 40 MW-class facility runs three to six years from decision to operation, with the "power clock" dominating. Durations are indicative and overlap.

StageTypical durationWhat gets locked

Site and power acquisition6–24+ months; interconnection often 4+ yearsLand, entitlement, secured MW

ProcurementLong-lead items ordered 12–36 months aheadVendor slots, specification
Construction12–24 months; modular 6–12Everything physical
Commissioning2–6 months, overlapping turnoverProof against the OPR
Operations and refresh15–20+ years; IT refreshed every 3–5 yearsOperating cost
DecommissioningProject-specificAsset retirement

What stretched. Interconnection routinely exceeds four years in primary markets; PJM projects averaged over three years to an interconnection service agreement and about four more to energise. Substation transformer lead times ran about 140 weeks in 2023, 150 in 2025 and over 160 in 2026; generators, switchgear and chillers quote 12–24+ months (Data Center Knowledge, 2026). Despite developers preordering up to 24 months ahead, more than half of 2025 projects were delayed three months or more, and the average global equipment lead time is 33 weeks, 50% above pre-2020 (JLL, 2026 Global Data Center Outlook). Neither the utility nor the transformer factory answers to a contractor, so these two items set the critical path. What compressed: the build itself, via prefabrication, repeatable reference designs and parallel work.

The influence curve. Influence is highest at concept and collapses as the project proceeds while the cost of change rises. Secured power, N+1 versus 2N, density and cooling approach, and the one-line are cheap on paper and very expensive later. Of 97 operators surveyed in 2024, 54% attributed their most recent significant outage primarily to power; power caused 45% of impactful outages in 2025; 54% said that outage cost over $100,000 and one in five over $1 million (Uptime Institute, Annual Outage Analysis, 2024 and 2025). Trade reporting puts AI-readiness retrofits at $10–50 million (2026; unattributed in the dump, illustrative only).

Parties and roles

  • Owner or developer: commissions, finances and usually owns the facility; sets requirements and carries ultimate risk.
  • Owner's representative: the owner's project-management proxy for schedule, budget (construction, soft costs, OFE, contingency) and stakeholders.
  • Owner's engineer: technical adviser who reviews designs against the owner's standards.
  • Architect: building design, code, envelope; under AIA forms also certifies payment and interprets the contract (AIA, A201).
  • MEP engineers: mechanical, electrical and plumbing design; the heart of the project because power and cooling are 75–80% of construction cost (§9).
  • General contractor builds to a completed design under DBB; a design-build or EPC contractor carries design and construction in one contract; subcontractors do most of the work.
  • Commissioning agent (CxA): plans, witnesses and signs off testing against the Owner's Project Requirements. Its value rests on independence from the parties being checked; a CxA reporting to the GC rather than the owner tends to soften findings that threaten a milestone. A widely held industry position rather than a measured result; Topic 10 develops it.
  • Equipment OEMs (Schneider, Vertiv, Eaton, Caterpillar, Cummins and peers), increasingly contracted directly by the owner.
  • Utility: supplies grid power and executes the interconnection agreement; now often the pace-setter.

Contracting models and contract families

The models differ in how design and construction responsibility are packaged, and therefore in who carries which risk.

ModelDesign riskCost riskSchedule riskPrice premiumSpeed
Design-bid-build (DBB)Owner (warrants drawings)Contractor, fixed priceOwner (sequential, change orders)Lowest bid, high claims exposureSlowest
Design-build (DB)Design-builderDesign-builder (lump sum or GMP)Design-builder (fast-track)Slight premium, lower cost growthFast

DBB suits well-defined scope, budget certainty over speed, or public low-bid rules. DB is the workhorse of private data center delivery when speed and single-point accountability matter. EPC/turnkey buys maximum risk transfer, typically for project-financed plant. CMAR/GMP gives early contractor input plus a cost ceiling; the GMP is documented via AIA A102 and A133. IPD is rare in data centers. The hyperscaler pattern (DB or CMAR on owner-standard reference designs with owner-furnished long-lead equipment) lets the owner control specification, lock supply-chain slots early and repeat a kit of parts across sites while pushing execution risk to the contractor.

"EPC" is borrowed vocabulary. It comes from oil, gas and power generation, where the FIDIC Silver Book (Conditions of Contract for EPC/Turnkey Projects, 1999; 2nd edition 2017) formalised single-point turnkey risk transfer for privately financed plants. Data centers use "EPC" loosely, often for a design-build package covering the power and cooling plant; it rarely maps to the Silver Book because owners keep more control (owner-standard designs, OFE) than classic project-financed EPC allows.

Owner-furnished equipment. Buying transformers, switchgear and generators directly and novating them to the contractor starts the equipment clock in parallel with design and interconnection, but moves coordination, delivery, storage and integration-warranty risk back to the owner; the contract must allocate this explicitly (schedule relief and compensation for late or defective OFE). Topic 9 owns procurement.

Contract families. - AIA: the US default for private building work. A101 (owner-contractor, stipulated sum), A201 (general conditions), A141 (owner-design-builder). Liquidated damages are not pre-set in A201; the rate is drafted into A101 §4.5, triggered by §3.3.3, and A201 §15.1.7 waives consequential damages. Contingency, escalation and force majeure sit in the agreement or supplementary conditions (AIA, A101/A201/A141). - ConsensusDocs: launched 2007, endorsed by about 40 associations including AGC; more balanced than architect-centric AIA; LDs and waivers are explicit fill-ins (ConsensusDocs 200 §6.6). - DBIA: forms purpose-built for design-build. - FIDIC: Red Book (owner's design, owner carries design risk), Yellow Book (contractor designs), Silver Book (EPC/turnkey, maximum transfer, fixed price and date); used outside the US and on internationally financed projects. - NEC: UK-origin, collaborative, option-based (target-cost Option C).

Where the risk clauses sit. Liquidated damages compensate the owner for late completion, critical when a tenant lease rides on the date. Force majeure excuses defined uncontrollable events. Escalation clauses allocate equipment inflation, heavily negotiated since 2020. Contingency is explicit and often shared in a GMP; in lump-sum DBB or EPC it is buried in the price.

Evidence on delivery-method outcomes

The strongest quantitative evidence is a 20-year update of the 1998 CII benchmark of 350-plus projects (CII/Pankow, Molenaar and Franz, "Revisiting Project Delivery Performance 1998–2018", published by DBIA, 2018). Cost: design-build cost about 1.9% less per square foot than CMAR and 0.3% less than DBB, with 2.4% less cost growth than CMAR and 3.8% less than DBB. Schedule: 3.9% less schedule growth than CMAR and 1.7% less than DBB; 13% faster construction than CMAR and 36% faster than DBB; 61% faster than CMAR and 102% faster than DBB from design through completion, the concurrency advantage.

Transferability. These are cross-sector samples, not data-center-specific, and predate the AI supply-chain crunch. The direction transfers; the decimals do not. An older CII sample found DBB sometimes edged DB on schedule growth, so the literature is not unanimous, though its weight favours design-build.

Cost benchmarks by source and scope

Cost is quoted per MW of IT (or "critical") load and almost always excludes IT equipment. Scope differs: shell-and-core only is the narrowest; all-in or turnkey (shell plus full MEP fit-out) the broadest; some exclude land and powered shell (about 15–20%) or soft costs. Always ask: per MW of what, air or liquid, shell or all-in, land in or out.

Electrical plus mechanical is 75–80% of construction cost; the shell is a minority. Other trade sources frame MEP as about 50% of total budget with shell-and-core at 15–20% and electrical alone 40–45%; the difference is the denominator (total budget including land and soft costs), not a disagreement. Liquid cooling shifts spend from shell and electrical toward mechanical.

Cost drivers. Density, redundancy (2N roughly duplicates electrical gear versus N+1), climate (hot metros pay a cooling penalty but gain on power, land and tax), scarce MEP and commissioning labour, tariffs and equipment inflation (47% of respondents saw 6–15% bid-price rises in the past year, 21% over 15%; Turner & Townsend, 2025–26), and speed premiums (more crews and shifts against fixed lead times).

Operating cost composition

Facility opex is dominated by electricity, then maintenance, staffing, land or lease, insurance, taxes and water.

  • Facility cash opex about $1.0–1.5M per MW per year, electricity the largest line but "rarely above 40–45%" in temperate, well-managed sites (ITK Research, aggregating MCIM benchmarks); other models put total opex at $800–2,000 per kW per year.
  • Electricity via PUE: IT MW × 8,760 h × PUE × $/kWh. Dropping PUE from 1.5 to 1.2 cuts cooling electricity about 60% and the total bill about 20% (ITK Research).
  • Maintenance often modelled at about 3% of capex per year and insurance about 0.7% (modelling conventions in the dump; verify against an operator's accounts).
  • Staffing roughly 1.5 FTE per MW for colocation, about 0.2 for single-workload AI campuses (staffing benchmarks in trade press, 2026).
  • US data centers used about 17 billion gallons of water in 2023 (LBNL/DOE, 2024).
  • A one-point UPS efficiency gain at a 10 MW site saves about $1.4M over 10 years (Data Center Knowledge, citing Vertiv).

Total cost of ownership: method and worked example

Method. Owners model 10–20 year TCO, discounting future opex to present value: facility depreciated over 15–30 years, a major MEP refresh at mid-life, IT refreshed every 3–5 years, discount rate near the owner's WACC (commonly 8–10% for infrastructure). TCO surfaces capex-opex trade-offs: efficient UPS costs more up front and saves electricity; value-engineered cooling saves capex and raises PUE for the life of the building.

Reference facility. 1,000 racks of multi-tenant colocation on the outskirts of a hot US metro (Phoenix or Dallas archetype). Canonical density profile: 300 racks at 8 kW, 300 at 15 kW, 200 at 40 kW, 200 at 120 kW (AI pods); 2.4 + 4.5 + 8.0 + 24.0 = 39.1 MW, rounded to "40 MW IT". The AI band is 20% of racks and 61% of load. The dump's flat 40 kW average is not how a colo is built; every later chapter uses this profile. Design PUE 1.4, N+1 concurrently maintainable, industrial power about $0.07/kWh. Every number below is indicative.

Gross opex of $65M is about $1.6M/MW/yr, the upper end of ITK's band for a hot, dense site. Discounting at 8% roughly halves the weight of future opex, which is why owners model it. A liquid-cooled AI build on the same IT load would add 7–10% on the T&T basis or move to the $15–20M/MW class ($600–800M-plus). The dump's summary quotes a 15-year TCO of $1.2–1.4B, but its own arithmetic gives $1.48B undiscounted and $1.03B discounted; the course uses the arithmetic.

Delivery choice for this facility. Design-build with owner-furnished long-lead equipment on a repeatable reference design, phased in about 10 MW increments. With interconnection (4+ years) and transformers (about 3 years) binding, the owner starts the power and equipment clocks in parallel (favouring OFE) while transferring execution risk and gaining single-point responsibility (favouring design-build, per §7). CMAR/GMP is the main alternative for an open-book cost ceiling with early contractor input.

How tenants pay

Wholesale colocation is priced per kW of committed power per month; retail per rack with bundled power. Both are typically modified-gross leases with power passed through, metered or at a PUE-marked rate, so the operator's electricity is a pass-through rather than a margin risk. Powered shell is usually triple-net. Retail terms run 1–3 years, wholesale 5–15 (§1). The national average lease rate "rose by 6.5% year over year to $195.94 per kW per month, marking the fourth straight annual increase" for a 250–500 kW requirement (CBRE Research, North America Data Center Trends H2 2025); Dallas and Phoenix run about $120–160 per kW for larger blocks.

Revenue sanity check. At full lease, 40,000 kW × $160/kW/month = $6.4M/month, about $77M/yr; at $196/kW it approaches $94M/yr. Against $30–65M/yr opex (depending on pass-through), the spread services the $450M capex: the utility-like economics that draw REIT and infrastructure capital. Indicative only.
