Facility types and definitions

A data center houses computing, storage and network equipment and supplies power, cooling, connectivity and security. Ownership and sales models describe who provides the facility and what customers buy. Scale describes how large it is, workload describes what the computers do, and construction method describes how it is built. These dimensions can overlap.

Hyperscale is a scale and use category, not an alternative to wholesale ownership: a hyperscaler can lease a wholesale facility. AI factory chiefly identifies a workload and density, not a single ownership model. Modular or prefabricated build is a construction method, not a customer type. Boundaries blur further when developers build single-tenant, build-to-suit facilities and master-plan 100–500 MW campuses (datacenters.com, 2025).

Enterprise capacity fell from 56% of global capacity in 2018 to about 32% at end-2025, with 19% forecast for 2031. About 60% of hyperscale capacity is own-built and 40% leased; the neocloud market is forecast to approach $400B by 2031 (Synergy Research Group, Q4 2025). Powered land and shell is roughly 15–20% of finished cost (trade sources, unattributed in the dump).

Reference rack mix. The course's 1,000-rack, mixed retail and wholesale campus has 300 × 8 kW = 2.4 MW, 300 × 15 kW = 4.5 MW, 200 × 40 kW = 8.0 MW, and 200 × 120 kW = 24.0 MW. The sum is 38.9 MW IT, rounded to 40 MW IT for design sizing and financial examples. The 200 racks at 120 kW are liquid-cooled AI pods: 20% of racks account for about 62% of the stated rack load. The provider owns the building, the retail and wholesale tenants own their computers, and the campus needs both air and liquid cooling. The facility is planned in four roughly 10 MW halls, with shared site routes planned for all four.

Where the electricity goes, and PUE

Essentially all energy drawn by IT leaves as heat that must be removed continuously. Cooling takes power from a branch of the facility's electrical supply and removes heat from the racks; it is not a load through which all IT electricity passes. Room air carries heat from the 8 kW and 15 kW racks, while circulating liquid carries it from the 120 kW AI pods; the 40 kW group needs a deliberate cooling choice. Both routes reject heat through the site's cooling plant. The UPS protects designated critical loads, not every watt used by cooling, lighting and other facility equipment. In a typical facility IT takes 60–65% of total power and cooling 30–40%; a teardown of a 100 MW facility at PUE 1.54 gave about 65% to IT, around 4 MW to UPS and distribution losses, around 4 MW to lighting, security, BMS and fire, and the rest to cooling (DC Atlas, 2025). Respondent average PUE was 1.54–1.56 (Uptime Institute, Global Data Center Survey, 2024 and 2025); Google reports a 2025 fleet-wide PUE of 1.09, "83% less overhead energy than the industry average" (Google, 2025 Environmental Report).

PUE, defined once: total facility power divided by IT power. A PUE of 1.0 is the ideal; 1.5 means facility power is 50% above IT power. Topic 7 owns efficiency metrics.

Three distinct course conditions. At design PUE 1.4, the rounded 40 MW IT sizing case draws 56 MW facility power. The extra 16 MW covers all support uses, including cooling, power conversion and losses, lighting and other services, not cooling alone. This is the case used for the course's full-design-load electricity bill. Expected full-load operating PUE is near 1.25, while forecast phase-1 part-load metered PUE is 1.55–1.65 as shared equipment runs below its eventual capacity. Those operating figures are course estimates, not substitutions for 1.4 in the design-case bill or promises of measured performance.

Units. IT load is the power drawn by servers, storage and network equipment; the reference facility is stated as 40 MW IT after rounding its rack mix (§1). Facility load adds cooling, losses and ancillary loads. Critical load is the portion supported by protected, redundant power, typically close to IT load plus critical cooling; it does not mean every facility load passes through the UPS. Capacity, cost ($/MW), leases ($/kW) and interconnection are denominated in power, not floor area. A rack unit is 1.75 in; a standard rack has about 42U. kW per rack measures density: traditional colocation ran 3–10 kW, while AI deployments run 20–130 kW and can require liquid cooling. White space is the IT floor and gray space the support area, with a rough planning ratio of 1:1 that rises with redundancy and density.

Market context 2025–26

US data centers used 176 TWh in 2023, or 4.4% of US electricity, up from 58 TWh in 2014; 2028 projections run 325–580 TWh (6.7–12.0%). Compound growth rose from about 7% in 2014–18 to about 18% in 2018–23, chiefly from accelerated servers (LBNL/DOE, 2024 United States Data Center Energy Usage Report, December 2024). Globally, data centers used about 415 TWh in 2024, roughly 1.5% of world electricity (IEA, 2024).

Hyperscale operators ran 1,360 large data centers at end-Q4 2025 and "now account for 48% of the worldwide capacity of all data centers"; Amazon, Microsoft and Google hold 58% of hyperscale capacity, and the US holds about 55%. Enterprise accounted for about 32% of worldwide capacity at end-2025 (Synergy Research Group, Q4 2025). These are measures of use and capacity, not a partition into mutually exclusive hyperscale and colocation ownership: hyperscalers use both their own buildings and leased wholesale colocation (§1).

In North American primary markets, vacancy "fell to a record low 1.4% at year-end" 2025, with record net absorption of 2,497.6 MW; Northern Virginia led at 1,102.0 MW (CBRE Research, North America Data Center Trends H2 2025). CBRE reported "74.3% of all under-construction capacity already committed" in its H1 2025 North American data-center report (CBRE, H1 2025, 8 September 2025). JLL separately reported "77% of the construction pipeline pre-committed to tenants" (JLL, 2026 Global Data Center Outlook, January 2026). Committed or preleased capacity does not establish grid availability: a signed lease is a demand signal, and the utility connection still governs when a hall can open. The global pipeline is about $2.3 trillion, with $492 billion under construction (Cushman & Wakefield, 2026 Development Cost Guide). Community opposition has reportedly blocked or delayed more than $64B of US projects since 2023 (trade reporting, 2026; unverified), pushing developers toward power-advantaged metros such as Dallas and Phoenix.

Lifecycle stage durations

A 40 MW-class facility commonly takes three to six years from decision to operation, with the power clock often dominating. The durations below describe overlapping workstreams, not stages to add end to end. Site and grid work, design, procurement and construction can all be active at once. The four roughly 10 MW phases are not equal-cost builds: the first carries shared site, shell and utility work that later halls reuse.

Baseline and sensitivity. The 128-week transformer lead time is the course-selected planning assumption, counted from an order around month 8, with target arrival around months 35–38. The report separately quotes over 160 weeks in 2026 as a longer-lead market scenario, not the course baseline. At roughly 160 weeks after month 8, delivery approaches month 45 (Data Center Knowledge, 2026). The course considers a gas-power bridge only conditionally if permanent grid power is late; it is not a secured substitute for interconnection. Behind-the-meter gas power is an option under discussion in the report, not an assured opening date.

What stretched. Interconnection routinely exceeds four years in primary markets; PJM projects averaged over three years to an interconnection service agreement and about four more to energise. Reported substation transformer lead times ran about 140 weeks in 2023, 150 in 2025 and over 160 in 2026; generators, switchgear and chillers commonly quote 12–24+ months (Data Center Knowledge, 2026). Despite preordering up to 24 months ahead, more than half of 2025 projects were delayed three months or more; average global equipment lead time was 33 weeks, 50% above pre-2020 (JLL, 2026 Global Data Center Outlook). The utility and equipment manufacturers set clocks that a builder cannot compress by accelerating site work. What compressed: builder-controlled work through prefabrication, repeatable designs and parallel construction.

The influence curve. Influence is highest at concept and falls as the cost of change rises. Secured power, N+1 versus 2N, density, cooling and the electrical one-line are cheaper to change on paper than after installation. Of 97 operators surveyed in 2024, 54% attributed their most recent significant outage primarily to power; power caused 45% of impactful outages in 2025. In the reported outage-cost findings, 54% said their most recent significant outage cost over $100,000 and one in five over $1 million (Uptime Institute, Annual Outage Analysis, 2024 and 2025).

Parties and roles

  • Owner or developer: commissions, finances and usually owns the facility; sets requirements, secures the power strategy and carries ultimate project risk.
  • Owner's representative: the owner's project-management proxy for schedule, budget and stakeholders. Owner's engineer: technical adviser who reviews design against the owner's standards.
  • Architect: building design, code and envelope; under AIA forms also certifies payment and interprets the contract (AIA, A201).
  • MEP engineers: mechanical, electrical and plumbing design. Power and cooling dominate the published construction-cost allocations (§9).
  • General contractor: builds to a completed design under DBB. A design-build or EPC contractor carries its agreed design and construction scope in one contract; subcontractors perform most trade work.
  • Commissioning agent (CxA): plans, witnesses and records testing against the Owner's Project Requirements. The CxA is independent of the parties whose work it checks and reports findings directly to the owner. A CxA reporting through the GC risks softening findings that threaten a milestone; this is a widely held industry position, not a measured result. Topic 10 develops commissioning.
  • Equipment OEMs: manufacturers including Schneider, Vertiv, Eaton, Caterpillar, Cummins and peers, sometimes contracted directly by the owner.
  • Utility: an independent, schedule-setting party that supplies grid power and executes the interconnection agreement. Neither the owner's builder nor its commissioning agent controls utility timing.
  • Facility operator: takes responsibility for running the site after testing and operating handover. Operators should be engaged before handover so they can witness commissioning and accept the hall with knowledge of its systems. Construction completion produces installed systems; commissioning demonstrates their operation against requirements before the operator accepts them. A test-ready construction handover and an operating handover are not the same milestone.

The models package design and construction differently. The equipment buyer and exclusions must be stated separately in each agreement; neither a model name nor a fixed price allocates the utility's schedule.

DBB suits defined scope, budget certainty over speed, or public low-bid rules. DB is widely described as the workhorse of private data-center delivery (research dump synthesis; no single source). EPC prices broad contractor responsibility, typically for a project-financed plant, but turnkey does not by itself transfer an interconnection date or delay to owner-furnished equipment. CMAR/GMP gives early contractor input and a cost ceiling; US GMP arrangements use AIA A102 and A133. A guaranteed maximum price is a cost ceiling for agreed scope, not an unconditional completion-date guarantee. IPD is rare in data centers. An owner-standard DB or CMAR program lets an owner control specification, book supply slots early and repeat a kit of parts while the builder carries its agreed execution risk.

"EPC" is borrowed vocabulary. It comes from oil, gas and power generation, where the FIDIC Silver Book (Conditions of Contract for EPC/Turnkey Projects, 1999; 2nd edition 2017) formalised single-point turnkey risk transfer for privately financed plants. Data centers use "EPC" loosely, sometimes for a power-and-cooling package rather than the whole site; owner-standard designs and OFE leave more with the owner than classic project-financed EPC.

Owner-furnished equipment. Direct owner orders for transformers, switchgear and generators can start procurement alongside design and interconnection. An OFE schedule must identify the buyer and promised delivery date; who receives and inspects the gear, who stores and protects it, who documents and remedies defects, and who installs, tests and integrates it. It must also allocate integration and warranty interfaces, contractor schedule relief and compensation when late or defective OFE affects its work, and the evidence needed for a claim. Assignment or novation to the contractor does not silently erase the owner's supply obligations. Topic 9 owns procurement.

Contract families. - AIA: the US default for private building work. A101 (owner-contractor, stipulated sum), A201 (general conditions) and A141 (owner-design-builder). Liquidated damages are not pre-set in A201; the rate is drafted into A101 §4.5, triggered by §3.3.3, and A201 §15.1.7 waives consequential damages. Contingency, escalation and force majeure sit in the agreement or supplementary conditions (AIA, A101/A201/A141). - ConsensusDocs: launched in 2007 and endorsed by about 40 associations including AGC; a more balanced alternative to architect-centric AIA, with explicit LD and waiver fill-ins (ConsensusDocs 200 §6.6). - DBIA: forms purpose-built for design-build. - FIDIC: Red Book for owner's design, Yellow Book for contractor design, and Silver Book for fixed-price, fixed-date EPC/turnkey risk transfer; used outside the US and on internationally financed work. - NEC: UK-origin, collaborative and option-based, including target-cost Option C.

Where risk clauses sit. Liquidated damages compensate the owner for defined late completion, especially when a tenant lease depends on it. Force majeure excuses defined uncontrollable events. Escalation clauses allocate equipment inflation, heavily negotiated since 2020. Contingency is explicit and often shared in a GMP; in lump-sum DBB or EPC it is usually buried in the price. Each clause still operates within the contract's scope, exclusions and relief provisions.

Evidence on delivery-method outcomes

Historical quantitative evidence comes from a 20-year update of the 1998 CII benchmark of more than 350 projects (CII/Pankow, Molenaar and Franz, "Revisiting Project Delivery Performance 1998–2018", published by DBIA, 2018). In that cross-sector sample, design-build cost about 1.9% less per square foot than CMAR and 0.3% less than DBB, with 2.4% less cost growth than CMAR and 3.8% less than DBB. It had 3.9% less schedule growth than CMAR and 1.7% less than DBB; construction was 13% faster than CMAR and 36% faster than DBB, while design through completion was 61% faster than CMAR and 102% faster than DBB. The longer comparison captures concurrency between design and construction.

Transferability. These are historical building and infrastructure samples, not data-center-specific outcomes, and they predate the AI supply-chain crunch. Treat their direction as evidence that concurrency can shorten builder-controlled design and construction work, not their percentages as a forecast for this campus. An older CII sample found DBB sometimes edged DB on schedule growth, so the literature is not unanimous. No delivery method guarantees a utility interconnection date or delivery of owner-procured equipment. Even a builder that finishes early can hand over a hall that waits for grid power or a missing transformer (§4 and §6).

Cost drivers. Density, redundancy (2N roughly duplicates electrical gear versus N+1), climate (hot metros pay a cooling penalty but can gain on power, land and tax), scarce MEP and commissioning labour, tariffs and equipment inflation (47% of respondents saw 6–15% bid-price rises in the past year and 21% over 15%; Turner & Townsend, 2025–26), and premiums for compressed builder schedules.

Operating cost composition

Facility gross opex includes electricity, maintenance, staffing, insurance, tax, water and other running costs. Gross means before any tenant reimbursement.

  • Facility cash opex is benchmarked around $1.0–1.5M per MW per year, with electricity the largest line but "rarely above 40–45%" in temperate, well-managed sites (ITK Research, aggregating MCIM benchmarks); other models put total opex at $800–2,000 per kW per year. The reference hot-climate design-case bill puts electricity at about 53% of its roughly $65M gross total. Do not apply that design-case share as a measured phase-1 operating ratio or assume all electricity is reimbursed.
  • Electricity via PUE: IT MW × 8,760 hours × PUE × $/kWh, with unit conversion as needed. At 40 MW IT, design PUE 1.4 and $0.07/kWh, the course models 56,000 kW × 8,760 × $0.07 = $34.339M, rounded to $34.3M a year. Use $0.07/kWh for every course bill and ownership-cost calculation. A separate $0.10/kWh loss-cost convention, if used to value an avoided outage, is not this electricity tariff. Dropping PUE from 1.5 to 1.2 cuts cooling-attributable electricity about 60% and the total electricity bill about 20% in the cited illustration (ITK Research).
  • Maintenance is often modelled at about 3% of capex a year and insurance at about 0.7% (modelling conventions in the dump; verify against an operator's accounts). At the course's $450M midpoint, those lines round to $13.5M and $3.2M.
  • The report's rough colocation staffing benchmark is 1.5 FTE/MW and its 40 MW calculation gives 60 people and $7.8M at about $130,000 loaded each. This is the report's ceiling, not the central course staffing plan. The course uses 50–58 people at 40 MW, centrally about 55 people and $7.2M. The separate trade benchmark for a single-workload AI campus is about 0.2 FTE/MW (staffing benchmarks in trade press, 2026).
  • The course's illustrative $6.8M property-tax, water, connectivity, G&A and other allowance is not a bid. Together, $34.3M electricity + $13.5M maintenance + $3.2M insurance + $7.2M staffing + $6.8M other = about $65M gross per year; non-power opex is about $30.7M. US data centers used about 17 billion gallons of water in 2023 (LBNL/DOE, 2024).

The course's expected full-load operating PUE near 1.25 and forecast phase-1 part-load metered PUE of 1.55–1.65 describe different operating conditions (§2). Neither is the PUE used to calculate this full-design-load bill.

Total cost of ownership: method and worked example

Method. Owners model 10–20 year total cost of ownership (TCO), discounting future opex to present value. Common modelling assumptions include facility depreciation over 15–30 years, a major mid-life MEP refresh, IT refresh every 3–5 years, and a discount rate near the owner's WACC, often 8–10%. TCO exposes capex-opex trade-offs: a one-point UPS efficiency gain at a 10 MW site saves about $1.4M over 10 years (Data Center Knowledge, citing Vertiv), while cheaper cooling can reduce capex but raise lifetime electricity expense.

Reference facility and common assumptions. The course models a 1,000-rack, mixed retail and wholesale colocation campus outside a hot US metro. The rack groups are 300 at 8 kW, 300 at 15 kW, 200 at 40 kW and 200 liquid-cooled AI-pod racks at 120 kW. Their powers sum to 2.4 + 4.5 + 8.0 + 24.0 = 38.9 MW, rounded to 40 MW IT for every financial example (§1). The course holds mixed-cooling campus capex at $400–500M, uses the $450M midpoint, design PUE 1.4, N+1 concurrently maintainable systems, $0.07/kWh for electricity, a rounded $65M gross annual opex, one $54M MEP replacement modelled around year 12, and 15 years of operation. All three TCO totals below use those same assumptions and exclude tenant computers.

Interpretation and limits. The undiscounted total is $450M + $975M + $54M = $1.479B. The 8% present-value calculation discounts the same level annual gross expenses and the later replacement; it is not a second operating forecast. The $0.96B figure subtracts power reimbursement receipts from owner-borne cost, rather than claiming that gross electricity expense disappears. Full 40 MW load from year one and complete power reimbursement are simplifying assumptions, not a phased occupancy forecast or guaranteed lease terms. The report's summary quotes $1.2–1.4B, but its detailed undiscounted arithmetic is about $1.48B; the course uses the shown arithmetic.

Delivery choice. The course uses design-build with owner-furnished long-lead equipment on a repeatable reference design, opening four roughly 10 MW halls in phases. Power acquisition, the course's 128-week transformer procurement baseline and builder-controlled execution run on overlapping clocks (§4). CMAR/GMP is the main alternative for an open-book construction ceiling with early contractor input. Neither model promises the utility's date or cures late owner gear (§6–7).

How tenants pay

Wholesale colocation commonly charges base rent per kW of committed capacity per month under longer leases; that capacity charge is not itself a metered electricity bill. Electricity may be separately metered, charged at a contracted PUE-marked rate or otherwise allocated under the lease. Retail colocation commonly charges per rack, sometimes with power bundled, and its terms can differ. Powered shell is usually triple-net. Retail terms commonly run 1–3 years and wholesale 5–15 (§1). Electricity is often passed through, but not necessarily fully reimbursed; the contract determines the operator's remaining power exposure.

The national average lease rate "rose by 6.5% year over year to $195.94 per kW per month, marking the fourth straight annual increase" for a 250–500 kW requirement (CBRE Research, North America Data Center Trends H2 2025); Dallas and Phoenix run about $120–160 per kW for larger blocks. These observed and indicative rates do not establish this campus's lease terms.

Revenue sanity check. At hypothetical full lease, 40,000 kW × $160/kW/month = $6.4M/month or $76.8M/year in capacity rent; at $196/kW/month it approaches $94M/year. Treat those as base-rent illustrations only if electricity is charged extra. Comparing rent alone to the course's approximately $65M gross annual expense omits any separately contracted power receipts; comparing rent to the approximately $30.7M non-power expense assumes the corresponding $34.3M power bill is fully reimbursed. A consistent revenue comparison includes base rent, actual electricity reimbursements and the corresponding gross electricity expense. The $0.96B owner-borne TCO in §11 requires complete reimbursement of all modelled electricity; it is not the default for every wholesale or retail agreement.
