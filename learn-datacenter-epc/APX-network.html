From three tiers to Clos, and what non-blocking costs

Why the industry moved. The three-tier design (access, aggregation, core) was built for north-south traffic and leaned on Spanning Tree Protocol to break loops, which blocked redundant links and left half the fabric idle. Modern workloads, above all AI training, are overwhelmingly east-west, and in a tree two servers in different racks climb to the aggregation or core and back, adding hops, latency and drops. The spine-leaf or folded-Clos topology (Charles Clos, 1953) flattens this: every leaf connects to every spine, and traffic between servers on different leaves crosses two switch-to-switch links, leaf to spine and spine to leaf. Servers on the same leaf need no spine. ECMP balances traffic across available uplinks without blocking a redundant link. Scaling adds leaves for ports and, subject to the topology, spine capacity for bandwidth (research synthesis).

Oversubscription. This is the ratio of server-facing to spine-facing bandwidth on a leaf. Legacy enterprise fabrics ran 3:1 or worse; AI back-end fabrics favor 1:1, non-blocking, because collectives such as all-reduce synchronize thousands of GPUs at once and stall on the slowest link. On a radix-64 leaf with equal-speed ports, a 1:1 split gives 32 server ports and 32 uplinks; 3:1 gives 48 and 16. For a fixed 96 server ports, the illustrated 3:1 allocation needs two leaves and 32 leaf uplinks; the 1:1 allocation needs three leaves and 96 leaf uplinks. Leaves rise by 50%, but leaf uplinks triple in this example. Each fiber uplink needs an optic at each end, so its inter-switch optics also triple if those uplinks are all optical. Spine count depends on spine radix, how links are distributed, redundancy and the larger fabric topology; it does not follow a general 50% rule. This port-and-optics cost helps explain why AI fabrics are expensive (§5, §6).

Tiers by scale. A two-tier Clos on radix-64 800G silicon reaches roughly 2,048 GPUs at 1:1 (IP Infusion) (verify: secondary source). Beyond that, a super-spine tier can interconnect pods and scale a three-stage Clos to 16,000+ GPUs; NVIDIA's reference architecture adds it at roughly 1,024 nodes and above. A planning rule is pod = two tiers, hall or campus = three, but actual tier count follows port and traffic requirements. Each added tier adds a hop and another layer of links and optics. Hall 1's AI pod, at 50 racks and 900 compute trays, stays under the stated node threshold (§12).

51.2T silicon and tier count. Broadcom's Tomahawk 5 (BCM78900) delivers 51.2 Tb/s on one chip as 64 × 800G, 128 × 400G or 256 × 200G; Marvell's Teralynx 10 is also 51.2T with a radix up to 512 × 100G. A 51.2T switch has four times the nominal throughput of a 12.8T switch, not twelve times. The claim that one replaces twelve 12.8T switches (650 Group) may describe a particular network arrangement, but is not a like-for-like throughput comparison (verify: topology and port assumptions). A higher-radix chip can collapse three tiers to two, with a saving of over 1 MW claimed for a large cluster (Marvell) (verify: configuration). As forecast in the 2025–26 research, the successor is 102.4T with 1.6T ports (§5) (verify: dated forecast).

Where the switch sits, and the control plane

A leaf connects servers or other endpoints; a spine connects leaves and offers alternate paths between them. Those paths preserve service after a spine failure only if the remaining links and switches have adequate capacity.

ToR versus EoR/MoR. Top-of-rack (ToR) puts a leaf in every rack: server links are short in-rack DAC and only uplinks leave, which minimises long runs but multiplies switches and strands ports in light racks; the failure domain is one rack. End-of-row (EoR) or middle-of-row (MoR) consolidates one larger switch or pair per row with every server cabled to it, cutting switches and stranded ports but demanding long, dense runs and a bigger failure domain. For AI back-ends copper reach favors a hybrid: leaves sit in an adjacent network rack because typical 800G passive copper reach is about 2 m (§5). The reference facility runs ToR in the 15 and 40 kW pods (the research's fiber arithmetic assumes it), leaves MoR open for the 8 kW retail pod, and puts the AI pod's leaves in a network rack every two GPU racks (§3, §12).

Underlay. One common large-Clos choice is external Border Gateway Protocol (eBGP). RFC 7938 (August 2016) describes an eBGP design with a unique private ASN per ToR (64512–65534), one ASN for all Tier-1 devices, single-hop sessions on point-to-point links and Layer 3 to the ToR. This limits the scope of some faults, but is a design choice, not a requirement for every operator or tenant. The 2021 Meta outage (§8) cautions that BGP withdrawals can also have far-reaching effects.

Overlay and ownership. Ethernet VPN over VXLAN (EVPN/VXLAN) is a common overlay choice on a Layer-3 underlay for multi-tenancy, Layer-2 adjacency and workload mobility. A single-tenant AI back-end can instead use a routed RoCE fabric without that overlay. The tenant chooses its in-cage switching and compute fabric; the operator's facility networks serve building systems and any retail network services it elects to provide. For this course, the operator's facility LAN and retail services use EVPN/VXLAN, while Hall 1's AI tenant back-end does not. That course choice is not an operator-wide or industry-wide mandate.

The four networks of an AI pod, and what a rail is

Four networks (NVIDIA GB300 NVL72 AI Factory reference architecture). (1) Back-end GPU compute: east-west RDMA, highest bandwidth, rail-optimized, non-blocking, ConnectX-8 SuperNICs at 800G per GPU operated as 2 × 400G dual-plane. (2) Front-end: north-south node management, storage access and user traffic, each node with a BlueField-3 DPU at 2 × 400G to two switches. (3) Storage, often merged with the front-end at small scale. (4) Out-of-band management, 1 GbE RJ45 to every node's BMC (§8). These describe the cited NVIDIA design, not every tenant installation.

Scale-up versus scale-out. Scale-up is NVLink inside the rack, where 72 GPUs form one domain at 900 GB/s per GPU (1,800 GB/s bidirectional) on copper, Topic 8's subject. Scale-out is the network between racks and pods. A collective uses scale-up first, then hands the remainder to scale-out. The boundary is the rack.

Rail. A rail groups same-index GPU NIC positions within a reachable switch group. In a small group, same-index ports can attach to the same leaf and communicate without a spine; that does not mean one copper-connected leaf reaches every rack in a large zone. Larger groups repeat leaves and use spine links between them, including for traffic that crosses leaf groups. In the cited NVL72-class dual-plane design, each GPU's 800G port breaks into 2 × 400G, one to each of two planes, each described as scaling to 1,024 × 400G interfaces. Same-index placement and physical reach must both be checked against the tenant's rail map.

Scalable unit and switch counts (verify). The report calls one GB300 NVL72 rack, with 72 GPUs, 18 trays and four ConnectX-8 SuperNICs per tray, a scalable unit (SU). It also attributes an illustrative 8 leaf + 4 spine figure per two-rack SU to the NVIDIA GB300 NVL72 AI Factory reference architecture. Those are different unit definitions, and the report's worked-example leaf and spine totals do not follow by simply multiplying 8 + 4 by its two-rack count. Before using per-unit switches as a bill of materials, confirm the exact NVIDIA reference-design revision, whether 8 + 4 covers one or two racks and one or both fabric planes, switch port speeds, oversubscription and the super-spine boundary (verify). The course instead labels its two-rack, dual-plane counts indicative, scaled from the report's 200-rack worked example (§12).

Floor-plan consequence. Copper-connected NICs must reach their local leaves, so leaves sit in or beside the GPU racks and cable lengths follow the rail map. In the reference row the pattern is GPU, GPU, network rack, repeated: passive DAC from the adjacent rack and AEC where the designed route fits its longer reach. Only the local leaf uplinks leave the group on fiber (§5).

InfiniBand, RoCEv2 and Ultra Ethernet

InfiniBand (NVIDIA Quantum). NDR is 400G per port and XDR is 800G (Quantum-X800; the Q3400 switch is 144 × 800G). Credit-based flow control is built in. SHARP performs in-network reduction, with a claimed reduction of all-reduce traffic up to 9× for applicable collectives, not a guaranteed saving for every job. About 1–2 µs latency is an indicative configuration-dependent comparison. The cited NVIDIA implementation uses a Subnet Manager and NVIDIA-specific tooling; its procurement concentration depends on the chosen system.

Ethernet with RoCEv2 (NVIDIA Spectrum-X, Broadcom Tomahawk 5 and 6). RDMA is encapsulated in UDP/IP over Ethernet; PFC plus ECN/DCQCN need tuning for the chosen workload. Spectrum-X (SN5600 with BlueField-3 or ConnectX-8) claims about 95% effective bandwidth through adaptive routing and SuperNIC packet reordering. About 2–5 µs latency is indicative, not a guaranteed difference from InfiniBand. Ethernet allows different vendor and tooling choices, although a Spectrum-X deployment can still be NVIDIA-coupled. This is the reference pod's proposed approach.

Ultra Ethernet 1.0. Released 11 June 2025: an open, multi-vendor, 562-page stack with a new Ultra Ethernet Transport (UET), packet-level multipathing and congestion control intended to address RoCEv2 limitations. Backers include AMD, Arista, Broadcom, Cisco, Intel, Meta, Microsoft, Oracle and later NVIDIA. As described in the 2025–26 research, products were expected from late 2025 into 2026 (verify: dated forecast); interoperability, latency and price depend on products and configuration. The reference pod specifies UEC-ready NICs as a course choice, not a guarantee that transport can be changed without other equipment or software changes.

The sales crossover (Dell'Oro). "When we first initiated our coverage of AI Back-end Networks in late 2023, the market was dominated by InfiniBand, holding over 80 percent share" (Sameh Boujelbene, 15 July 2025). That describes Dell'Oro's market coverage at the time, not a measured installed-base share. By late 2025 Ethernet "accounted for more than two-thirds of data center switch sales in AI back-end networks both during the quarter and across the first three quarters of the year, up from less than half in the same period last year" (5 December 2025). For full-year 2025, Ethernet "more than doubles size of InfiniBand" in the reported sales comparison (10 March 2026) (verify: 2026-dated). Thus 2025 is the crossover year in AI back-end switch sales, driven by hyperscalers and neoclouds, not proof that most installed switches were Ethernet. Broadcom's Hock Tan said AI networking had reached about 40% of AI semiconductor revenue, "almost a situation where stars are aligned," and guided toward 30% as XPUs ramp (Q1 FY2026 call via SDxCentral) (verify: 2026-dated).

Speeds, optics and the physics of reach

Shipping and forecast. The 2025–26 research describes 400G and 800G shipping in volume and says 800G ports passed 20 million within three years of first shipments, against six to seven years for 400G (verify: reporting period). Its expectation of 1.6T ports (8 × 200G) and 102.4T silicon in 2026 is a dated forecast, not a current procurement guarantee (verify). IEEE 802.3dj, covering 200G per lane and 200/400/800G and 1.6T over specified media, was at draft D2.4 and tracking to late 2026, with a September 2026 target in that research (verify: dated schedule).

Form factors and power. OSFP has a larger thermal envelope, up to about 24 W in the cited comparison, and is favored for 800G and 1.6T; QSFP-DD is denser with tighter thermals. Standard 800G gray optics are cited at 14–18 W; the DSP alone at 6–7 W, or 12–14 W at 1.6T. Linear-drive pluggable optics (LPO) omit the DSP. Co-packaged optics (CPO) move the optical engine onto the switch package to reduce power per port at the cost of field replaceability. The research's volume-ramp expectation from 2026 on InfiniBand and Ethernet is a dated forecast (verify).

Optics as share and failure source. Transceivers consume about 50% of a traditional switch system's power and more than 50% of its cost in Broadcom's cited comparison; a fully loaded 32-port 800G switch can have over 500 W of optics, more than its ASIC. An optic is an active, laser-bearing component, so thousands create a substantial spares and service burden. The research calls them the dominant hardware failure source in an AI fabric (verify: failure-rate basis). Switch-system watts may already include plug-in optics (§6).

Copper reach at 800G. The research describes 112 Gb/s PAM4 lanes at about 28 GHz Nyquist, with increasing loss limiting passive copper to about 2 m; it cites IEEE 802.3ck for a 2 m maximum at 112G. This is floor-plan guidance, not a promise that every certified 800G cable assembly works at 2 m. Retimed active electrical cable (AEC, about 10–11 W per end in the cited example) can fill a roughly 3–7 m reach gap. Check the selected cable, port combination and certified length. Fiber reach likewise belongs to the selected optic and link budget, not to OS2 glass alone.

How much of an AI pod is network

For the specified GB300 NVL72 cluster (SemiAnalysis, 2025), networking is 15% of total cluster cost for a three-layer InfiniBand design and 18% for four layers. These are not shares of facility construction capex or of every tenant's network. In the cited three-layer design with NVIDIA LinkX optics, optical transceivers are about 60% of networking cost and 45% of networking power; the back-end is about 85% of networking cost and 86% of networking power. About six optical transceivers per GPU is a rail-optimized design assumption, and the report projects transceiver power approaching 180 MW across a million GPUs. Neither figure is a count or measurement of this campus.

Caveats. (verify) Six optics per GPU is used here as an upper-bound design assumption for the course's smaller copper GPU-to-leaf pod, not an observed Hall 1 bill of materials. Its 72 GPUs per rack and 200 AI racks give an assumed 14,400 GPUs across the four halls, with 50 racks and 3,600 GPUs in Hall 1. (gap) No network weight per rack or weight breakdown was provided. (gap) Cost per rack and per port was not quantified (§11).

Reading the breakdown. Indicatively scaling the research's 200-rack, 24 MW zone, 14,400 assumed GPUs × six optics per GPU × about 16 W gives about 1.4 MW of back-end optics. The course's indicative allowance for switch electronics and other network load is about 1.1–1.6 MW, yielding roughly 2.5–3 MW of network power, or 10–12% of that zone's IT load; GPU racks and storage account for the remainder. Some switch power specifications already include plug-in optics. In particular, the report's illustrative 580 switches × 2.5 kW cannot simply be added to the separately calculated optics watts if that switch rating includes optics (verify: specification boundary). The 1.1–1.6 MW line is the course's indicative non-overlapping allowance scaled from the research, not a measured electronics total. Hall 1's 50-rack AI pod is a quarter of each campus-zone line, about 0.6–0.7 MW of indicative network load in addition to its 6 MW GPU-rack load (§12).

The edge of the building

Carrier entry. TIA-942 calls for two diverse entrance facilities fed by separate duct banks and maintenance holes; its redundancy guidance gives at least 20 m (66 ft) separation for two access-provider entrance pathways (verify: applicable rating and clause). Twenty metres is a route-planning target, not proof that the carrier services remain independent upstream. A 40 MW campus should plan for four or more carriers, mixing dark fiber, lit circuits and wavelength services. Topic 3 scored the Phoenix-leaning site partly for metro fiber density and two separate routes; the course fixes two entrance rooms on opposite walls of Hall 1, at least 20 m apart, with separate duct banks from those routes. An end-to-end diversity check must also examine carrier laterals, conduits, upstream routes, equipment and handoffs.

Meet-me rooms. A meet-me room (MMR) is where carriers and tenants interconnect; it may be carrier-neutral and operator-owned, or carrier-owned. The course opens one carrier-neutral operator MMR at Hall 1's NOC end (Topic 4), cross-connected to both entrance rooms. Those two entrances still share one room. A second separated MMR with Hall 2 belongs to the campus diversity plan, subject to separate paths and service design; it is not present in the opening-phase arrangement. The course treats cross-connects as a recurring operator line but the research gives no campus count, so it supplies no campus revenue figure.

Cross-connects. Physically a single-mode fiber jumper joins customers' patch panels (Equinix standardised to single-mode only on 31 March 2024). The monthly fee pays for an operator-managed connection, space, operation and support, not the purchase price of the jumper. Pricing is about $100–300 a month (Vendr, 2026) (verify: 2026 pricing), with metro quotes of $220–350. Interconnection is 19% of Equinix's recurring revenue: per its Q4 and FY2024 results (12 February 2025), interconnection revenues "stepped up 9% year-over-year… accounting for 19% of Equinix's recurring revenue," with "more than 482,000 total interconnections." "Interconnection and other" is about 8% of Digital Realty's total revenue (FY2024), a different denominator. Wholesale halls carry far fewer cross-connects because one tenant owns the hall; the reference facility's 40 and 120 kW pods will hand the operator little of this line.

Data center interconnect. Dark fiber lets the customer light its own wavelengths; a lit service is carrier-provided. DWDM multiplexes wavelengths onto a fiber pair. Coherent pluggables, 400ZR and 800ZR (OIF; 800ZR finalised October 2024), plug into router ports; the research gives about 80–120 km amplified for metro ZR and 600–1,000+ km for ZR+ variants (verify: selected product and link). Fiber propagation latency is about 5 µs per km, important to multi-site training links.

Transit, peering and on-ramps. Transit pays an upstream for full routes; peering exchanges traffic directly, usually at an internet exchange point. Cloud on-ramps (Direct Connect, ExpressRoute, Cloud Interconnect) provide private paths to hyperscalers. In Phoenix the campus would peer at PHX-IX (verify) and carrier hotels; in Dallas at the Infomart and major IXPs.

Delivery and risk. Carrier laterals to a new building can take months; payment is negotiated among carrier, developer and tenant. Carrier agreements precede opening, and the opening MMR must be commissioned before a tenant can light a circuit. On Topic 3's clock the course places carrier agreements at T+6–18 and MMR commissioning at T+36–40 (§11).

Out of band

Architecture. An out-of-band (OOB) management path uses switching separate from production, separate power and an external route such as a second carrier or cellular uplink. It reaches console servers, switch management ports and devices' BMC/IPMI/Redfish interfaces without traversing the switches under repair. NVIDIA's reference architecture uses SN2201-class switches (48 × 1G plus 4 × 100G) for node BMCs and switch management, uplinked into a separate core. For the reference facility, the course places OOB switches at each HDA and the MDA, on separately provided power, with a proposed second-carrier path at ER-B and cellular backup. Separate ports alone are insufficient: check the core, routing, DNS where used, authentication and remote-access service for dependencies shared with production (verify: final path design).

The Meta case. On 4 October 2021 a backbone-audit command withdrew Meta's BGP routes, partitioning its data centers and taking its DNS offline for about six hours. In Meta's words (Meta Engineering, "More details about the October 4 outage," 5 October 2021): "Our primary and out-of-band network access was down, so we sent engineers onsite to the data centers to have them debug the issue and restart the systems." Engineers reached routers' console ports physically because remote OOB access did not survive. The lesson is to test path, power and remote reachability, including shared routing and access dependencies.

What rides it (names only). Operator building-control OOB serves facility operational technology (OT): BMS, EPMS, DCIM, security, rack PDU and CDU controllers. Tenant device OOB serves tenant IT: server BMCs and switch management ports. These systems belong to Topic 10 and later; here they are management endpoints. Facility and tenant traffic need separate ownership and logical segments; where physical infrastructure is shared, VLAN isolation alone does not remove shared-power or shared-uplink failure risks.

The NOC's demands and ownership. Monitoring collectors and remote hands need an indicative 1–2 management ports per device on Cat6A at 1–10G (RJ45). The operator owns its building-control path; the tenant owns device OOB inside its cage and its agreed handoff. Bring up and test the appropriate management path before commissioning activities that depend on remote visibility or control, including relevant cooling, power-system and fabric tests. This does not require OOB to precede all facility power work. The course places the OOB and NOC service live at T+34–36.

Media, connectors and the standard's spaces

Copper. Cat6 supports 1G and, subject to installation, 10G to about 55 m (legacy). Cat6A supports 10GBASE-T to 100 m and is the management and PoE workhorse. Cat8 supports 25/40G to 30 m for short in-row runs. The course uses Cat6A for management, cameras and sensors, and short direct-attach copper (DAC) for suitable 800G rack links; these are not part of its permanent OS2 fiber plant.

Fiber. Single-mode OS2 (yellow) is the course default for permanent high-speed fiber runs, chosen so the plant can outlast several optic generations. Multimode OM3/OM4/OM5 historically allowed cheaper short-reach optics; an installed multimode run or a selected short-reach active optical assembly can be an optic-specific exception, not the default permanent plant. Reach and media compatibility come from the selected optical module and loss budget, not the OS2 designation alone.

Connectors and polarity. LC duplex serves some FR and LR optics; MPO-12, MPO-16 and MPO-24 serve selected parallel optics; very-small-form-factor connectors (MMC, SN, CS) can offer about three times the density: 216 SN-MT or MMC-16 fit where 80 MPO-16 fit (connector and cabling vendor material). TIA-942 spaces. The entrance room (ER) holds carrier handoff and demarcation; Hall 1 has two. The meet-me room (MMR) joins carriers and customers; one opens in Hall 1, with a second planned near Hall 2. The main distribution area (MDA) is the backbone hub and main cross-connect, one per hall. An intermediate distribution area (IDA), introduced in TIA-942-B and C, is an optional aggregation space; Hall 1 has none. A horizontal distribution area (HDA) serves a row or zone; Hall 1 has four, plus a reserved fifth position. A zone distribution area (ZDA) is an optional passive consolidation point, cited at at most 288 connections; Hall 1 has none. An equipment distribution area (EDA) is the rack and its outlets; Hall 1 has 250 equipment racks in its course mix.

TIA-942-C (2024) and BICSI 002. TIA-942-C recognises single-pair Ethernet for horizontal cabling, requires at least two Category 6A cables to high-throughput wireless access points, requires at least two fibers for horizontal and backbone, and allows any TIA-568.3-compliant fiber connector outside equipment outlets (verify: clauses and application). BICSI 002 is a companion design and planning standard for spaces, bonding, environment and cable management.

Pathways, testing and management

Pathways. Overhead cable tray, ladder rack and fiber raceway are the new-build choice here over underfloor routes. TIA-569 and NEC Articles 725, 770 and 800 inform applicable power and communications separation. In the AI pods the ceiling is shared with 120 kW busway and liquid manifolds. One coordinated example puts busway A and B highest, fiber raceway and copper tray on separate tiers below, and manifolds lowest and offset over the rack rear, with no tray directly under a manifold joint. That pictured order is not a universal required elevation. The electrical, cooling, structural and cabling teams must coordinate clearances, leak exposure, seismic bracing, access and local code before supports are installed. At least two communications trays per row and roughly half the planned tray area unfilled are course growth allowances, subject to applicable code, tray ratings, cable bend radius and manufacturer fill specifications, not a universal permission to fill any tray to 50%.

Bundles as a pathway load. An 800G DAC bundle is cited at 10–14 mm thick; 64 cables per switch can obstruct airflow and consume pathway capacity. This favors thin fiber beyond the nearby rack and explicit tray sizing.

Cross-connect versus interconnect. A cross-connect joins two panels by a jumper through a distributor (structured, flexible); an interconnect goes equipment to equipment (fewer connection points). Structured cabling with panels and trunks supports manageability at scale.

Testing. TIA-568.3 addresses insertion-loss requirements. Tier 1 measures insertion loss and length with a light source and power meter; Tier 2 adds an optical time-domain reflectometer (OTDR) trace. Required loss limits, trace acceptance criteria, polarity checks, test direction, approved components and warranty documentation must be specified for the actual link by the contract and manufacturer. Tier 2 is not a universal warranty condition for every AI link. The reference facility chooses Tier 1 on horizontal runs and Tier 2 on every backbone and all AI-pod fiber, subject to its contract and the selected connectivity manufacturer's requirements.

Labeling, bonding and airflow. TIA-606-D governs labeling of cables, panels and ports; TIA-607 governs bonding and grounding. Blanking panels, brush grommets and sealed, firestopped containment penetrations keep hot and cold air apart where cables cross the boundary.

Division 27 and the demarcation

Division 27. Under design-build, structured cabling is Division 27 communications low-voltage work, either subcontracted under the electrical contractor or assigned to a separate low-voltage contractor. Owner-furnished means the owner purchases and supplies named equipment or materials under the contract; contractor-furnished means the contractor purchases and supplies its assigned items. Neither term, by itself, assigns installation, certification or acceptance. A Registered Communications Distribution Designer (RCDD) is a professional design credential, not an automatic statutory stamp requirement; outside-plant design may call for an OSP designation. The applicable contract and jurisdiction set any required sign-off (verify). Conduit and tray follow the MEP rough-in; trunks and terminations follow once rooms are weather-tight; certification precedes service commissioning.

The demarcation. The course assigns entrance rooms and meet-me room (MMR) to main distribution area (MDA), the facility backbone, to the owner. MDA to a tenant's horizontal distribution area (HDA) is negotiable; inside the cage is the tenant's responsibility. Put the retail or wholesale boundary on the route drawings and in the lease, including ports, panels, test point and acceptance handoff. Wholesale tenants often take conduit, tray and power and build their own cabling; retail tenants often receive structured cabling to the cabinet. The owner pre-provisions conduit, tray and fiber trunks to each pod under Topic 2's 15–20% reserve principle, but that does not make every last cable an owner-installed retail service. In Hall 1 the 8 and 15 kW pods are retail and the 40 and 120 kW pods wholesale.

Name four parties per link. For operator backbone and retail links, the course assigns the operator as purchaser and accepting party; its contracted Division 27 team installs, while the named qualified test team certifies to the link contract and manufacturer requirements. For tenant in-cage and wholesale links, the tenant is purchaser and accepting party; its own team, cluster vendor or contracted low-voltage installer installs, and a separately named qualified team certifies. These are course scope allocations, not an assumption that buyer, installer and certifier are the same entity. The negotiated MDA-to-HDA run needs all four parties recorded on the lease drawing before work starts. Identify the buyer of optics separately from the buyer of permanent cable.

The AI pod: who pulls and certifies. The cluster vendor, a low-voltage contractor or the tenant may install and certify fiber under the agreed scope. Hall 1 structured cabling.

  • MMR: one, at the NOC end, cross-connected to both ERs; a second with Hall 2. MDA: one, beside the MMR. HDAs: four, one per pod, a fifth position reserved.
