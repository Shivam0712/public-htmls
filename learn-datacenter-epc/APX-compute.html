Coolant at the quick-disconnect

What the rack asks of the coolant where the tenant's warranty ends. NVIDIA's liquid-cooling collateral for the Blackwell generation lists inlet up to 45 °C, return up to 55 °C and rack flow around 2 L/s (~120 L/min); QCT's GB200 NVL72 CDU integration cites 130 LPM per rack; a rule of thumb is ~1.5 LPM per kW at a ~10 °C rise (NVIDIA; QCT). Tray connectors are dripless UQD-class quick-disconnects (for example UQD04) with 25–50 µm filtration (OCP UQD specification). Vendor claims for GB300 put about 90% of rack heat into liquid and ~10%, roughly 13 kW, into air from NICs, SSDs, DIMMs and power shelves. Those claims are not the course's 70–80% liquid-capture assumption.

The three flow numbers do not fully agree (verify). At a 10 K rise, 130 L/min carries about 90 kW (2.17 kg/s × 4.18 kJ/kg·K × 10 K) and NVIDIA's 2 L/s about 84 kW; 90% of a 120 kW rack is 108 kW, which needs ~155 L/min at 10 K, and 1.5 LPM/kW at 120 kW gives 180 L/min. The vendor-quoted ~130 L/min at 10 K is the origin of the ~90 kW heat-removal example, not the pod's flow at Topic 7's 8 K rise. At 40 °C supply and 48 °C return, about 160 L/min carries about 90 kW, or three quarters of a 120 kW rack; §12 uses this reconciled course planning number. A rack meeting a different liquid-capture claim or drawing more power needs a new heat-and-flow calculation.

Reconciliation with Topic 7. Topic 7 runs the pods on a warm loop, facility water 36/46 °C and technology coolant 40/48 °C, with a valved cross-tie for GB200-class inlet limits; a 40 °C supply sits inside NVIDIA's ≤ 45 °C inlet with 5 K of margin. The ~160 L/min is rack-side technology-coolant flow at the quick-disconnect, not a specification for flow in the separate facility-water or plant loop through the CDU. The course's 70–80% liquid capture is a planning assumption; the vendor's near-90% GB300 claim must be checked for the selected rack, including its air-side remainder. Contractual conditions at the agreed interface must specify coolant temperature, flow, filtration and chemistry, as well as who maintains the CDU and warrants each side.

Racks: standards, inventory, airflow and handling

EIA-310 fixes mounting width (19 in, 482.6 mm panel), U-height (1U = 44.45 mm) and the hole pattern, not depth, external width or load rating, which are the maker's (EIA/ECA-310-E). Typical cabinets are rated ~1,360 kg static and ~1,020 kg dynamic (APC NetShelter SX). Those are a manufacturer's cabinet ratings, not capacities conferred by a mounting standard. The 8 and 15 kW zones are standard 19-inch racks.

OCP Open Rack v3: 21 in (537 mm) equipment width, 1 OpenU = 48 mm, ~44 OpenU at a typical 2,286 mm, 19-inch gear via adapters; a 48 V DC busbar fed by power shelves at ~18 kW per shelf, 15 kW in N+1, ~33 kW multi-shelf; ~1,600 kg static and ~1,400 kg dynamic (OCP ORv3 base specification). It is Topic 6's internal rack busbar and shelf: one AC-to-DC conversion per rack. The overhead hall busway is a separate distribution system with tap-offs to rack positions. Open Rack Wide is the double-wide, tall AI frame for accelerator trays, ~1,400 kg class, used by AMD Helios and Meta's AI pods (OCP; AMD). The 40 and 120 kW zones are liquid-capable frames, OCP-derived or vendor rack-scale.

The ordered inventory of a GB300 NVL72-class rack, top to bottom. (1) 18 compute trays of 4 GPUs and 2 Grace CPUs, each with cold plates and dripless quick-disconnects to the in-rack manifold. (2) 9 NVLink switch trays forming the all-to-all copper backplane. (3) 8 power shelves of ~33 kW, six 5.5 kW PSUs each, feeding a 50–54 V DC busbar; GB300 shelves carry the 65 J per GPU capacitors (§4). (4) Vertical supply and return manifold with per-tray quick-disconnects. (5) One 800 Gb/s SuperNIC per GPU, plus BlueField DPUs and local NVMe. (6) Front-access cable management: copper NVLink inside, fiber out (NVIDIA; Supermicro and HPE rack-scale guides).

Airflow discipline on a liquid rack. Blanking panels, strict front-to-back flow and baffles for side-breathing switches still matter, because a vendor-claimed ~10% of a GB300 rack's heat goes to air. The course's different liquid-capture planning assumption is in §2.

Shipped loaded. Rack-scale systems ship factory-integrated and populated. The course plans about 1,500 kg wet, including equipment and coolant, for a GB300 NVL72-class position, the figure Topic 4's slab is rated for; a populated GB300 has also been cited at about 1,580 kg (ModulEdge). Check the dock, ramps, doors, slab and final position against the selected vendor's actual populated mass, including handling limits, not a kilograms-per-kilowatt formula. Such a rack cannot ride a standard raised-floor tile. Rack-and-stack survives in the general-purpose zones. The fit-out boundary sits at the rack: the owner delivers a hall busway tap-off, A/B feeds, manifold stubs and network pathways to the position; the IT vendor owns everything inside to the quick-disconnect. xAI's Colossus brought 100,000 H100 GPUs (~130–150 MW) online in 122 days, live 22 July 2024, then doubled in 92 more; factory-integrated racks supported that schedule (xAI; NVIDIA).

One coherent load: transients and their fixes

Bulk-synchronous parallel. An enterprise hall's racks may run uncorrelated workloads whose peaks average out, the basis of Topic 2's diversified design load. If one training job spans the participating racks, its GPUs execute bulk-synchronous-parallel steps, hit a global barrier together and checkpoint together. Those participating racks can move in lockstep, so diversity cannot be assumed for that job. The reference campus has multiple tenants; its 200 training positions do not automatically constitute one synchronized job.

Cluster-size arithmetic at 72 GPUs and ~120–140 kW per rack: 1,000 GPUs ≈ 14 racks ≈ 1.7–2 MW; 10,000 ≈ 139 racks ≈ 17–19 MW; 100,000 ≈ 1,390 racks ≈ 170–195 MW. Calibration: Colossus reached 100,000 Hopper GPUs at 64 per rack and ~150 MW, ~250 MW at 200,000 (xAI; SemiAnalysis).

The transient evidence. Microsoft, OpenAI and NVIDIA report that aggregated across tens of thousands of synchronized GPUs the swings between compute and communication phases can reach hundreds of megawatts and create oscillations that threaten grid stability; FFT analysis of production DGX-H100 workloads puts the dominant content in the 0.2–3 Hz band, roughly once a second (Power Stabilization for AI Training Datacenters, arXiv:2508.14318, 2025). At the rack, more than 50% of TDP can swing within milliseconds (University of Alberta, arXiv:2502.01647, 2025). The 0.2–3 Hz band aligns with the resonant frequencies of turbine generators and transmission lines. These are measurements of particular workloads, not a calibrated trace of this campus.

Reconciling with Topic 5 (verify). Topic 5 cites faster components, tens of hertz, at the rack. Both are real at different timescales: the millisecond edges of each step are what the rack PDU, busway and UPS see; the 0.2–3 Hz band is what the transformer, generator and grid see. The course says "about once a second" for the grid-facing swing and "milliseconds" for the rack-facing edge.

Why it is regulated. NERC issued a rare Level 3 "Essential Actions" alert on 4 May 2026, its highest-urgency tier, after 2024–25 events in which 1,000+ MW of computational load dropped off the bulk power system in seconds, including a July 2024 Northern Virginia event that tripped ~1.5 GW within seconds (NERC 2025 State of Reliability Report; Utility Dive) (verify: 2026-dated). Facility-side it means current pulses, harmonics and K-rated transformers (Topic 5).

Those large, sudden load-trip events are distinct from the routine oscillations measured during synchronized training; evidence of one does not establish the size of the other.

The fixes. (1) Rack-level energy buffers: GB300 power shelves integrate electrolytic capacitors, about half the shelf volume, 65 J per GPU (~4.68 kJ per rack), charging in idle intervals and discharging on spikes; NVIDIA states the peak power demand seen by the grid is reduced by 30% when training the Megatron LLM, with the shelf optimised together with LITEON (NVIDIA Developer Blog, 2025). That roughly 30% is a workload-specific test result, not a general reduction in steady draw. This is smoothing, not ride-through: no UPS sizing credit, UPS sizing unchanged. (2) Software smoothing: power floors near 90% of rated during all-reduce, startup ramp caps, "GPU burn" at tail-off; Meta's pytorch_no_powerplant_blowup=1 injects dummy load (Meta; SemiAnalysis). (3) GPU power capping and (4) megawatt-hour storage for peak shaving (Topic 5). The training-day profile in §12 is a qualitative sequence, not a calibrated hour-long trace.

Fabric counts handed to Topic 9

Scale-up stays in the rack. GB200 and GB300 use a passive copper backplane of about 2 miles (~3.2 km) of cable for all-to-all NVLink at 130 TB/s; no optics, saving ~20 kW of optics power per rack (NVIDIA).

Scale-out leaves it. One 400–800 Gb/s NIC per GPU gives 72 outward high-speed planning ports per NVL72 rack on GB300 (ConnectX-8 at 800 Gb/s), plus storage and management NICs. That makes 14,400 ports for 200 racks or 3,600 for the Phase 1 group of about 50; a port is not necessarily an individual fiber strand. A 100,000-GPU cluster terminates ~100,000+ high-speed optical links. Because the scale-up domain is rack-shaped and all-reduce is bandwidth-hungry, racks sharing a job must sit together; fabric topology, not floor tiles, dictates row layout.

What Topic 9 found. The course planning ratio handed from Topic 9 is about one air-cooled 20–30 kW network rack per two GPU racks: about 100 network racks and ~2–3 MW for the full pod, about 25 racks and ~0.5–0.75 MW in Phase 1, at the head of the rows in the pod figure. Fabric design is Topic 9's. These network racks, and tenant storage racks, sit outside the campus's stated 1,000 compute-rack positions, but their draw must fit within each phase's IT power total and the roughly 38.9 MW campus plan; they are not free additional capacity. Their count is recorded here so the support inventory in §12 is honest.

Floor loading and redundancy

Mass on the floor. ~1,360 kg base and ~1,500–1,580 kg populated on ~0.64 m² is 2,200–2,470 kg/m² (21–24 kN/m²), 1.6–2.4× what an enterprise raised floor at 1,000–1,500 kg/m² or TIA-942's ~1,224 kg/m² will carry (TIA-942; vendor data). A populated GB300 at 1,580 kg on 0.64 m² is ~505 psf on its own footprint; Topic 4's 400 psf pod specification holds only blended over rack plus aisle, and APX-building §4 should say so (verify: depends on row pitch). The course plans about 1,500 kg wet for a GB300 NVL72-class rack and rates its slab to that planning figure. Size and check the slab, final position and complete delivery route against the selected vendor's actual populated mass, which may be ~1,580 kg, rather than a kilograms-per-kilowatt formula. No cooling redesign fixes an under-rated floor.

Why AI facilities relax redundancy (dated). Because the GPUs, not the facility, are the scarce asset and checkpointing already gives job-level resilience, AI deployments frequently accept N+1 rather than 2N and single-corded GPU nodes. NVIDIA's reference guidance is a minimum of three rack PDUs on separate feeds so losing one leaves servers energized, rather than full 2N at the rack (NVIDIA reference architecture); Topic 6's two rack PDUs per side, four in all, satisfies it. The reference facility is Tier III concurrently maintainable with one optional 2N suite. Tier III class concurrent maintainability permits maintenance of a facility service element without stopping IT, component spares address failure of a component, and a tenant's single-feed node can still lose power when its feed is interrupted. None guarantees the others, and checkpointing limits lost work rather than supplying electricity.

Server platforms, efficiency and refresh

CPU servers. Two-socket 1U and 2U, but core counts and TDP have climbed: AMD EPYC "Turin" (Zen 5) reaches 192 cores at 500 W; Intel Xeon 6 "Granite Rapids" 128 P-cores (86-core SKUs most common) and "Sierra Forest" 288 E-cores, also 500 W (AMD; Intel). DDR5 at up to 12 channels, CXL for expansion. A two-socket server draws ~300–1,000 W, ~0.5–1.0 kW loaded. The chapter's simple sizing check is roughly 20–30 ordinary servers at 0.5–0.7 kW using a 15 kW rack allowance; the upper count depends on actual server and supporting-equipment draw, and power, not U-space, can be the limit.

Accelerator servers. An HGX 8-GPU node with B200 at ~1,000 W per GPU draws ~10.2 kW, ~14.3 kW with the system, in 4–10U at ~90–130 kg; two or three roughly 10–14 kW eight-GPU servers can use much of a 40 kW position, subject to their actual draw and other loads in the rack, with rear-door or liquid cooling per Topic 2 (NVIDIA HGX; Supermicro). GPU TDP has run H100 ~700 W, H200 ~1,000 W, B200 and B300 ~1,000–1,400 W, Rubin higher; HBM is the driver, since memory capacity and bandwidth, not FLOPS alone, set the envelope (GB300 at 288 GB HBM3e per GPU, verify).

Power supplies. 80 PLUS Titanium PSUs; GB300 shelves ~94.5% at full load (vendor data). Dual-cording doubles PSU count and cost, which is why AI nodes often go single-corded. 48 V and 800 VDC shelves replace per-server AC with one conversion per rack, less copper and less loss (§10).

Refresh. CPU 5+ years; GPU 2–5 years and accelerating, Hopper to Blackwell to Rubin at about 30 months a generation. IT refreshes 3–6 times inside one 15–20 year building, so the facility is built for the envelope of several generations, not one.

Allocation and prices (verify: dated March 2026). Blackwell allocation is largely consumed by hyperscaler forward orders through 2026–27; H100 one-year rental rose ~40% to ~$2.35 per GPU-hour by March 2026 with new capacity booked through about September 2026 (industry pricing reports). GPUs are the gating scarce asset.

Warranty and service. The IT vendor warrants the server to the quick-disconnect; coolant chemistry, filtration and facility-water quality beyond it are the operator's, so the contract must define the coolant-quality SLA at the interface (§12). Trays are front-serviceable, but a populated rack is one machine, and servicing a tray can mean draining part of the loop.

Storage

Architectures (SNIA-anchored). DAS: disks direct-attached to a server, in the compute rack, no fabric. SAN (FC, iSCSI, NVMe-oF): block over a dedicated network, in storage rows, moderate power. NAS: file (NFS, SMB) over IP, in storage rows, sharing Ethernet. Object (S3-compatible): scale-out HTTP store in cold rows, high capacity, low $/TB, light kW in some configurations. Disaggregated NVMe-oF and composable: flash pooled over fabric and composed on demand (VAST DASE), in flash rows near compute, high-bandwidth, flash-dense (SNIA; vendor documentation).

Tiers and media (verify: vendor figures). NVMe flash → SATA/SAS SSD → nearline HDD → tape. Solidigm's 122 TB QLC SSD is ~0.20 W/TB and over 1 PB per RU; Seagate's 30 TB HAMR HDD ~0.32 W/TB; Micron's 245 TB SSD peaks under 30 W and enables over 176 PB per rack; QLC delivers ~43–79% better TB/W than HDD (Solidigm; Seagate; Micron). A drive-filled HDD rack can be dense in mass and capacity while drawing a few kW, but this does not establish the weight or draw of every storage rack. A flash-dense row instead needs power, cooling and high-bandwidth network paths appropriate to its selected equipment. Size both by populated inventory, not a common storage-rack weight or a kilograms-per-kilowatt formula; provision power, weight and cooling per row.

What AI training needs. A parallel file system, Lustre, IBM Spectrum Scale (GPFS), WEKA or VAST. Workload-specific read illustrations include ~4 GB/s per GPU for data-heavy workloads, GPUDirect Storage at 40+ GB/s straight to GPU memory, a 512-GPU cluster at ~400–600 GB/s sustained and DDN delivering ~4 TB/s to NVIDIA Eos. None is a universal per-GPU throughput rule. Checkpoints also need write bandwidth: they write hundreds of gigabytes, a 70 B model ≈ 140 GB in FP16 and ~420 GB with optimizer state, and large clusters may seek 100 GB/s to 1 TB/s of write, though VAST's analysis of 85,000 production checkpoints found real needs often well below 1 TB/s (VAST Data; DDN; NVIDIA). Check read and checkpoint write speed, capacity and network paths against the tenant's workload.

Ratio and ownership. A training cluster is often compute-heavy; a handful of storage racks per 50–200 GPU racks is a workload-specific illustration, not a prescribed ratio. An enterprise or cloud hall may be more storage-balanced. Colocation storage is tenant-owned; data gravity, the cost and latency of moving petabytes, keeps tenants where their data lands. ITAD and data sanitisation are named here only; Topic 11 owns the procedure.

Orchestration and what the owner sees

The stack. Hypervisors and VMs (VMware, KVM) and containers on Kubernetes dominate enterprise and cloud; Slurm (SchedMD) owns HPC batch scheduling; bare metal is the norm for large training because it removes virtualisation overhead and exposes GPU topology. A tenant may use Slurm for training and Kubernetes for other work; hybrids such as NVIDIA's Slinky, Slurm on Kubernetes, are validated at 8,000+ GPUs (SchedMD; NVIDIA). The tenant's scheduler, not the rack count, determines which racks participate in one synchronized job.

Utilisation and the three loads. Virtualisation and scheduling raise utilisation above the historic 10–20% of dedicated enterprise servers, the gap behind Topic 2's diversified design load. Independent tenant applications can peak at different times, so their measured mix may diversify. A genuinely shared, gang-scheduled training job can pin its participating GPUs near 90–100% for hours and align their transients. Design for that coherent block where the lease and operating case require it, without assuming all 200 multi-tenant training positions always run one task.

Software fault tolerance versus facility redundancy. Checkpointing, replication and availability zones can let an operator accept N+1 rather than 2N where the resulting job-level risk is agreed. Where the logic fails: a single job on 10,000 GPUs is one coherent job, and a facility fault loses the whole run back to the last checkpoint. Software resilience does not remove concurrent maintainability; it changes the cost of an outage from many tenants to one very expensive job.

What the owner sees. Orchestration is run by the tenant, cloud provider or GPU cloud (neocloud); a colocation owner generally sees power and cooling meters and space telemetry, not jobs or the scheduler. Metered load can reveal changes without proving which racks share a job. That is why the lease must carry density and ramp conditions: the owner commits kW per rack, cooling and weight envelopes, and the tenant commits to stay within them.

Density trajectory and the copper wall

Segments in 2026. Enterprise density clusters around a ~9 kW mode, about 7.5 kW on a trimmed mean, with the mode rising into the 10–30 kW band (Uptime Institute Global Data Center Survey 2025); high-density colocation 15–30 kW; AI training 40–142 kW; inference lower but rising.

Roadmap. Shipping GB300 is 132–142 kW nominal. An approximately 190 kW Rubin variant is an announced model or design claim, a suggested roughly 192 kW GB300 busway provision is a separate vendor design recommendation, and approximately 600 kW for Rubin Ultra or Kyber is a later roadmap target, not installed reference-campus capacity (NVIDIA; HPE rack-scale guides) (verify: Kyber may have been revised at GTC 2026). Schneider Electric and NVIDIA note that 415 VAC and 54 VDC both become impractical above ~200 kW per rack and impossible near 400 kW, hence 800 VDC, which moves more than 150% more power through the same copper and eliminates the ~200 kg of busbar a 1 MW rack would need at 54 V, ~200,000 kg for a 1 GW site (Schneider Electric and NVIDIA 800 VDC white papers, 2025). Ohm's law drives the next power architecture. The limits that bite are busbar current, coolant flow, floor loading and weight; the course's installed 120 kW connection is below these higher-density claims.

Is the 120 kW pod obsolete on day one? As a maximum for an unrestricted GB300, yes, since GB300 exceeds it; as a course design point, no: 120 kW is an envelope for suitable GB200-class or approved power-limited equipment. Topic 6's route toward higher-rated busway and reserved DC sidecar bays is an upgrade path, not present permission to exceed a 120 kW tap-off. At 600 kW, 200 racks alone would need 120 MW IT, far above the campus's roughly 39 MW IT allowance. Higher voltage can change distribution losses and copper, but cannot preserve that rack count within the existing power, cooling and floor plan.

Verdict on the course explorer's rules of thumb (code unchanged here). Rule 1, a server at 0.35 kW: low; use ~0.7 kW (0.5–1.0) with the 42-server cap, so 8 kW reads as about eleven servers. Rule 2, an eight-GPU server at 9 kW: slightly low; use ~12 kW and say "10–14 kW each". Rule 3, GB200 NVL72 at 120–132 kW: correct as nominal-to-observed GB200 range; distinguish GB300's 132–142 kW nominal and reported ~155 kW peak from announced Rubin and ~600 kW targets. Rule 4, mass ≈ 400 kg + 8 kg per kW ± 20%: 1,360 kg at 120 kW matches a GB200 base figure by coincidence and 1,536 kg at 142 kW sits inside ±20% of 1,580, but the rule is unsafe for a populated AI rack or a storage rack. The explorer may retain a rough planning proxy below the 80 kW liquid threshold; the selected vendor's populated mass, not that proxy or a formula-derived floor, governs the slab, dock, ramps, doors and final position. Size storage by populated drives and chassis, not kW.

Capital treatment is the crux. A 3–5 year tenant IT asset, often the single biggest line item, sits inside a 15–20 year owner-built facility, so the lease must allow approved refreshes within the service envelope. Topic 3 gave the owner a lever on the facility's scarcity, slot reservations at T+3–6 with 10–30% deposits; there is no equivalent lever on GPU allocation.

Course delivery arrangement and lease shapes. The owner uses design-build construction and buys selected long-lead facility equipment. In a powered-shell lease, the tenant may bring its CDU and equipment past the agreed tap-off and manifold stubs. In this worked turnkey pod only, the owner furnishes the CDU and agreed building-side loop, while the tenant buys the racks and storage; temperature, flow, filtration, chemistry and warranties still need a contractual hand-off at the quick-disconnect. Do not extend this example's CDU purchase to every lease.

Separate the clocks and buyers. The owner's $180–230 million first roughly 10 MW phase, including shared work, is contained within its $400–500 million whole-campus construction estimate, not added to it. The illustrative ~$600 million for 200 tenant GB200-class racks is a separate IT purchase, not a pod facility construction bill. The course's approximately $65 million annual whole-campus operating estimate, including approximately $34 million for electricity, is separate from both capital purchases.

What changes fast (verify: 2026). The rack-kW roadmap, 120 → 142 → ~190 → ~600 kW in three years; whether 800 VDC becomes the default for sub-megawatt racks; GPU allocation as the binding constraint; inference splitting into its own density class (Rubin CPX, TPU 8i, Trainium); disaggregated NVMe-oF versus parallel file systems; and AI-ready lease terms, where the market has not settled how to price density, refresh rights, coolant SLAs and stranded-asset risk.

Reference pod worked example

The course's rack-group allowances add exactly to 300 × 8 + 300 × 15 + 200 × 40 + 200 × 120 = 38,900 kW, or 38.9 MW IT. Its 200 training positions are 24 MW, about 62% of that sum and 20% of the 1,000 stated rack positions. Rounding 38.9 MW to 40 MW and applying the design PUE of 1.4 gives approximately 56 MW at the site boundary; using the unrounded sum would give about 54.5 MW. Applying this energy ratio to power is a design-operating-point estimate, not an hourly peak forecast. Support-rack power must be accommodated within those IT totals, although support racks sit outside the stated 1,000 positions.

Per training rack.
