Coolant at the quick-disconnect

What the rack asks of the coolant where the tenant's warranty ends. NVIDIA's liquid-cooling collateral for the Blackwell generation lists inlet up to 45 °C, return up to 55 °C and rack flow around 2 L/s (~120 L/min); QCT's GB200 NVL72 CDU integration cites 130 LPM per rack; a rule of thumb is ~1.5 LPM per kW at a ~10 °C rise (NVIDIA; QCT). Tray connectors are dripless UQD-class quick-disconnects (for example UQD04) with 25–50 µm filtration (OCP UQD specification). About 90% of a GB300 rack's heat goes to liquid and ~10%, roughly 13 kW, to air from NICs, SSDs, DIMMs and power shelves.

The three flow numbers do not fully agree (verify). At a 10 K rise, 130 L/min carries about 90 kW (2.17 kg/s × 4.18 kJ/kg·K × 10 K) and NVIDIA's 2 L/s about 84 kW; 90% of a 120 kW rack is 108 kW, which needs ~155 L/min at 10 K, and 1.5 LPM/kW at 120 kW gives 180 L/min. The course keeps ~130 L/min as the pod planning number because two vendors quote it, and flags that on Topic 7's technology-cooling loop of 40 °C supply and 48 °C return (an 8 K rise) the same heat needs ~190 L/min per rack, or the rise must widen toward 12 K. Topic 7 owns the reconciliation; the pod totals in §12 are the low end.

Reconciliation with Topic 7. Topic 7 runs the pods on a warm loop, facility water 36/46 °C and technology coolant 40/48 °C, with a valved cross-tie for GB200-class inlet limits; a 40 °C supply sits inside NVIDIA's ≤ 45 °C inlet with 5 K of margin. Topic 7's 70–80% liquid capture is hall-wide; the pod alone runs near 90%.

Racks: standards, inventory, airflow and handling

EIA-310 fixes mounting width (19 in, 482.6 mm panel), U-height (1U = 44.45 mm) and the hole pattern, not depth, external width or load rating, which are the maker's (EIA/ECA-310-E). Typical cabinets are rated ~1,360 kg static and ~1,020 kg dynamic (APC NetShelter SX). The 8 and 15 kW zones are standard 19-inch racks.

OCP Open Rack v3: 21 in (537 mm) equipment width, 1 OpenU = 48 mm, ~44 OpenU at a typical 2,286 mm, 19-inch gear via adapters; a 48 V DC busbar fed by power shelves at ~18 kW per shelf, 15 kW in N+1, ~33 kW multi-shelf; ~1,600 kg static and ~1,400 kg dynamic (OCP ORv3 base specification). It is Topic 6's busbar and shelf: one AC-to-DC conversion per rack. Open Rack Wide is the double-wide, tall AI frame for accelerator trays, ~1,400 kg class, used by AMD Helios and Meta's AI pods (OCP; AMD). The 40 and 120 kW zones are liquid-capable frames, OCP-derived or vendor rack-scale.

The ordered inventory of a GB300 NVL72-class rack, top to bottom. (1) 18 compute trays of 4 GPUs and 2 Grace CPUs, each with cold plates and dripless quick-disconnects to the in-rack manifold. (2) 9 NVLink switch trays forming the all-to-all copper backplane. (3) 8 power shelves of ~33 kW, six 5.5 kW PSUs each, feeding a 50–54 V DC busbar; GB300 shelves carry the 65 J per GPU capacitors (§4). (4) Vertical supply and return manifold with per-tray quick-disconnects. (5) One 800 Gb/s SuperNIC per GPU, plus BlueField DPUs and local NVMe. (6) Front-access cable management: copper NVLink inside, fiber out (NVIDIA; Supermicro and HPE rack-scale guides).

Airflow discipline on a liquid rack. Blanking panels, strict front-to-back flow and baffles for side-breathing switches still matter, because ~10% of the rack's heat goes to air.

Shipped loaded. Rack-scale systems ship factory-integrated and populated, so a ~1,400–1,580 kg rack moves from dock to position loaded, on special casters, ramps and slab; it cannot ride a standard raised-floor tile, hence Topic 4's slab and wide doors. Rack-and-stack survives in the general-purpose zones. The fit-out boundary sits at the rack: the owner delivers a busway tap-off, A/B feeds, manifold stubs and network pathways to the position; the IT vendor owns everything inside to the quick-disconnect. xAI's Colossus brought 100,000 H100 GPUs (~130–150 MW) online in 122 days, live 22 July 2024, then doubled in 92 more; only racks that arrive finished make that possible (xAI; NVIDIA).

One coherent load: transients and their fixes

Bulk-synchronous parallel. An enterprise hall's racks run uncorrelated workloads whose peaks average out, the basis of Topic 2's diversified design load. A training cluster's GPUs execute bulk-synchronous-parallel steps, hit a global barrier together and checkpoint together: no diversity, so design load and actual load converge.

Cluster-size arithmetic at 72 GPUs and ~120–140 kW per rack: 1,000 GPUs ≈ 14 racks ≈ 1.7–2 MW; 10,000 ≈ 139 racks ≈ 17–19 MW; 100,000 ≈ 1,390 racks ≈ 170–195 MW. Calibration: Colossus reached 100,000 Hopper GPUs at 64 per rack and ~150 MW, ~250 MW at 200,000 (xAI; SemiAnalysis).

The transient evidence. Microsoft, OpenAI and NVIDIA report that aggregated across tens of thousands of synchronized GPUs the swings between compute and communication phases can reach hundreds of megawatts and create oscillations that threaten grid stability; FFT analysis of production DGX-H100 workloads puts the dominant content in the 0.2–3 Hz band, roughly once a second (Power Stabilization for AI Training Datacenters, arXiv:2508.14318, 2025). At the rack, more than 50% of TDP can swing within milliseconds (University of Alberta, arXiv:2502.01647, 2025). The 0.2–3 Hz band aligns with the resonant frequencies of turbine generators and transmission lines.

Reconciling with Topic 5 (verify). Topic 5 cites faster components, tens of hertz, at the rack. Both are real at different timescales: the millisecond edges of each step are what the rack PDU, busway and UPS see; the 0.2–3 Hz band is what the transformer, generator and grid see. The course says "about once a second" for the grid-facing swing and "milliseconds" for the rack-facing edge.

Why it is regulated. NERC issued a rare Level 3 "Essential Actions" alert on 4 May 2026, its highest-urgency tier, after 2024–25 events in which 1,000+ MW of computational load dropped off the bulk power system in seconds, including a July 2024 Northern Virginia event that tripped ~1.5 GW within seconds (NERC 2025 State of Reliability Report; Utility Dive) (verify: 2026-dated). Facility-side it means current pulses, harmonics and K-rated transformers (Topic 5).

The fixes. (1) Rack-level energy buffers: GB300 power shelves integrate electrolytic capacitors, about half the shelf volume, 65 J per GPU (~4.68 kJ per rack), charging in idle intervals and discharging on spikes; NVIDIA states the peak power demand seen by the grid is reduced by 30% when training the Megatron LLM, with the shelf optimised together with LITEON (NVIDIA Developer Blog, 2025). This is smoothing, not ride-through: no UPS relief, UPS sizing unchanged. (2) Software smoothing: power floors near 90% of rated during all-reduce, startup ramp caps, "GPU burn" at tail-off; Meta's pytorch_no_powerplant_blowup=1 injects dummy load (Meta; SemiAnalysis). (3) GPU power capping and (4) megawatt-hour storage for peak shaving (Topic 5). The training-day profile is in §12.

Fabric counts handed to Topic 9

Scale-up stays in the rack. GB200 and GB300 use a passive copper backplane of about 2 miles (~3.2 km) of cable for all-to-all NVLink at 130 TB/s; no optics, saving ~20 kW of optics power per rack (NVIDIA).

Scale-out leaves it. One 400–800 Gb/s NIC per GPU, 72 ports per rack on GB300 (ConnectX-8 at 800 Gb/s), plus storage and management NICs. A 100,000-GPU cluster terminates ~100,000+ optical links; the reference pod ~14,400 scale-out links, Phase 1 ~3,600. Because the scale-up domain is rack-shaped and all-reduce is bandwidth-hungry, racks sharing a job must sit together; fabric topology, not floor tiles, dictates row layout.

What Topic 9 found. Copper reach for the scale-out links forces about one air-cooled 20–30 kW network rack per two GPU racks inside the pod, not counted in the facility's 1,000 racks: ~100 network racks and ~2–3 MW for the full pod, ~25 racks and ~0.5–0.75 MW in Phase 1, at the head of the rows in the pod figure. Fabric design is Topic 9's; the count is recorded here so the inventory in §12 is honest.

Floor loading and redundancy

Mass on the floor. ~1,360 kg base and ~1,500–1,580 kg populated on ~0.64 m² is 2,200–2,470 kg/m² (21–24 kN/m²), 1.6–2.4× what an enterprise raised floor at 1,000–1,500 kg/m² or TIA-942's ~1,224 kg/m² will carry (TIA-942; vendor data). A populated GB300 at 1,580 kg on 0.64 m² is ~505 psf on its own footprint; Topic 4's 400 psf pod specification holds only blended over rack plus aisle, and APX-building §4 should say so (verify: depends on row pitch). No cooling redesign fixes an under-rated floor.

Why AI facilities relax redundancy (dated). Because the GPUs, not the facility, are the scarce asset and checkpointing already gives job-level resilience, AI deployments frequently accept N+1 rather than 2N and single-corded GPU nodes. NVIDIA's reference guidance is a minimum of three rack PDUs on separate feeds so losing one leaves servers energized, rather than full 2N at the rack (NVIDIA reference architecture); Topic 6's two rack PDUs per side, four in all, satisfies it. The reference facility is Tier III concurrently maintainable with one optional 2N suite.

Server platforms, efficiency and refresh

CPU servers. Two-socket 1U and 2U, but core counts and TDP have climbed: AMD EPYC "Turin" (Zen 5) reaches 192 cores at 500 W; Intel Xeon 6 "Granite Rapids" 128 P-cores (86-core SKUs most common) and "Sierra Forest" 288 E-cores, also 500 W (AMD; Intel). DDR5 at up to 12 channels, CXL for expansion. A two-socket server draws ~300–1,000 W, ~0.5–1.0 kW loaded; at 0.5–0.7 kW, 20–30 fit a 15 kW rack, so power, not U-space, is the limit even in the medium-density zone.

Accelerator servers. An HGX 8-GPU node with B200 at ~1,000 W per GPU draws ~10.2 kW, ~14.3 kW with the system, in 4–10U at ~90–130 kg; three fit the 40 kW rack with rear-door or liquid cooling per Topic 2 (NVIDIA HGX; Supermicro). GPU TDP has run H100 ~700 W, H200 ~1,000 W, B200 and B300 ~1,000–1,400 W, Rubin higher; HBM is the driver, since memory capacity and bandwidth, not FLOPS alone, set the envelope (GB300 at 288 GB HBM3e per GPU, verify).

Power supplies. 80 PLUS Titanium PSUs; GB300 shelves ~94.5% at full load (vendor data). Dual-cording doubles PSU count and cost, which is why AI nodes often go single-corded. 48 V and 800 VDC shelves replace per-server AC with one conversion per rack, less copper and less loss (§10).

Refresh. CPU 5+ years; GPU 2–5 years and accelerating, Hopper to Blackwell to Rubin at about 30 months a generation. IT refreshes 3–6 times inside one 15–20 year building, so the facility is built for the envelope of several generations, not one.

Allocation and prices (verify: dated March 2026). Blackwell allocation is largely consumed by hyperscaler forward orders through 2026–27; H100 one-year rental rose ~40% to ~$2.35 per GPU-hour by March 2026 with new capacity booked through about September 2026 (industry pricing reports). GPUs are the gating scarce asset.

Warranty and service. The IT vendor warrants the server to the quick-disconnect; coolant chemistry, filtration and facility-water quality beyond it are the operator's, so the contract must define the coolant-quality SLA at the interface (§12). Trays are front-serviceable, but a populated rack is one machine, and servicing a tray can mean draining part of the loop.

Storage

Architectures (SNIA-anchored). DAS: disks direct-attached to a server, in the compute rack, no fabric. SAN (FC, iSCSI, NVMe-oF): block over a dedicated network, in storage rows, moderate power. NAS: file (NFS, SMB) over IP, in storage rows, sharing Ethernet. Object (S3-compatible): scale-out HTTP store in cold rows, high capacity, low $/TB, light kW. Disaggregated NVMe-oF and composable: flash pooled over fabric and composed on demand (VAST DASE), in flash rows near compute, high-bandwidth, flash-dense (SNIA; vendor documentation).

Tiers and media (verify: vendor figures). NVMe flash → SATA/SAS SSD → nearline HDD → tape. Solidigm's 122 TB QLC SSD is ~0.20 W/TB and over 1 PB per RU; Seagate's 30 TB HAMR HDD ~0.32 W/TB; Micron's 245 TB SSD peaks under 30 W and enables over 176 PB per rack; QLC delivers ~43–79% better TB/W than HDD (Solidigm; Seagate; Micron). A rack of HDDs is dense in mass and capacity but draws a few kW, the opposite of an AI rack; tiers are facility facts, provisioned per row for power, weight and cooling.

What AI training needs. A parallel file system, Lustre, IBM Spectrum Scale (GPFS), WEKA or VAST. Rules of thumb: ~4 GB/s read per GPU for data-heavy workloads; GPUDirect Storage at 40+ GB/s straight to GPU memory; a 512-GPU cluster ~400–600 GB/s sustained; DDN delivers ~4 TB/s to NVIDIA Eos. Checkpoints write hundreds of gigabytes, a 70 B model ≈ 140 GB in FP16 and ~420 GB with optimizer state, and large clusters want 100 GB/s to 1 TB/s of write, though VAST's analysis of 85,000 production checkpoints found real needs often well below 1 TB/s (VAST Data; DDN; NVIDIA).

Ratio and ownership. A training cluster is compute-heavy, a handful of storage racks per 50–200 GPU racks, whereas an enterprise or cloud hall is storage-balanced. Colocation storage is tenant-owned; data gravity, the cost and latency of moving petabytes, keeps tenants where their data lands. ITAD and data sanitisation are named here only; Topic 11 owns the procedure.

Orchestration and what the owner sees

The stack. Hypervisors and VMs (VMware, KVM) and containers on Kubernetes dominate enterprise and cloud; Slurm (SchedMD) owns HPC batch scheduling; bare metal is the norm for large training because it removes virtualisation overhead and exposes GPU topology. Slurm owns the training cluster and Kubernetes everything else; hybrids such as NVIDIA's Slinky, Slurm on Kubernetes, are validated at 8,000+ GPUs (SchedMD; NVIDIA).

Utilisation and the three loads. Virtualisation and scheduling raise utilisation above the historic 10–20% of dedicated enterprise servers, the gap behind Topic 2's diversified design load. Training inverts this: a gang-scheduled job pins GPUs near 90–100% for hours, so the same building needs a diversified design load for its enterprise halls and a near-nameplate one for its pods.

Software fault tolerance versus facility redundancy. Checkpointing, replication and availability zones let an operator buy less Tier, a major reason AI colocation accepts N+1 rather than 2N. Where the logic fails: a single job on 10,000 GPUs is one coherent job, and a facility fault loses the whole run back to the last checkpoint. Software resilience does not remove concurrent maintainability; it changes the cost of an outage from many tenants to one very expensive job.

What the owner sees. Orchestration is run by the tenant, cloud provider or GPU cloud (neocloud); a colocation owner sees only power, cooling and space telemetry, not the scheduler. That is why the lease, not the facility, must carry density: the owner commits kW per rack, cooling and weight envelopes, and the tenant commits to stay within them.

Density trajectory and the copper wall

Segments in 2026. Enterprise density clusters around a ~9 kW mode, about 7.5 kW on a trimmed mean, with the mode rising into the 10–30 kW band (Uptime Institute Global Data Center Survey 2025); high-density colocation 15–30 kW; AI training 40–142 kW; inference lower but rising.

Roadmap. GB300 132–142 kW → VR200 NVL72 ~120–190 kW (H2 2026) → Rubin Ultra and Kyber ~600 kW on 800 VDC (2027) (NVIDIA) (verify: Kyber may have been revised at GTC 2026). Schneider Electric and NVIDIA note that 415 VAC and 54 VDC both become impractical above ~200 kW per rack and impossible near 400 kW, hence 800 VDC, which moves more than 150% more power through the same copper and eliminates the ~200 kg of busbar a 1 MW rack would need at 54 V, ~200,000 kg for a 1 GW site (Schneider Electric and NVIDIA 800 VDC white papers, 2025). Ohm's law, not compute, drives the next power architecture. The limits that bite are busbar current, coolant flow, floor loading and weight; the 120 kW busway and slab are near today's practical ceiling and below the 2027 roadmap.

Is the 120 kW pod obsolete on day one? As a maximum, effectively yes, since GB300 exceeds it; as a design point, no: 120 kW is a defensible, coolable, buildable envelope for 2025–26 GB200 and early-Rubin tenants. The failure mode is designing only for 120 kW with no headroom path. Topic 6's dual busway with headroom toward ~190 kW and its reserved DC sidecar bays are that path.

Verdict on the course explorer's rules of thumb (code unchanged here). Rule 1, a server at 0.35 kW: low; use ~0.7 kW (0.5–1.0) with the 42-server cap, so 8 kW reads as about eleven servers. Rule 2, an eight-GPU server at 9 kW: slightly low; use ~12 kW and say "10–14 kW each". Rule 3, GB200 NVL72 at 120–132 kW: correct; widen the band to 100–145 kW, name "GB200 120–132, GB300 132–142 kW", and replace "beyond racks shipping in 2025" with "GB300 peaks near 155; Vera Rubin 120–190 kW from H2 2026; Kyber ~600 kW on 800 VDC in 2027". Rule 4, mass ≈ 400 kg + 8 kg per kW ± 20%: 1,360 kg at 120 kW matches GB200 dry by coincidence and 1,536 kg at 142 kW sits inside ±20% of 1,580, but it badly over-predicts storage racks. Keep it below the 80 kW liquid threshold; above it show a vendor floor, max(400 + 8·d, 1,400) kg, labelled "vendor-published 1,400–1,600 kg for rack-scale AI", and state that storage racks are sized by drive count, not kW.

Capital treatment is the crux. A 3–5 year IT asset, often the single biggest line item, sits inside a 15–20 year building, so the building must be an envelope that hosts 3–6 GPU generations and the lease must let the tenant swap generations without stranding the owner. Topic 3 gave the owner a lever on the facility's scarcity, slot reservations at T+3–6 with 10–30% deposits; there is no equivalent lever on GPU allocation.

Lease shapes. Powered shell: the tenant brings racks, CDUs and everything past the tap-off and manifold stubs, and owns the coolant SLA at its own CDU. Turnkey: the owner furnishes the CDU and the loop to the manifold and sells kW, litres per minute and kilograms per position. The reference pod is turnkey, so the coolant SLA has one owner and the owner controls water quality, at the price of a less clean warranty line.

What changes fast (verify: 2026). The rack-kW roadmap, 120 → 142 → ~190 → ~600 kW in three years; whether 800 VDC becomes the default for sub-megawatt racks; GPU allocation as the binding constraint; inference splitting into its own density class (Rubin CPX, TPU 8i, Trainium); disaggregated NVMe-oF versus parallel file systems; and AI-ready lease terms, where the market has not settled how to price density, refresh rights, coolant SLAs and stranded-asset risk.

Reference pod worked example

All numbers indicative. The pod is 200 GB300 NVL72-class racks at 120 kW nominal, 24 MW IT, 61% of the campus's ~39 MW on 20% of its racks, leased as an envelope.

Per rack. 72 GPUs and 36 Grace CPUs; 72 × 800 Gb/s NICs, so 72 fiber links out plus storage and management; ~1,400–1,580 kg on ~0.64 m²; ~130 L/min at the quick-disconnects (§2), inlet ≤ 45 °C, return ~55 °C; ~13 kW to air. Busway: A/B tap-offs at 120 kW nominal; GB300's ~155 kW peak is why HPE provisions to ~192 kW and why Topic 6's headroom toward ~190 kW is the pod's peak allowance, not a luxury.

Pod totals, 200 racks. GPUs 200 × 72 = 14,400. CPUs 200 × 36 = 7,200. IT 200 × 120 kW = 24 MW nominal, ~26–28 MW at GB300 nominal, ~31 MW at the 155 kW peak, which is what the busway headroom is for. Coolant 200 × 130 = 26,000 L/min (~433 L/s); at 190 L/min on Topic 7's 8 K rise, ~38,000 L/min (verify). Fiber 200 × 72 = 14,400 scale-out links. Mass 200 × ~1.5 t ≈ 300 t. Plus a handful of flash racks at hundreds of GB/s to ~TB/s and, per Topic 9, ~100 air-cooled network racks at 20–30 kW, ~2–3 MW, outside the 1,000-rack count.

Phase 1, ~50 AI racks in Hall 1. GPUs 3,600; CPUs 1,800; IT ~6 MW; coolant 6,500 L/min; ~3,600 fiber links; ~75 t; ~25 network racks. On Hall 1's mixed ~10 MW profile the other zones' quarter-shares add ~3.7 MW for ~9.7 MW, consistent with Topic 5's 10 MW critical block.

Training-day profile. Near-idle at launch → rate-limited ramp → ~90–100% of ~120 kW per rack for hours → ~1 Hz swings between compute and all-reduce → checkpoint stalls → tail-off or "GPU burn". The GB300 capacitors shave ~30% off the grid-facing peak; the facility still sees a fast-cycling, near-nameplate 24 MW block and the UPS gets no relief.

IT versus facility cost. A GB200 NVL72 rack ≈ $3M (verify: widely cited estimate; GB300 higher), so IT ≈ 200 × $3M = $600M. The facility for 24 MW at $11–20M per MW ≈ $264–480M, midpoint ~$370M (Topic 1's ranges). The GPUs cost more than the building and are replaced 3–5 times over its life, which is why allocation, lead time and refresh dominate the pod's economics.

Interface ownership and the four protections. IT vendor to the quick-disconnect, facility beyond; the CDU is owner-furnished because the pod is turnkey, and coolant quality, 25–50 µm filtration and the temperature and flow SLA at the manifold are lease terms either way. The tenant that changes GPU generation mid-lease is the biggest owner risk; the hall protects itself by (1) leasing to a power, cooling and weight envelope, not a SKU; (2) pre-provisioning busway headroom toward ~190 kW and manifold capacity; (3) writing refresh and return conditions and coolant SLAs into the lease; (4) keeping a path to 800 VDC in the electrical room, Topic 6's sidecar bays. N+1 and single-corded nodes are the tenant's risk to accept in writing.
