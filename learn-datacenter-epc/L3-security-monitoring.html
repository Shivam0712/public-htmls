How a data center protects itself and proves it

Deep detail and sources: the appendix for this topic. Short version: sixty words. The research report this chapter was distilled from. Learn it as cards: 81 cards. Earlier version: archived 2026-09-26.

Imagine a 1,000-rack colocation campus, where customers rent space for computing equipment, near Phoenix or Dallas. At full build-out, its information technology (IT) equipment needs about 40 megawatts (MW), while cooling and other overhead raise the whole site's draw to about 56 MW. Hall 1 opens as the first roughly 10 MW phase, before the other halls are filled.

The campus aims for Tier III-class concurrent maintainability, meaning critical equipment can be serviced without a planned shutdown. Guards, fire protection, monitoring and meters answer four related questions: who entered, what went wrong, what responded, and what the plant consumed. Following a visitor, a hazard and a meter makes each question concrete.

Start with the campus

The campus has one construction budget but opens in roughly four phases. We call the first 10 MW hall Phase 1. Its shared electrical and cooling infrastructure may run long before every rack is occupied.

Takeaway: At build-out, the gap between 40 MW and 56 MW is the facility's overhead, not missing computing capacity. Part of it keeps running when only Hall 1 is open. That distinction will matter when we test an energy claim (APX-security-monitoring §12).

Where the course numbers come from
  • The rack mix is 300 at 8 kilowatts (kW), 300 at 15 kW, 200 at 40 kW and 200 at 120 kW. The last 200 racks are artificial intelligence (AI) pods: one fifth of the racks but about three fifths of the IT load.
  • Phase 1 costs about $180–230 million, including shared site and shell work for all four halls. At full operation, running costs are about $65 million yearly, including about $34 million for electricity.

Put boundaries between the road and the rack

A vehicle must pass a guarded entrance and a crash-rated barrier before anyone reaches the building. The M50 designation means a barrier was tested against a 15,000-pound truck at 50 miles per hour; its foundation matters too. A perimeter intrusion detection system (PIDS) watches the fence. A mantrap is an interlocked, two-door portal that admits one checked person at a time. A cage is a tenant's lockable fenced area inside a hall.

Takeaway: The plan tightens access toward the rack, while keeping the delivery dock on its own controlled route. A customer renting a whole hall or walled suite may control its inner doors, as the lease specifies. The dock needs an alarmed, camera-covered vestibule because it must sometimes open to receive equipment (APX-security-monitoring §1).

What the plan does not decide
  • An M50 test rating is not a universal requirement. Select barriers from the assessed vehicle threat and install them with a suitable foundation; a penetration rating states how far the test vehicle travels past the barrier.
  • Physical standards describe protected layers, while audits inspect whether doors, badges, guards and cameras actually work. See APX-security-monitoring §1.

Make entry leave usable evidence

A legitimate visitor is the best way to test these boundaries. The guard checks an approved list, and reception issues a visitor-only badge and names an escort. Staff entering the hall use a badge plus a biometric check; a visitor follows the site's supervised guest procedure rather than borrowing the escort's badge. Anti-passback requires a recorded exit before that badge can enter again.

Takeaway: An on-site guard can intervene; a remote monitoring service can only alert someone. The campus therefore uses both, without claiming that an audit standard dictates a particular staffing level. For a tenant's payment-card-sensitive area, the Payment Card Industry Data Security Standard (PCI DSS) requires at least 90 days of retained entry-monitoring records, from video, access controls or both. It does not categorically demand 90 days of camera footage (APX-security-monitoring §2, APX-security-monitoring §3).

Who controls the inner doors
  • A wholesale customer normally rents a hall or suite and may run its inner access controls; a retail customer usually rents a smaller space or rack under the operator's controls. The lease settles access authority and audit rights.
  • One round-the-clock guard post takes roughly 4.5 full-time workers' worth of hours once shifts and relief are counted. Camera coverage, camera uptime and visitor logs still need regular review.
  • System and Organization Controls 2 (SOC 2) audits and International Organization for Standardization (ISO) 27001 certification examine whether written access rules match actual records and escorts.

Detect fire early, then protect the room

Strong cooling airflow can carry smoke past ceiling spot detectors. Aspirating smoke detection (ASD) continuously draws air through sampling pipes for earlier warning. A pre-action sprinkler keeps water out of its dry pipes until a detector operates; a double-interlock design also waits for a sprinkler head to open. Inert gas can suppress a fire without wetting electrical equipment, provided the room holds the gas. These protections solve different problems.

Takeaway: Only sprinkler heads opened by heat actually discharge water; double interlock does not make water damage impossible. Gas-protected rooms need an integrity test using a temporary door fan, plus a safe evacuation sequence. The fire alarm panel makes life-safety decisions independently of the building's monitoring software (APX-security-monitoring §4, APX-security-monitoring §5, APX-security-monitoring §8).

Changing agents and room-specific tests
  • A door-fan test checks that effective suppressant concentration is retained for at least ten minutes. High-pressure discharge noise can also disturb spinning hard drives.
  • 3M announced it would leave Novec 1230 fluid production by end-2025, but generic fluoroketone (FK) agent FK-5-1-12 remains available. The hydrofluorocarbon (HFC) fire agent HFC-227ea faces a phasedown. Consider inert gas or water mist without treating code-required sprinklers as optional.
  • Some insurers require very-early-warning alarms to slow cooling airflow before release. The approved actions depend on the room; neither one fan speed nor one release timer suits every space.

Treat battery gas and coolant leaks separately

A lithium-ion uninterruptible power supply (UPS) keeps computers on until backup generation takes over. A failing cell can vent flammable gas before smoke appears; off-gas sensors watch for hydrogen (H₂) and carbon monoxide (CO). A deflagration vent relieves blast pressure, while ventilation and explosion prevention reduce the chance of ignition. Water-based suppression cools cells if they burn.

The cutaway shows lithium iron phosphate (LFP), one possible battery chemistry. Its five-minute runtime, three-foot spacing, two-hour wall and ventilation rate are examples, not universal requirements. The airflow label is cubic feet per minute (CFM) per square foot (ft²), a room-design value.

Takeaway: The 2026 edition of the National Fire Protection Association (NFPA) 855 does not treat venting alone as a complete gas-hazard strategy. Underwriters Laboratories (UL) 9540A fire-test data and the local fire authority guide the actual room layout. The liquid-cooled computing racks create a different incident, which needs a different sensor and shutoff.

Takeaway: Conductive leak cable suits water-glycol; nonconductive coolant needs optical or vapor sensing. The 2024 NFPA 75 provisions cover immersion tanks, not a complete direct-to-chip loop design. Fire protection and leak isolation therefore remain separate decisions (APX-security-monitoring §6, APX-security-monitoring §7).

Why the battery drawing is illustrative
  • Venting and explosion prevention follow different approaches under NFPA 68 and NFPA 69. The adopted code edition, test data and insurer's requirements determine room spacing and ventilation.
  • Insurers may ask for noncombustible coolant piping, flexible links and a leak shutoff limited to one rack. A rapid shutoff target is a project specification, not an automatic code requirement.

Give each monitoring system a job

Imagine a voltage dip followed by a cooling pump slowing down. The electrical power monitoring system (EPMS) timestamps the power event, and the building management system (BMS) records the cooling response. If generators start, supervisory control and data acquisition (SCADA) coordinates their switching. Data center infrastructure management (DCIM) maps affected equipment to tenants and spare capacity, so the operator knows whom to tell.

Takeaway: The last two systems are intentionally separate, not missing from an integrated screen. They exchange approved signals, for example to coordinate safe egress, without allowing a dashboard to control fire release. EPMS and BMS also need the same clock, or one fault may look like two unrelated events (APX-security-monitoring §9).

Why one screen takes real integration
  • Building automation and control network (BACnet), Modbus and Simple Network Management Protocol (SNMP) carry data in different forms. Gateways must map device identity, units and alarms, not simply copy numbers.
  • Operational technology (OT) is the equipment that monitors or controls the physical plant. An out-of-band (OOB) network carries management traffic apart from tenant traffic; segment it, record vendor access and keep fire-release functions independent of it.

Turn sensor readings into decisions

A monitored point is a named value, such as the temperature at one rack. An alarm is a condition on a point that demands an operator response, not every message a controller can send. Hall 1 has roughly 2,500–4,500 points across power, cooling, racks and leak detection. Their points list records the address, units, scaling, alarm setting and owner for each one.

Takeaway: A power dip can produce hundreds of follow-on alarms and bury the first failure. Alarm rationalisation assigns each alarm a likely cause, consequence, priority and response, then filters duplicates. During testing, inject a fault and confirm that the electrical and cooling records agree on its order. The fire panel still follows its own approved detector-to-action sequence, while sending needed status to other systems (APX-security-monitoring §9).

Check the data, not just the cable
  • More than ten alarms in ten minutes at one operator console is a common alarm-flood threshold. Suppression of duplicates must not hide the initiating alarm.
  • Agree on one time source, test every point end to end and record how remote vendors reach controls. A sensor can be connected yet mapped to the wrong scale or rack.

Name the meter behind each efficiency claim

Power usage effectiveness (PUE) divides total facility energy by energy used by IT equipment over the same period. A kilowatt-hour (kWh) is energy drawn by a one-kilowatt load for an hour, not an instantaneous power reading. Water usage effectiveness (WUE) divides on-site water in litres (L) by those IT kWh. The uninterruptible power supply (UPS) feeds a power distribution unit (PDU), which feeds the rack; the IT meter's location changes the PUE category.

Takeaway: At full design load, 56 MW divided by 40 MW gives a design PUE of 1.4, not a measured year. Hall 1 has PDU output meters for Category 2, but an annual measured claim needs 12 matching months of site and IT readings. The owner's site WUE target is no more than 0.2 L/kWh. Near-zero-water cooling in a hot city may require more electric cooling, so the two targets must be assessed together (APX-security-monitoring §10, APX-security-monitoring §11).

What else belongs in the boundary
  • Include shared cooling and account for on-site generation as well as imported electricity. Calibrate meters and keep their timestamps aligned; a utility bill alone need not equal total site energy.
  • Server fans consume energy inside the IT denominator. Pushing cooling work onto servers can flatter PUE without saving electricity.
  • Carbon usage effectiveness (CUE) measures emissions per IT energy; energy reuse effectiveness (ERE) credits useful exported heat. The near-zero-water design's 0.0–0.05 L/kWh site estimate remains indicative until measured.
  • European Union (EU) rules require annual reporting by qualifying EU data centers with at least 500 kilowatts (kW) of installed IT. They do not directly regulate this United States (US) campus, though tenants may request comparable data.

Expect the first hall to look less efficient

Why might the first hall's PUE exceed 1.4 even with the right equipment? Part of the plant's power draw continues at low load: controls, electrical losses and minimum pump and fan speeds. To see the effect, split the full-campus 16 MW of overhead into 6 MW of fixed consumption and 10 MW that rises with IT load. These are illustrative assumptions, not measured results.

Takeaway: The 1.55–1.65 range is a planning case, not a guaranteed reading; even the favorable example is above 1.4. A performance guarantee should state the load, weather, meter boundary and test period, or wait for annual measurement after occupancy grows. Better air containment and liquid cooling can cut overhead, but a more efficient UPS bypass mode also changes power protection and needs operator approval (APX-security-monitoring §11, APX-security-monitoring §12).

Check the part-load arithmetic
  • The load-related portion is assumed to be 0.25 times IT power: 10 MW at full load, 2.5 MW at 10 MW IT. Add the first hall's 10 MW of IT to its online fixed load and 2.5 MW of load-related overhead, then divide by 10 MW.
  • Which shared equipment actually runs depends on the staged design and the weather. Measure it rather than treating any scenario as a forecast.

Hand over proof, not just hardware

The last connections cross contract lines. Under design-build, one contractor designs and constructs the site, while the owner supplies some long-lead gear as owner-furnished equipment (OFE). The owner may also buy fleet-wide security software and tenant-asset software directly. A master systems integrator (MSI) joins monitoring products and maintains the points list, which the owner must approve.

Takeaway: The local fire authority can block occupancy until life-safety tests pass. A tenant can delay move-in until its security audit has usable records. Monitoring often finishes last because its sensors and their addresses depend on work by other trades.

Assign every interface and test to an owner

The fire engineer and builder must prove approved panel actions, while owner-selected software still needs tested links and usable logs. Agree who owns the points list, when end-to-end tests occur and who pays for missing or mis-scaled signals. Specify when and at what load efficiency is tested, not just a target of 1.4 (APX-security-monitoring §8, APX-security-monitoring §9, APX-security-monitoring §11, APX-security-monitoring §12).

What an opening-day evidence pack contains
  • Fire-authority acceptance, documented sprinkler and gas-room tests where applicable, and tested egress.
  • Visitor and door records, camera coverage and uptime, with the correct retention policy.
  • Signed points list, injected-fault results, synchronized clocks, and calibrated power and water meters.

How much IT power does the full campus use at design load, what does the whole site draw, and how much IT load opens first?

40 MW of IT and 56 MW at the site at full load; the first phase has 10 MW of IT.

Which checkpoints take an escorted visitor from the gate to an opened rack?

Guarded gate, reception, corridor door, hall mantrap, tenant cage or suite, and the rack cabinet.

What protects an air-cooled hall, and what protects selected electrical rooms?

Aspirating and spot smoke detection with double-interlock pre-action sprinklers in the hall; smoke detection and inert gas in selected electrical rooms.

Which monitoring system records voltage waveforms, which runs cooling, and which remains independent for fire release?

EPMS records waveforms, BMS controls cooling, and the fire alarm panel operates independently.

Which IT meter gives PUE Category 2, and which meter supplies the numerator for site WUE?

The PDU output is the Category 2 IT meter; the incoming site water meter supplies the site WUE numerator.

What first-hall PUE does the figure give when 3–4 MW of fixed overhead is online, and when are end-to-end alarms tested?

1.55–1.65; the integrated tests before occupancy check sensor-to-screen alarms.
