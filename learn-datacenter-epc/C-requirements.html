Card 1 of 64problem

What must a data center owner resolve to turn a business case into a buildable, certifiable facility, and why are density and availability especially consequential early decisions?

The owner must turn business goals into measurable requirements for capacity, rack density, availability, efficiency, budget, schedule, and certification. Engineers can then design against those requirements, and the finished facility can be tested against them. Density is especially consequential because it shapes power, cooling, structure, space, and cost; availability determines redundancy and distribution paths. Changing either after design begins can be expensive.

Card 2 of 64foundation

What is the Owner's Project Requirements (OPR) document, who owns it, and what role does it play in judging the finished facility?

The Owner’s Project Requirements (OPR) is the owner’s plain-language record of what the data center must accomplish and how success will be measured. The owner, or its representative, writes it before design begins. It guides design and construction decisions and becomes the yardstick at handover: commissioning checks whether the completed facility and its systems meet its stated requirements.

Card 3 of 64methodology

What business, capacity, resilience, density, efficiency, environmental, budget, schedule, operational, certification, and site constraints belong in a colocation OPR?

A colocation OPR should state the market and tenants served; day-one and ultimate IT capacity, growth triggers, and the rack-density mix. It should specify the availability target, design Power Usage Effectiveness (PUE), Water Usage Effectiveness (WUE) stance, site climate conditions, and required ASHRAE thermal-envelope class—not equipment choices. It should also set budget, delivery dates, certification and compliance targets, staffing and monitoring expectations, owner standards, and local authority constraints.

Card 4 of 64foundation

What is the engineer's Basis of Design (BOD), and which assumptions, calculations, code decisions, system topologies, space plans, and equipment choices must it document to answer the OPR?

The Basis of Design (BOD) is the engineer’s technical explanation of how the design meets the Owner’s Project Requirements (OPR). It records applicable codes and design assumptions, including outdoor dry-bulb and wet-bulb temperatures and indoor setpoints—not just the OPR’s broad environmental targets. It also documents load and sizing calculations, electrical and cooling topologies, redundancy, space and floor-loading plans, efficiency calculations, and major equipment choices.

Card 6 of 64interpretation

Why might an OPR be issued with an RFP and used for acceptance while remaining outside the contract-document set, and how are its intended commitments made enforceable?

The owner issues its Owner’s Project Requirements (OPR) with the request for proposals so bidders understand the desired outcome, then uses it as the benchmark for acceptance. Keeping it outside the contract-document set avoids potentially conflicting contract language. To make commitments enforceable, the contract must translate them into explicit, measurable performance obligations—for example, a specified constructed-facility Tier certification—not merely refer to an aspiration in the OPR.

Card 7 of 64foundation

What distinguishes a code, standard, guideline, and certification, and how do AHJ adoption, incorporation by reference, contracts, and local editions determine what is binding?

A code sets minimum legal rules when a jurisdiction adopts it. A standard gives technical requirements and can become legally binding when an adopted code incorporates it by reference. A guideline offers advice unless a contract or owner makes it mandatory; certification is third-party verification against a standard. The Authority Having Jurisdiction (AHJ) determines and enforces the applicable editions and local amendments, so the Basis of Design must identify them.

Card 9 of 64interpretation

How do design-bid-build, design-build, and turnkey EPC allocate design and construction risk differently, particularly when the owner's scope is vague?

In design-bid-build, the owner holds the design contract and bears design-error risk; omissions can become owner-funded construction change orders. In design-build, one firm handles design and construction, but vague scope at signing can undermine price certainty as it fills gaps with the cheapest compliant interpretation. Turnkey EPC shifts much performance risk to a fixed-price contractor—but only a precise output specification makes that protection reliable.

Card 12 of 64implication

How do measurable performance specifications, repeatable owner reference designs, and early decisions on density and availability reduce late scope changes?

Measurable performance specifications define what the completed facility must achieve and how acceptance will be judged, leaving fewer gaps for conflicting interpretations. Card 17 of 64concept

Why should capacity planning describe density across rooms, pods, and cabinets—including each pod's average and maximum cabinet load—instead of relying on one hall-wide average?

A hall-wide average can hide very different loads: 5 kW storage racks and 100 kW AI racks may share a facility. Planning therefore breaks rooms into pods and cabinets, assigning each pod an average and a peak per-cabinet power. The average helps size overall capacity; the peak is a firm limit because the pod’s power distribution cannot support a cabinet above it.

What are the capital and utilization tradeoffs between installing the ultimate mechanical and electrical plant immediately, adding modular phases, and reserving space for later reconfiguration?

Card 31 of 64foundation

What does Uptime's Tier Standard classify, and why is it a standard for infrastructure topology rather than a prescribed technology or guaranteed uptime percentage?

Uptime’s Tier Standard classifies the expected availability of a data center’s site infrastructure by its redundancy topology: how capacity components and distribution paths are arranged to support maintenance and withstand failures. It specifies outcomes rather than particular equipment or schematics. Published uptime percentages are indicative, not guarantees, because actual availability also depends heavily on how the facility is operated.

Card 32 of 64concept

How do Uptime Tiers I–IV differ in required capacity components, distribution paths, concurrent maintainability, and survival of a single failure, and why is added N+1 equipment alone insufficient for Tier III?

Tier I has one distribution path and no redundant capacity components. Tier II adds redundant components (N+1) but retains a single path. Tier III adds redundant paths, allowing any component to be serviced without shutting down IT—called concurrent maintainability. Tier IV uses physically isolated, simultaneously active paths so the facility survives any single failure automatically. Extra N+1 equipment alone cannot remove Tier II’s single-path limitation.

What do ASHRAE 90.4's Mechanical Load Component and Electrical Loss Component measure, how do they differ from PUE for compliance, and why is only the mechanical limit climate dependent?

ASHRAE 90.4 checks two separate efficiency measures: the Mechanical Load Component (MLC) is cooling, fan, pump, and heat-rejection power divided by IT power; the Electrical Loss Component (ELC) captures losses in the electrical power chain.
