The concentric model and the frameworks that audit it

Security is designed as concentric rings so that no single failure grants access to a rack: perimeter (vehicle), site (approach on foot), envelope (walk-in), hall (tailgating), cage or suite (other tenants), rack (insider). The loading dock is the envelope's weak point, because it must open to receive gear, and so gets its own interlocked, camera-covered, alarmed vestibule (research synthesis; TIA-942; EN 50600-2-5).

Crash ratings. The US Department of State K-ratings (K4, K8, K12: a 15,000 lb truck stopped at 30, 40 and 50 mph) are superseded by ASTM F2656 M-ratings (M30, M40, M50) with a P penetration suffix: P1 under 1 m, P2 about 1–7 m, P3 7–30 m. A data center typically specifies M50/P1 at the primary vehicle approach, but many projects over-buy K12 when an M30/P1 covers the assessed threat "at half the cost" (uparkbollards, 2026) (verify: single vendor blog). Crash performance depends on the foundation as much as the barrier.

Standards. TIA-942 ties the rings to its Rated 1–4 tiers. EN 50600-2-5 (2021) formalises a four-layer model with Protection Classes 1–4 and requires "a minimum of three protection classes … to safeguard against unauthorized access" (TÜV Nord). BICSI 002 gives design practice, ASIS the risk-assessment method. Topic 2's compliance frameworks map onto the rings rather than replacing them:

Credentials, mantraps, cameras and the 90-day record

Credentials. The 2025–26 norm at the hall is two-factor, card (increasingly mobile or NFC) plus biometric, with card plus PIN at outer doors. Biometrics in use: fingerprint, hand geometry, iris, increasingly face. Dual-authentication portals "employing two separate readers (security card plus biometrics)" are "very typical in the data center security entrance control environment" (Wikipedia, mantrap). Anti-passback refuses a badge a second entry without a matching exit.

Mantraps. Interlocking two-door portals and security revolving doors confirm a single occupant with weight sensors, time-of-flight or stereo vision. Throughput is deliberately low, about five people per minute, acceptable because few staff need the white space. The escort rule, not the door, is what SOC 2 tests: the auditor reads the visitor log against the badge log and expects the escort's card at every door the visitor passed.

Cameras and the retained record. Cover every entry, exit and aisle with no blind spot at a door. The "PCI requires 90 days of video" line is a widespread myth. PCI DSS sets no camera-footage retention period; Requirement 9.1.1 says to use "video cameras and/or access control mechanisms" and retain that record, video or access logs, at least 90 days (verify: check the v4 text). Ninety days is the defensible working figure, and access logs can meet it if camera storage falls short. SOC 2 has no fixed number: it tests whatever retention the operator wrote into its own policy, plus evidence the cameras were up. Storage scales with camera count, resolution, frame rate and retention; H.265 and event recording keep it affordable.

Indicative costs. About $1,500–4,000 per standard door and $3,000–10,000 per biometric door (getsafeandsound, 2026) (verify: vendor pricing); a mantrap portal is a five-figure item before integration.

Guards, remote monitoring and multi-tenant control

The guard-post arithmetic. One 24×7 post needs about 4.5 FTE: 168 guard-hours a week, roughly 728–730 hours a month with relief and a supervisor. Indicative annual cost is a wide vendor-sourced band of $130,000–440,000, average-wage midpoint near $166,000, metro single posts $216,000 and above, armed posts more (verify: vendor blogs). Remote video or SOC monitoring is cited at $1,000–3,000 a month, about $10–30k a year, claimed 60–88% cheaper (verify: vendor blogs); it is detection and notification, not intervention. Hyperscalers document "security officers … around-the-clock video surveillance by the operations center" (Microsoft); colocation anchors advertise "trained security personnel on-site" (Iron Mountain); compliance and insurance frameworks expect documented 24/7 presence plus CCTV and a SOC. The reference campus needs both. The dump prices one post; the course fixes two, gatehouse and lobby/SOC, about $0.35–0.45M a year, plus a remote SOC.

Wholesale against retail. A wholesale tenant leases a hall or suite and controls the inner rings (suite door, cage, rack); the operator controls perimeter to building. In retail colocation the operator controls everything to the cabinet and the tenant badges in under escort rules. Cages (mesh) are cheaper and faster; suites (hard-walled) give a tenant its own four walls and are preferred by regulated tenants, hence the hard-walled 2N suite. Tenants negotiate audit rights to inspect logs and walk the space.

Delivery. A dedicated security consultant, not the electrical engineer, owns the design and threat assessment. A security integrator under the design-builder installs the field devices, but the access-control platform and video management system are typically an operator corporate standard bought owner-direct so every site shares one head-end; the chapter keeps them vendor-neutral. Failing the first tenant audit (SOC 2, ISO 27001 or PCI, pre-occupancy) can gate move-in, delay rent commencement and trigger remediation at the operator's cost, so access logging and camera uptime must be complete and evidenced at handover, not "90% done".

Detection: spot against aspirating

Spot smoke detectors at the ceiling or return air are the code baseline but respond late in a high-airflow room, because the cooling air dilutes the smoke and carries it past them. The data-center standard is aspirating smoke detection (ASD, VESDA-class): a pipe network samples air continuously and detects combustion products at about 100× lower concentration. Sensitivity is graded by EN 54-20 Class A, B and C, A the highest, "before visible smoke"; VESDA-E is typically Class A. Placement follows the airflow: hot-aisle or return sampling, under a raised floor, above the ceiling, in-cabinet for the highest-value racks.

Codes. NFPA 72 (fire alarm; transport-time rules for ASD pipes); NFPA 75 (IT equipment; detection under floors and above ceilings); NFPA 76 (telecommunications; two-stage very-early-warning sensitivity). FM Global DS 5-32 adds that on a VEWFD pre-alarm the cooling air velocity is limited to ≤ 5 ft/s (1.5 m/s) so detection and suppression can work; in the reference hall that is a BMS action commanded from the fire panel.

Nuisance alarms. Dust at construction turnover, humidity, diesel or exhaust ingress. ASD's sensitivity makes commissioning-era false alarms a real management issue, so thresholds are set per room against measured background particulate at turnover, and the alarm path pauses for an operator before a second detector confirms.

The chemistry exit. Per 3M's 20 December 2022 announcement, filed as an SEC 8-K, 3M "will exit per- and polyfluoroalkyl substance (PFAS) manufacturing and work to discontinue the use of PFAS across its product portfolio by the end of 2025," including Novec 1230; generic FK-5-1-12, patent expired July 2020, continues from other SNAP-listed suppliers. HFC-227ea phases down under the AIM Act to 2036. PFAS regulation may catch the generics too, which is why the reference campus specifies no halocarbon and no FK-5-1-12 anywhere.

Acoustic risk. High-pressure discharge noise can vibrate spinning disks and cause I/O errors; FM DS 5-32 section 2.4.7.3.7 requires nozzle placement based on listed sound-pressure levels to protect HDDs.

The door-fan test. Mandatory for any gaseous system: NFPA 2001 requires 85% of the adjusted minimum design concentration held at the highest combustible level for at least 10 minutes. Walls must run slab to deck or the agent leaks over the ceiling, making the test an architectural item and a gate the AHJ witnesses. Refill differs: halocarbon and FK are a bottled recharge, and DOT 49 CFR §180.205 requires a 5-year hydrostatic cylinder retest; inert gas is a large cylinder bank; mist needs water and pump service.

Why no gas in the white space. FM prefers control over water-avoidance, the clean agents are exiting, discharge noise hurts drives, and gas would reopen Topic 6's EPO opt-out (§8). The plan's alternatives, inert gas in the 2N suite or mist in the AI pods, were not taken.

Battery rooms

Topic 5 fixed LFP, five minutes at full block load, UL 9540A tested, in dedicated NFPA 855 rooms; Topic 4 fixed the room (600 kWh per fire enclosure, 3 ft spacing, 1–2 h barriers, not below grade). This is what the fire designer puts inside it.

Governing documents. NFPA 855 with UL 9540A thermal-runaway test data as the key input and the currency with AHJs and insurers. Drivers: off-gas detection (H₂, CO and HF sensors before ignition; UL FSRI identifies off-gas sensing as the early-warning method, though NFPA 855 sets no setpoints), deflagration venting (NFPA 68) and explosion prevention (NFPA 69), ventilation (NFPA 855 §4.9.3: ≥ 1 CFM/ft² continuous, or intermittent at 25% of the lower flammable limit), 3 ft spacing (reducible with UL 9540A data; insurers often want more), and water-based suppression to cool, because a clean agent does not stop runaway (Topic 5's appendix).

The 2026 change. NFPA 855's 2026 edition states that standalone deflagration venting is no longer sufficient: systems must add NFPA 69-compliant explosion prevention (verify: 2026 text). The chapter's image draws sprinklers, not a gas nozzle, for this reason.

What the room carries. Off-gas sensors above the cabinets, aspirating sampling overhead, spot detection, sprinklers, a deflagration vent panel in an outer wall, NFPA 69 prevention, the ventilation duct, the rated wall to the UPS room. Where FM insures, DS 5-33 applies on top of NFPA 855.

Liquid cooling meets fire

The unsettled frontier; the reference hall's AI pods (Topic 7: 7 × 1 MW-class CDUs, PG25 in the technology loop) sit on it.

NFPA 75. The 2024 edition added requirements for ITE immersion cooling (§8.2.2): the liquid must be noncombustible or have a closed-cup flash point ≥ 135 °C (275 °F), and tanks need a lid or closed piping and must be listed or approved. It does not mandate a suppression agent for immersion areas (NFPA 13 still governs) and does not yet prescribe rules for direct-to-chip CDU and loop installations.

Chemistry. Single-phase synthetic-hydrocarbon dielectrics are combustible (NFPA 30 Class IIIB). Glycol and refrigerants in direct-to-chip loops "are flammable at high concentrations and temperatures" (FM Global, Bob Kasiski, October 2025). Two-phase fluorinated fluids are non-flammable but carry PFAS and HF exposure.

FM's prescriptions. DS 5-32 is far more prescriptive than NFPA 75: non-combustible welded stainless piping, braided-steel flexible connections, piping arranged to isolate a leak to a single rack, runs kept low or at the room edge, and the blunt advice to "minimize glycol concentration … avoid oils entirely." The reference hall's PG25 loop is a conversation with the insurer, not a default.

Leak detection as a parallel system. Separate from fire detection, wired to automatic coolant shut-off with a target under 60 seconds; the sensor matches the fluid: conductive cable for water-glycol, optical or vapour for non-conductive dielectrics.

Unsettled. Suppression for immersion tanks, sprinkler interaction with a full tank, code-level direct-to-chip rules, dielectric HF and PFAS behaviour. The NFPA 75 committee "will look into more guidance around liquid-to-chip cooling systems" (NFPA Journal, February 2026) (verify). The fastest-growing cooling method in the AI era is being built ahead of its fire code.

Egress, life safety and the FM-insured owner

Life safety follows the IBC and IFC: occupant load, travel distance, two remote exits, emergency lighting, exit signage. The data-center wrinkle is the EPO and NEC 645.10. Topic 6 opted out of Article 645 (Chapter 3 wiring, no button, agreed with the AHJ and fire marshal before schematic design). NFPA 75 8.4.2.1 ties power-off to a gaseous discharge, so the opt-out holds only where there is no gas; with none in the white space it stands, and the inert-gas electrical rooms are raised with the fire marshal as their own question.

The AHJ as a gate. The AHJ and fire marshal hold acceptance authority; fire-alarm acceptance, sprinkler flush and door-fan witness are a hard schedule gate, named here and proven in Topic 11.

FM as deal terms. An FM Global-insured owner designs to DS 5-32 (Data Centers) and DS 5-33 (Electrical) on top of code. Per FM's 6 May 2025 FM Intellium release, "Today the company protects approximately 1,100 data centers, representing US$250 billion of insurable value," and "FM's engineers now spend about 30,000 hours a year working with some of the world's leading data center providers" (reaffirmed 13 January 2026) (verify: 2025–26 press); its recommendations "often become deal terms." FM adds what code does not: FM-Approved components only, VEWFD with airflow limits on pre-alarm, mist for all DC areas since January 2023, the HDD sound-pressure rule, the piping prescriptions of §7. Topic 4 assumed the FM-insured build; this chapter is where most of its cost lands.

Why detection is set so sensitive. Allianz Commercial's 2026 report, based on "221 claims totaling approximately €677 million ($782 million)," finds fire "accounts for well over 50%" of loss severity, though by volume "water damage led at 21% of claims, followed by willful acts at 19% and fire" (verify). Severity, not frequency, is what the insurer prices.

Delivery. A fire protection engineer of record stamps the design; sprinkler and fire-alarm subcontractors install under the design-builder, inert-gas and mist specialists as further subs; the AHJ acceptance test is the gate.

Vendors appear here and not in the chapter by the plan's decision: the reference operator's DCIM and access-control platforms are "corporate standard, owner-direct" and unnamed.

Islands. Access control and the fire alarm panel are deliberate islands: life safety and security must not depend on the BMS. The panel signals out through contacts and a gateway and nothing commands it; the access system releases doors on alarm and logs to itself.

Convergence against reality. Vendors sell a "single pane of glass," and newer platforms do normalise BACnet, Modbus, SNMP, Redfish and OPC UA into one data model, but in practice these are separate products from separate vendors installed by separate contractors, so an operator often "cannot correlate a cooling event with the electrical event that caused it" without a gateway or a manual cross-reference. Protocol bridging (chiller Modbus to BACnet, PDU SNMP to Modbus) is normal work; one NTP or PTP clock lets the EPMS sequence-of-events record and the BMS log order a fault the same way.

Alarm management follows ISA-18.2 / IEC 62682 with EEMUA 191: a documented philosophy, rationalisation (every alarm actionable, with priority, cause, consequence and response) and flood control. An alarm flood is more than 10 alarms in any 10-minute window at one position; a healthy system averages under 6 alarms per operator per hour with a priority mix near 80% low, 15% medium, 5% high (EEMUA 191). A power dip fanning into hundreds of alarms buries the real fault. Capacity dashboards show headroom and stranded capacity (Topic 2); asset lifecycle and change tickets live in DCIM.

OT security. The stack is an operational-technology estate under IEC 62443 (zones and conduits, security levels) and NIST SP 800-82 Rev 3, which adopted the 62443 model. Practices: segment OT from corporate IT with a DMZ, kill flat networks, broker vendor remote access through a jump host with MFA and session recording, keep a passive asset inventory, patch carefully and often late because availability dominates. Topic 9's OOB network carries the traffic, itself segmented, with facility OT and tenant IT on separate segments. Vendor remote access is the most scrutinised conduit because documented OT incidents used it.

The MSI and the points list. A master systems integrator ties BMS, EPMS and DCIM together. Someone must own the points list, the register of every monitored point with its address, scaling and alarm setpoint; it is the most fought-over integration artefact. The course fixes it: the owner's controls lead owns it, the MSI maintains it, and the integration test is a dated contract milestone before integrated systems testing (Topic 11). Monitoring is the system most often unfinished at handover because it depends on every other trade finishing first, the integration test is late, and change orders pile up as points are added or re-scaled.

PUE and its siblings

PUE is total facility energy divided by IT energy, standardised by ISO/IEC 30134-2 (2016; a 2026 revision adds mixed-use-building and unaccounted-energy guidance) (verify: 2026 text), originally by The Green Grid. The categories differ by where the IT number is measured: Category 1 at UPS output (easiest, most common, most flattering); Category 2 at PDU output (more accurate); Category 3 at the rack-PDU (most rigorous; only Category 3 credibly supports sub-1.2 claims); Category 0 is an instantaneous estimate. A proper PUE needs 12 coincident months, not a good-day reading. Partial PUE (pPUE) isolates a subsystem or pod, useful for a liquid-cooled AI pod, but is not comparable across pods. Most quoted figures never state the category and are often design PUE (dPUE); ask "which category, measured or design, annualised or spot?"

How PUE curves with load. PUE is worst at low load because fixed overheads (transformer no-load losses, minimum fan and pump speeds, control power, lighting, non-linear UPS losses) are spread over little IT. A 40 MW plant running one 10 MW hall reads well above its 1.4 design point (§12).

Industry context. Per the Uptime Institute Global Data Center Survey 2024, "the industry average PUE of 1.56 … reveals a continuing trend of inertia," flat for the fifth consecutive year. Uptime's 7 February 2024 analysis: "In 2023, industry average PUE stood at 1.58 … the capacity-weighted PUE figure is 1.47," down from about 2.5 in 2007, 1.98 in 2011, 1.65 in 2014 and 1.58 in 2018; hyperscaler fleets report about 1.1–1.2. The capacity-weighted 1.47 below the 1.58 per-site average confirms that big new sites outperform the ageing tail.

WUE (ISO/IEC 30134-9:2022) is water divided by IT energy in L/kWh. Site WUE (Category 1) counts water at the facility meter; source WUE adds the water used off-site to generate the electricity. Most operators, Equinix included, report WUE1 from a single incoming meter. WUE runs from 0 (fully air-cooled) to about 2.5 L/kWh (evaporative). The reference campus's tension: near-zero-water heat rejection drives site WUE toward zero but usually costs more electricity for mechanical cooling in a hot climate, pushing PUE up, so PUE ≤ 1.4 and WUE ≤ 0.2 are one squeeze, not two targets.

CUE (30134-8) is carbon divided by IT energy, location-based against market-based factors. ERE and ERF (30134-6) credit reused heat; ERF is a headline metric in the EU scheme (§11).

"PUE is done." With PUE stuck near 1.56 and AI raising IT power itself, critics argue it no longer captures useful work and push water, carbon and compute-productivity metrics; defenders keep it as the one comparable, decade-long baseline. Both are right: PUE stays the common yardstick, now one of several.

Pitfalls, levers, regulation and the guarantee

How PUE is gamed. Category 1 called 1.1; cool-night instantaneous readings; excluded losses (a shared chilled-water plant, the OOB network, security and office loads); and the server-fan problem: server fans sit inside the IT number, so a design that pushes heat onto them flatters PUE while wasting energy. Meter accuracy: proving PUE ≤ 1.4 and WUE ≤ 0.2 to an auditor needs revenue-grade (about Class 0.5 or better) meters at the fence and at UPS or PDU output, plus a calibrated water meter, logged over 12 months.

In eco or bypass mode the load rides the bypass with the inverter on standby, 98–99% against 94–96%, but standard eco-mode drops to IEC 62040-3 Class 3, the lowest protection class; vendor "eConversion" modes claim about 99% and Class 1. Topic 5 fixed VFI double-conversion with eco-mode off; the lever stays in the table to show what is refused, about 0.5–1 MW and $0.3–0.7M a year, for a load that never sees the grid.

EU reporting. Energy Efficiency Directive 2023/1791, Article 12, with Delegated Regulation (EU) 2024/1364 (in force 6 June 2024): every EU data center with ≥ 500 kW installed IT reports to the European Database on Data Centres, 24 data points yielding PUE, WUE, ERF and REF, first report 15 September 2024, then annually by 15 May (2025 data due 15 May 2026). A proposed A–G label is expected around 2027 (verify: speculative). The campus is US-sited and not bound, but EU-owned tenants and lenders increasingly want the data, so the course fixes a voluntary annual EED-format PUE and WUE report.

Other rules. The EN 50600-4 series defines KPI methods (PUE per EN 50600-4-2). Germany's EnEfG sets PUE limits, ≤ 1.5 for new centers from July 2026 and ≤ 1.3 from 2030. ASHRAE 90.4 is the US design-side check (Topic 2): not PUE but a Mechanical Load Component and an Electrical Loss Component against 19 climate-zone maxima, with water in scope from 90.4-2025. A design can hit its PUE target and still fail MLC.

LEED and ENERGY STAR. LEED at certification, with EAp3 whole-facility metering feeding Portfolio Manager. ENERGY STAR deferred: it needs 12 months of data and a score of 75 or better on measured PUE, which Phase 1 at part load will not reach (course decision).

Who guarantees PUE. Under a performance specification the design-builder (or commissioning agent or OEM) warrants a figure, but the fight is when and how it is measured. At acceptance on a lightly loaded Phase 1 PUE reads about 1.55–1.65 and "fails"; after a year at design load it can hit 1.4. Best practice writes the guarantee as a curve or a loaded-condition test (PUE at ≥ X% load) or a 12-month annualised measurement, so neither party is punished for the physics of ramp. A naive "1.4 at acceptance" makes the builder eat liquidated damages for a plant that is fine. The reference campus writes the curve.

WUE. One incoming water meter; the near-zero-water design makes make-up tiny, so site WUE lands about 0.0–0.05 L/kWh, comfortably under the 0.2 ceiling.

Phase 1 part-load PUE, worked. Phase 1 is 10 MW IT in Hall 1. Overhead splits into a fixed part (transformer no-load, control power, minimum fan, pump and chiller speeds, the UPS conversion floor, lighting) and a load-proportional part (chiller lift, fan and pump work). At the full 40 MW design: facility ≈ 56 MW, overhead ≈ 16 MW, PUE = 56/40 = 1.40. Split the 16 MW as about 6 MW fixed plus 10 MW proportional (0.25 × IT); this split is the researcher's assumption, not a measured figure. At 10 MW IT the proportional overhead is 2.5 MW; if about 40% of the fixed plant is online, 2.4 MW, overhead ≈ 4.9 MW and PUE ≈ 14.9/10 = 1.49. If all 6 MW of fixed plant must run, common when shared chilled water and switchgear energise campus-wide, overhead ≈ 8.5 MW and PUE ≈ 18.5/10 = 1.85. The realistic answer sits between, about 1.55–1.65: it misses 1.4 from part-load physics, not bad design, and this is the number to raise with any builder guaranteeing "1.4 at acceptance."

Capex, against the $400–500M ex-IT campus. Physical security (barriers, fence, PIDS, access control, CCTV, mantraps, guardhouse) about $8–20M; fire detection and suppression (ASD, pre-action, inert gas, battery-room protection, alarm) about $10–25M; monitoring and controls (BMS, EPMS, DCIM, MSI, metering) about $10–25M. Combined about $30–70M, roughly 3–6% of ex-IT capex (verify: indicative), small next to power and cooling and the part most likely to be unfinished at handover.

Scope split. Design-builder: fire detection and suppression (FPE of record, sprinkler and alarm subs), BMS and EPMS (MSI and controls subs), security infrastructure (pathways, door hardware, field devices). Owner-direct: DCIM and the access-control and CCTV platform; often the EPMS head-end and sometimes the generator-paralleling SCADA are OFE too. The interfaces are the risk: the fire alarm must signal the BMS to shut dampers and stamp the EPMS, and the access system must release doors on alarm. The owner's controls lead owns the points list, the MSI maintains it, and the integration test is a dated milestone before IST.

What auditors inspect. SOC 2 and ISO 27001: complete, time-synced access logs, camera coverage and uptime records, visitor logs, alarm and event records, evidence of review. PCI DSS Requirement 9: the ≥ 90-day record for sensitive areas, badge issuance and revocation, escort logs. All three care that the systems were operating and evidenced, not merely installed.

Changing fast in 2026. Clean-agent phase-outs, with PFAS regulation possibly catching generic FK-5-1-12; NFPA 855 (2026) explosion prevention and insurer spacing beyond code; liquid-cooling fire guidance unwritten for direct-to-chip loops; EED reporting live and an A–G label around 2027; PUE supplemented by WUE, CUE and compute-productivity metrics as ISO/IEC 30134-2:2026 refreshes the definition; and AI-driven operations, where per Uptime Institute's 2024 survey "42% of operators said they would not trust an adequately trained AI system to make operational decisions … up 18 percentage points since 2022," positive trust falling from 76% (2021) to 58% (2024): promising, not yet trusted for autonomous control.
