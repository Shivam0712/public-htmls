The concentric model and the frameworks that audit it

Security has six physical protection layers so that no single failure grants access to a rack: perimeter (vehicle), site (approach on foot), building envelope (walk-in), hall (tailgating), cage or suite (other tenants), and rack (insider). These describe what must be protected, not the number of stops on a visitor's route. A visitor encounters successive checkpoints at the guarded gate, reception, corridor door, hall portal, tenant boundary and rack. The loading dock is the envelope's weak point because it must open to receive gear. It gets its own interlocked, camera-covered, alarmed vestibule (research synthesis; TIA-942; EN 50600-2-5).

Crash ratings. The US Department of State K-ratings (K4, K8, K12: a 15,000 lb truck stopped at 30, 40 and 50 mph) are superseded by ASTM F2656 M-ratings (M30, M40, M50) with a P penetration suffix: P1 under 1 m, P2 about 1–7 m, P3 7–30 m. The reference plan shows M50/P1 at its primary vehicle approach, not a universal data-center minimum. Select the rating against the assessed vehicle threat and specify an installed foundation that supports the tested performance. The claim that projects over-buy K12 when M30/P1 would cover the threat "at half the cost" comes from uparkbollards (2026) (verify: single vendor blog).

Standards. TIA-942 connects security design with its Rated 1–4 framework. Topic 2's Tier III-class concurrent maintainability describes serviceable critical infrastructure, not a physical-security rating or a crash-barrier specification. EN 50600-2-5 (2021) formalises a four-layer physical-protection model with Protection Classes 1–4 and requires "a minimum of three protection classes … to safeguard against unauthorized access" (TÜV Nord). Those four protection classes are not the campus's six physical layers or its successive visitor checkpoints. BICSI 002 gives design practice, ASIS the risk-assessment method. Topic 2's compliance frameworks map onto the physical layers rather than replacing them:

Credentials, mantraps, cameras and the 90-day record

Credentials. The 2025–26 working pattern for staff entering the hall is two-factor, card (increasingly mobile or NFC) plus biometric, with card plus PIN at outer doors. Biometrics in use include fingerprint, hand geometry, iris and increasingly face. Dual-authentication portals "employing two separate readers (security card plus biometrics)" are "very typical in the data center security entrance control environment" (Wikipedia, mantrap). Anti-passback refuses a badge a second entry without a matching exit. Approved guests follow a separate procedure: the guard checks the authorised list, reception issues a visitor-only badge and assigns an escort, and the guest's supervised passage is recorded. The course does not assume that an escorted visitor is enrolled for staff biometrics.

Mantraps. Interlocking two-door portals and security revolving doors confirm a single occupant with weight sensors, time-of-flight or stereo vision. Throughput is deliberately low, about five people per minute, acceptable because few staff need the white space. The escort procedure, not the door alone, supplies audit evidence: compare the visitor record with the escort's door events and the approved guest route.

Cameras and the retained record. Cover every entry, exit and aisle with no blind spot at a door. "PCI requires 90 days of video" is not the rule. The research report attributes the video-camera and/or access-control language to PCI DSS Requirement 9.1.1, but that numbering belongs to an earlier edition, not the cited PCI DSS v4 (verify: primary-edition numbering). For PCI DSS v4, check Requirement 9.2.1.1 for monitoring individual access to sensitive areas and retaining collected data for at least three months, unless restricted by law (verify: published v4 text and exact numbering). The campus specifies at least 90 days of entry-monitoring records, using video, access-control records or both. This is its working retention figure, not a universal rule to keep every camera's footage for 90 days. SOC 2 sets no fixed number here: the auditor tests the operator's stated policy and evidence that cameras were operating. Storage scales with camera count, resolution, frame rate and retention; H.265 and event recording help control it.

Indicative costs. About $1,500–4,000 per standard door and $3,000–10,000 per biometric door (getsafeandsound, 2026) (verify: vendor pricing); a mantrap portal is a five-figure item before integration.

Guards, remote monitoring and multi-tenant control

The guard-post arithmetic. One 24×7 post needs about 4.5 FTE to cover 168 guard-hours a week, roughly 728–730 hours a month, with relief and supervision. Indicative annual cost is a wide vendor-sourced band of $130,000–440,000, with an average-wage midpoint near $166,000, metro single posts at $216,000 and above, and armed posts costing more (verify: vendor blogs). Remote video or SOC monitoring is cited at $1,000–3,000 a month, about $10–30k a year, and claimed to be 60–88% cheaper (verify: vendor blogs). It detects and notifies; it does not intervene on site. Hyperscalers document "security officers … around-the-clock video surveillance by the operations center" (Microsoft), and colocation anchors advertise "trained security personnel on-site" (Iron Mountain). These are examples of operating practice, not proof that SOC 2, ISO 27001 or PCI DSS automatically requires a 24-hour on-site guard. Staffing follows the operator's assessed risk, tenant contracts and insurance terms. This campus chooses two 24-hour posts, at the gatehouse and lobby/SOC, for about $0.35–0.45M a year, plus a remote SOC. That is its operating decision, not a universal prescription.

Wholesale against retail. In wholesale colocation, a tenant leasing a hall or suite may control inner doors, cages and racks, while the operator controls the perimeter and building. In retail colocation the operator usually controls access down to the cabinet. The lease settles inner-door authority, guest rules and audit rights in either arrangement; neither model alone grants unrestricted tenant control. Cages (mesh) are cheaper and faster; suites (hard-walled) give a tenant four walls and suit the course's regulated 2N tenant. Tenants negotiate audit rights to inspect logs and walk the space.

Delivery. A dedicated security consultant, not the electrical engineer, owns the design and threat assessment. A security integrator under the design-builder installs field devices, but the access-control platform and video management system are typically operator corporate standards bought owner-direct so sites share one head-end; the chapter keeps them vendor-neutral. Failing the first tenant audit (SOC 2, ISO 27001 or PCI, pre-occupancy) can gate move-in, delay rent commencement and trigger remediation at the operator's cost. Access logging and camera uptime must therefore be complete and evidenced at handover, not "90% done".

Detection: spot against aspirating

Why airflow changes detection. Strong cooling airflow dilutes smoke and can carry it past ceiling or return-air spot smoke detectors, delaying their response in a data hall. Spot detection remains the code baseline; aspirating smoke detection (ASD, VESDA-class) draws air continuously through a sampling-pipe network for earlier warning, detecting combustion products at about 100× lower concentration. Sensitivity is graded by EN 54-20 Class A, B and C, with A the highest, "before visible smoke"; VESDA-E is typically Class A. Sampling placement follows airflow: hot aisles or returns, under a raised floor, above the ceiling and, for the highest-value racks, in cabinets.

Codes and room sequence. NFPA 72 covers fire alarms and ASD pipe transport time; NFPA 75 covers IT equipment detection under floors and above ceilings; NFPA 76 addresses two-stage very-early-warning sensitivity in telecommunications spaces. FM Global DS 5-32 gives a VEWFD pre-alarm airflow limit of ≤ 5 ft/s (1.5 m/s) so detection and suppression can work. If the insurer requires that action for a particular room, its fire-panel signal and BMS response belong in the approved room sequence. Do not apply a single fan-speed or pre-alarm action to every room.

Nuisance alarms. Dust at construction turnover, humidity, and diesel or exhaust ingress can produce nuisance signals. ASD's sensitivity makes commissioning-era false alarms a management issue. Set thresholds by room against measured background particulate at turnover, and document the approved operator response and detector-confirmation sequence rather than treating an unconfirmed first signal as a universal release instruction.

Suppression options in full

The US white-space baseline is a pre-action sprinkler. In a double-interlock arrangement, both a detector must operate and a sprinkler head must open before the pipe charges with water. Charging the pipe is distinct from discharge: water leaves only through heads that have opened. Single-interlock logic differs and should not be described as requiring both events. Gas or mist can serve rooms where water contact is unacceptable, subject to their own approvals. The clean-agent market is changing; inert gas and water or mist are the durable 2026 choices identified in the report.

The chemistry exit. Per 3M's 20 December 2022 announcement, filed as an SEC 8-K, 3M "will exit per- and polyfluoroalkyl substance (PFAS) manufacturing and work to discontinue the use of PFAS across its product portfolio by the end of 2025," including its Novec 1230 fluid. That is 3M's exit, not an end to all FK-5-1-12 production: the patent expired in July 2020 and generic agent continues from other SNAP-listed suppliers. HFC-227ea phases down under the AIM Act to 2036. PFAS regulation may affect generic FK-5-1-12 too, so this reference campus specifies no halocarbon or FK-5-1-12.

Acoustic risk. High-pressure discharge noise can vibrate spinning disks and cause I/O errors. FM DS 5-32 section 2.4.7.3.7 requires nozzle placement based on listed sound-pressure levels to protect HDDs.

The door-fan test. A gaseous system needs room-integrity proof: NFPA 2001 requires 85% of the adjusted minimum design concentration held at the highest combustible level for at least 10 minutes. Walls must run slab to deck or agent can leak over the ceiling. The test becomes an architectural item and an AHJ acceptance gate. Refill differs: halocarbon and FK require bottled recharge, and DOT 49 CFR §180.205 requires a 5-year hydrostatic cylinder retest; inert gas uses a large cylinder bank; mist needs water and pump service.

Why no gas in the white space. FM prioritises reliable fire control over water avoidance, agent supply and regulation are changing, and discharge noise threatens drives. Gas would also reopen Topic 6's EPO question (§8). The plan considered inert gas in the 2N suite or mist in the AI pods but did not select either.

Battery rooms

The campus decisions are fixed. Topic 5 selected LFP UPS batteries, five minutes at full block load and UL 9540A testing. Topic 4 fixed dedicated rooms with 600 kWh per fire enclosure, 3 ft spacing, 1–2 h barriers and no below-grade placement. The battery-room.png labels LFP, 5 min, 3 ft spacing and 2 h wall therefore depict this campus's decisions, not optional illustrations. The drawing's 2 h wall is a selected value within Topic 4's barrier range.

Governing documents. NFPA 855, UL 9540A cell-fire and thermal-runaway test data, the locally adopted edition, the AHJ and the insurer govern final room protection. Drivers include off-gas detection (H₂, CO and HF before ignition; UL FSRI identifies it as an early-warning method, though NFPA 855 sets no sensor setpoints), deflagration venting under NFPA 68 and explosion prevention under NFPA 69. The report cites NFPA 855 §4.9.3 ventilation of ≥ 1 CFM/ft² continuous, or intermittent ventilation triggered at 25% of the lower flammable limit (verify: provision and applicability in the locally adopted edition). Those rates and the vent and prevention details are code- and insurer-derived design inputs, not labels that independently approve the room. Three-foot spacing may be reducible using UL 9540A data, while insurers may demand more. Water-based suppression cools burning cells; a clean agent does not stop thermal runaway (Topic 5's appendix).

The 2026 change. The research report says NFPA 855's 2026 edition no longer treats standalone deflagration venting as sufficient and calls for NFPA 69-compliant explosion prevention (verify: published 2026 text and local adoption). The final strategy must follow applicable test data, the adopted code and insurer review.

What the room carries. The design includes off-gas sensors above cabinets, overhead aspirating sampling, spot detection, sprinklers, an outer-wall deflagration vent panel, NFPA 69 prevention, ventilation ductwork and the rated separation from the UPS room. Where FM insures the campus, DS 5-33 applies on top of applicable NFPA 855 requirements. Final detection, ventilation, spacing, venting and suppression are tied to UL 9540A cell-fire data and the insurer's approval, not settled by the cutaway alone.

Liquid cooling meets fire

This remains an unsettled frontier for the reference hall's AI pods (Topic 7: 7 × 1 MW-class CDUs and PG25 in the technology loop).

NFPA 75. The 2024 edition added requirements for ITE immersion cooling (§8.2.2): immersion liquid must be noncombustible or have a closed-cup flash point ≥ 135 °C (275 °F), and tanks need a lid or closed piping and must be listed or approved. It does not mandate a particular suppression agent for immersion areas (NFPA 13 still governs). These immersion-tank provisions do not apply to direct-to-chip CDU piping as if it were a tank rule; NFPA 75 does not yet give a complete prescriptive direct-to-chip loop design.

Chemistry. Single-phase synthetic-hydrocarbon dielectric fluids are combustible (NFPA 30 Class IIIB). Glycol and refrigerants in direct-to-chip loops "are flammable at high concentrations and temperatures" (FM Global, Bob Kasiski, October 2025). Two-phase fluorinated fluids are non-flammable but carry PFAS and HF exposure concerns.

FM's prescriptions. DS 5-32 is more prescriptive than NFPA 75 about loops: noncombustible welded stainless piping, braided-steel flexible connections, isolation of a leak to one rack, and runs kept low or at the room edge. FM advises "minimize glycol concentration … avoid oils entirely." The reference hall's PG25 loop is a conversation with its insurer, not a universal default.

Leak detection is a parallel system. Keep it separate from smoke and fire detection and wire it to automatic coolant isolation. Use conductive sensing cable for water-glycol and a suitable optical or vapor method for nonconductive fluid. Isolating a leak within 60 seconds is this owner's rapid-shutoff target, not a universal code limit. Test the sensor, affected-rack isolation and fire-alarm paths separately.

Unsettled. Suppression for immersion tanks, sprinkler interaction with a full tank, code-level direct-to-chip loop rules, and dielectric HF and PFAS behaviour remain open. The NFPA 75 committee "will look into more guidance around liquid-to-chip cooling systems" (NFPA Journal, February 2026) (verify). Direct-to-chip installations are advancing faster than detailed fire-code guidance.

Egress, life safety and the FM-insured owner

Life safety follows the IBC and IFC: occupant load, travel distance, two remote exits, emergency lighting and exit signage. The data-center wrinkle is EPO and NEC 645.10. Topic 6 opted out of Article 645, using Chapter 3 wiring and no general Article 645 EPO button, subject to agreement with the AHJ and fire marshal before schematic design. NFPA 75 8.4.2.1 ties power-off considerations to gaseous discharge. With no gas in the white space, that room's opt-out stands as the course decision; each inert-gas electrical room still needs its own approved power-off and egress interpretation. There is no single EPO answer for every room.

Panel independence and coordination. The fire alarm panel keeps its approved detection and suppression-release logic independent of BMS, DCIM and the management network. Its approved signals can coordinate BMS damper actions, electrical status and access-control responses. Code-compliant egress must work at each secured opening under its approved hardware and fire sequence; do not assume that every secured door simply unlocks on every alarm. The fire engineer, security designer and AHJ must test the actual routes and signals.

The AHJ as a gate. The AHJ and fire marshal hold acceptance authority. Fire-alarm acceptance, sprinkler flush and door-fan witness are hard schedule gates, named here and proven in Topic 11.

FM as deal terms. An FM Global-insured owner designs to DS 5-32 (Data Centers) and DS 5-33 (Electrical) on top of code. Per FM's 6 May 2025 FM Intellium release, "Today the company protects approximately 1,100 data centers, representing US$250 billion of insurable value," and "FM's engineers now spend about 30,000 hours a year working with some of the world's leading data center providers" (reaffirmed 13 January 2026) (verify: 2025–26 press). Its recommendations often become deal terms. FM adds requirements and guidance beyond code, including FM-Approved components, VEWFD airflow responses subject to the approved room sequence, mist approved for DC areas since January 2023, the HDD sound-pressure rule and the piping prescriptions in §7. Topic 4 assumed the FM-insured build; this chapter places much of its associated scope.

Why detection is sensitive. Allianz Commercial's 2026 report, based on "221 claims totaling approximately €677 million ($782 million)," finds fire "accounts for well over 50%" of loss severity, though by volume "water damage led at 21% of claims, followed by willful acts at 19% and fire" (verify). Severity, not frequency alone, affects insurer concern.

Delivery. A fire protection engineer of record stamps the design. Sprinkler and fire-alarm subcontractors install under the design-builder, with inert-gas and mist specialists as needed. AHJ acceptance is the gate.

Vendors appear here and not in the chapter by the plan's decision: the reference operator's DCIM and access-control platforms are unnamed corporate standards, bought owner-direct.

One fault, four views. On a voltage dip, EPMS captures the waveform and electrical event order. If a cooling pump slows, BMS records its flow and temperature response. If generation is needed, SCADA coordinates generator paralleling and switchgear commands. DCIM identifies affected racks, tenants and remaining capacity; it neither runs the pump nor releases fire protection. Shared timestamps let the operator connect cause and consequence.

Islands and approved signals. Access control and the fire alarm panel are deliberate islands: life safety and security must not depend on BMS. The panel's approved outputs convey status or request coordinated damper and egress actions, but BMS, DCIM, a gateway or the management network must not become a dependency of fire release. Access events remain logged in the security system; egress follows each approved door sequence (§8).

Convergence against reality. Vendors sell a "single pane of glass," and newer platforms normalise BACnet, Modbus, SNMP, Redfish and OPC UA into one model. In practice separate products and contractors mean an operator may "cannot correlate a cooling event with the electrical event that caused it" without a gateway or manual cross-reference. Protocol bridging (chiller Modbus to BACnet, PDU SNMP to Modbus) is ordinary integration work. Agree an NTP or PTP time source and verify its use by EPMS, BMS and event logs so they order a fault consistently.

Alarm management follows ISA-18.2 / IEC 62682 with EEMUA 191: a documented philosophy, rationalisation (each alarm has an actionable priority, cause, consequence and response) and flood control. An alarm flood is more than 10 alarms in any 10-minute window at one position; a healthy system averages under 6 alarms per operator per hour, with a priority mix near 80% low, 15% medium, 5% high (EEMUA 191). A power dip fanning into hundreds of alarms buries the initiating fault. Capacity dashboards show headroom and stranded capacity (Topic 2); asset lifecycle and change tickets live in DCIM.

OT security. The stack is an operational-technology estate under IEC 62443 (zones and conduits, security levels) and NIST SP 800-82 Rev 3, which adopted the 62443 model. Segment OT from corporate IT through a DMZ, avoid flat networks, broker vendor access through a jump host with MFA and session recording, maintain a passive asset inventory, and plan patching around availability. Topic 9's OOB network carries management traffic but must itself be segmented; facility OT and tenant IT occupy separate segments. Fire release remains independent of that network. Vendor remote access is a scrutinised conduit because documented OT incidents have used it.

The MSI and the points list. A master systems integrator ties BMS, EPMS and DCIM together. The points list records each monitored point's identity, address, units, scaling and alarm setting. The owner's controls lead owns it, the MSI maintains it, and the owner explicitly approves it. Before integrated systems testing (Topic 11), a dated contractual integration milestone checks alarms end to end, from injected sensor condition through correct scaling, timestamp, display, priority and operator record. Monitoring is often unfinished at handover because it depends on other trades finishing first; late integration and point changes generate change orders.

PUE and its siblings

PUE is total facility energy divided by IT energy over the same period, standardised by ISO/IEC 30134-2 (2016; a reported 2026 revision addresses mixed-use buildings and unaccounted energy) (verify: published 2026 text), originally by The Green Grid. Categories depend on the IT denominator's meter position, not a different facility numerator: Category 1 at UPS output, Category 2 at PDU output and Category 3 at rack or rack-PDU output. Moving downstream counts more distribution loss as facility overhead. Category 0 is an instantaneous estimate (verify: category designation in the applicable edition). A proper annual PUE uses 12 coincident months of facility and IT energy, not a good-day reading. Partial PUE (pPUE) isolates a subsystem or pod, useful for a liquid-cooled AI pod, but is not directly comparable across different boundaries. Ask of any quoted figure: which category and boundary, design power ratio or measured energy, annual or spot? The campus's 56 MW / 40 MW = 1.4 is a full-load design power ratio; matching 1.4 in annual readings is a separate claim.

The facility boundary. The numerator must account for shared cooling, security, offices and management loads attributable to the site, rather than quietly omitting a shared plant. Account for on-site generation as well as imported electricity, and reconcile generation, imports and any exports in the agreed meter boundary; a utility bill alone need not equal facility energy. IT and facility meter timestamps must match.

How PUE curves with load. PUE is worst at low load when transformer no-load losses, minimum fan and pump speeds, control power, lighting and non-linear UPS losses are spread over little IT. A 40 MW plant running one 10 MW hall can read above its 1.4 design point (§12).

Industry context. Per the Uptime Institute Global Data Center Survey 2024, "the industry average PUE of 1.56 … reveals a continuing trend of inertia," flat for the fifth consecutive year. Uptime's 7 February 2024 analysis says: "In 2023, industry average PUE stood at 1.58 … the capacity-weighted PUE figure is 1.47," down from about 2.5 in 2007, 1.98 in 2011, 1.65 in 2014 and 1.58 in 2018; hyperscaler fleets report about 1.1–1.2. The capacity-weighted result below the per-site average indicates that larger new sites outperform the ageing tail.

WUE (ISO/IEC 30134-9:2022) is water divided by IT energy, in L/kWh. Metered site WUE (Category 1) uses the incoming facility water meter and excludes water consumed off-site in electricity production; source WUE adds that off-site power-source water. Most operators, Equinix included, report WUE1 from a single incoming meter. Reported WUE ranges from 0 (fully air-cooled) to about 2.5 L/kWh (evaporative). Near-zero-water heat rejection drives this campus's site WUE toward zero but can increase mechanical-cooling electricity in a hot climate. PUE ≤ 1.4 and WUE ≤ 0.2 are therefore a combined engineering constraint.

CUE (30134-8) is carbon divided by IT energy, using location- or market-based factors. ERE and ERF (30134-6) credit reused heat; ERF is a headline metric in the EU scheme (§11).

"PUE is done." With PUE near 1.56 industry-wide and AI raising IT power, critics argue that it misses useful work and favour water, carbon and compute-productivity measures. Defenders retain it as a comparable, decade-long baseline. PUE remains useful alongside those other measures.

Pitfalls, levers, regulation and the guarantee

How PUE is gamed. Calling a Category 1 result 1.1 without stating the meter position; using a cool-night instantaneous reading; excluding a shared chilled-water plant, the OOB network, security or office loads; and moving cooling work onto server fans, whose energy sits inside the IT denominator. To demonstrate PUE ≤ 1.4 and WUE ≤ 0.2, use revenue-grade (about Class 0.5 or better) meters at the agreed facility boundary and UPS or PDU output, plus a calibrated water meter and 12 months of aligned readings.

Conditional levers against about 16 MW of full-load overhead. These are indicative opportunities, not additive or guaranteed savings: performance depends on load, weather, plant design and operating limits.

Eco-mode, reconciled with Topic 5. In conventional eco or bypass mode the load rides the bypass with the inverter on standby, reaching 98–99% rather than 94–96% in double conversion, but standard eco-mode drops to IEC 62040-3 Class 3, the lowest protection class; vendor "eConversion" modes claim about 99% and Class 1. Topic 5 fixed VFI double-conversion with eco-mode off. The bypass saving stays in the table as a refused option, not a saving included in the campus forecast.

EU reporting. Energy Efficiency Directive 2023/1791, Article 12, with Delegated Regulation (EU) 2024/1364 (in force 6 June 2024), requires qualifying EU data centers with ≥ 500 kW installed IT to report to the European Database on Data Centres. Its 24 data points yield PUE, WUE, ERF and REF; the first report was due 15 September 2024, then annually by 15 May (2025 data due 15 May 2026). A proposed A–G label around 2027 remains speculative (verify). This US campus is not directly bound by those EU rules; the course nevertheless chooses a voluntary annual EED-format PUE and WUE report for tenants and lenders.

Other rules. The EN 50600-4 series defines KPI methods (PUE per EN 50600-4-2). Germany's EnEfG sets limits for applicable German centers, ≤ 1.5 for new centers from July 2026 and ≤ 1.3 from 2030, not limits on this campus. ASHRAE 90.4 is the US design-side check (Topic 2): it uses a Mechanical Load Component and Electrical Loss Component against 19 climate-zone maxima rather than PUE, with water in scope from 90.4-2025. A design can meet its PUE target and still fail MLC.

LEED and ENERGY STAR. LEED at certification includes EAp3 whole-facility metering feeding Portfolio Manager. ENERGY STAR is deferred: it needs 12 months of data and a score of 75 or better on measured PUE, which the course does not expect Phase 1 at part load to reach.

Who guarantees PUE. A performance specification can make the design-builder, commissioning party or OEM responsible for an agreed result, but must say at what load, in what weather, across which facility and IT meter boundary, and over what measurement period. It must also say whether the test is a loaded-condition point on a curve or 12 months of coincident energy readings. The campus writes a load-conditioned curve: Phase 1 carries a 1.55–1.65 planning case, while 1.4 is the full-load design target. A bare "1.4 at acceptance" could penalise a sound plant for operating at part load.

The reference hall, assembled

Every worked number is indicative. The $400–500M ex-IT campus opens in roughly 10 MW phases. Phase 1 costs about $180–230M, including shared site and shell work for the later halls; it is not one quarter of the eventual construction bill.

Security zones, gate to rack. The metering tree. Electrical: utility revenue meter at the fence (Class 0.5) → MV switchgear and relays → substation transformers → generator and UPS input meters → UPS output meters → PDU meters → busway tap-offs → rack-PDU outlet meters. Mechanical: chiller, pump, CRAH and CDU submeters plus BTU meters on the facility side. Water: one incoming site meter plus optional make-up submeters. Include shared cooling and reconcile on-site generation with imports in the facility-energy boundary. UPS-output metering supports PUE Category 1, PDU output Category 2, and rack-PDU outlet metering, present for tenant billing, Category 3. The hall plans Category 2 annual reporting and Category 3 on request.

Point counts, Hall 1 (~10 MW): utility, MV and transformers 30–60; generators and UPS in and out 100–200; PDUs and branch circuits 400–800; rack-PDU outlets across ~250 racks 1,000–2,000; mechanical (CRAH, CDU, BTU, differential pressure) 300–600; environmental (temperature, humidity, leak) 500–1,000. Total about 2,500–4,500, which is why owner approval of the points list matters.

WUE. One incoming water meter measures site water, not source water. The near-zero-water design yields an indicative site WUE of about 0.0–0.05 L/kWh, below the 0.2 target if confirmed by annual measurements.

Phase 1 part-load PUE, worked. At full design, 40 MW IT plus about 16 MW overhead gives 56/40 = 1.40 as a design power ratio, not an annual measured result. Split the 16 MW into 6 MW fixed (transformer no-load, controls, minimum fan, pump and chiller speeds, UPS conversion floor and lighting) and 10 MW load-proportional (0.25 × IT). This split is the researcher's assumption, not a measured figure. At 10 MW IT, proportional overhead is 2.5 MW. With 40% of the fixed plant online, another 2.4 MW, PUE is (10 + 2.5 + 2.4)/10 = 1.49. With all 6 MW fixed online, it is (10 + 2.5 + 6)/10 = 1.85. The course carries 1.55–1.65 for Phase 1 (APX-threads §7) as an intermediate planning case. All three are fixed-overhead scenarios, not inevitable readings; staged equipment, occupancy and weather determine the outcome.

Capex, against the $400–500M ex-IT campus. Physical security (barriers, fence, PIDS, access control, CCTV, mantraps, guardhouse) is about $8–20M; fire detection and suppression (ASD, pre-action, inert gas, battery-room protection, alarm) about $10–25M; monitoring and controls (BMS, EPMS, DCIM, MSI, metering) about $10–25M. Those ranges add to $28–70M, roughly 6–18% of a $400–500M campus (verify: indicative). The research report carried both the subsystem ranges and a 3–6% combined share; the arithmetic supports 6–18%, not 3–6%. Topic 12's capex line still quotes 3–6% pending reconciliation. These are vendor-priced and planning estimates, not standards-based costs.

Scope split. Design-builder: fire detection and suppression (FPE of record, sprinkler and alarm subs), BMS and EPMS (MSI and controls subs), and security infrastructure (pathways, hardware, field devices). Owner-direct: DCIM and access-control and CCTV platforms; often the EPMS head-end and sometimes generator-paralleling SCADA are OFE too. The fire panel's release logic stays independent while approved signals coordinate BMS dampers, electrical status and code-compliant egress, not blanket door unlocking. The owner's controls lead owns and approves the points list; the MSI maintains it. End-to-end alarm checks and the integration test are dated milestones before IST.

What auditors inspect. SOC 2 and ISO 27001: complete time-synced access logs, camera coverage and uptime, visitor logs, alarm and event records, and evidence of review. PCI DSS Requirement 9: the applicable retained entry-monitoring record for sensitive areas, badge issuance and revocation, and escort logs (§2). All three care that systems operated and produced evidence, not merely that they were installed.
