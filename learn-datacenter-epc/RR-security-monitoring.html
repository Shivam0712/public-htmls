The Protective and Observational Layer of a New-Build Data Center (2025–2026)

The Desktop Research report for Topic 10, run 2026-09-25 from briefs/ and published as delivered: no editing, no lint. The chapter and appendix were distilled from it; where they and this report disagree, the chapter is the course's position.

A working-knowledge chapter with numbered technical appendix. Audience: a technically literate self-learner preparing to talk to engineers, contractors and operators. Every worked number for the reference facility is labelled indicative.

Running reference facility (locked): 1,000-rack colo campus, ~40 MW IT (≈39 MW across 300×8 kW, 300×15 kW, 200×40 kW, 200×120 kW; AI pods = 20% of racks, 61% of load), design PUE 1.4 (≈56 MW at the fence), hot US metro (Phoenix/Dallas), Tier III concurrently maintainable + one optional 2N suite, design-build with owner-furnished long-lead gear (OFE), ~10 MW phases, Phase 1 ≈10 MW IT in Hall 1, capex $400–500M ex-IT, electricity ≈$34M/yr. OPR fixes PUE ≤ 1.4 and WUE ≤ 0.2 L/kWh; certifications to support SOC 2, ISO 27001, PCI DSS, NIST 800-53. Near-zero-water heat rejection, lithium-ion UPS, liquid-cooled AI pods with CDUs, out-of-band (OOB) network are fixed from prior topics.

TL;DR

  • Four systems, one job: prove control. Physical security (perimeter→rack), fire detection/suppression, the instrumentation stack (BMS/EPMS/DCIM/SCADA) and efficiency metering are the layer that lets an operator observe and defend the plant. All four converge on evidence an auditor or insurer can inspect — access logs, camera uptime, alarm records, PUE/WUE meters — and monitoring is the system most often unfinished at handover, so it is where you should ask the hardest delivery questions.
  • 2026 is a year of moving targets. Clean-agent chemistry is being squeezed out (per 3M's Dec. 20, 2022 announcement, filed as an SEC 8-K, 3M "will exit per- and polyfluoroalkyl substance (PFAS) manufacturing and work to discontinue the use of PFAS across its product portfolio by the end of 2025," including its Novec 1230 (FK-5-1-12) fire protection fluid — though generic FK-5-1-12, whose patent expired July 2020, continues via other SNAP-listed suppliers; and HFC-227ea is phasing down under the AIM Act to 2036), so inert gas and water/water-mist are the durable choices; NFPA 855 (2026) hardened lithium-ion room rules (deflagration venting alone no longer sufficient); NFPA 75-2024 newly addresses immersion tanks but leaves direct-to-chip loops largely to FM Global and still-evolving committee work; and the EU EED now forces annual PUE/WUE reporting above 500 kW.
  • For the reference campus, the honest headline is that Phase 1 will miss 1.4. A 40 MW plant running one 10 MW hall carries fixed cooling and electrical overhead against a quarter of its load, so an indicative part-load PUE lands near 1.55–1.65, not 1.4 — which is exactly why performance specs argue over "measured at acceptance vs after a year of ramp." Security + fire + monitoring together are a modest slice of capex (indicatively 3–6% of the $400–500M) but carry outsized schedule and audit risk.

1. Layered Physical Security, Perimeter to Rack

1.1 The concentric model — what each layer stops

Security is designed as concentric rings so that no single failure grants access to a rack. Working inward:

  1. Perimeter — fence, setback/standoff, crash-rated vehicle barriers. Stops the vehicle-borne threat and casual intrusion. Crash ratings: the old US Dept. of State K-ratings (K4/K8/K12 = 15,000 lb truck stopped at 30/40/50 mph) are now superseded by ASTM F2656 M-ratings (M30/M40/M50 at the same speeds) with a P penetration suffix (P1 <1 m, P2 ~1–7 m, P3 7–30 m). A data center typically specifies M50/P1 at the primary vehicle approach, but experienced specifiers note many projects over-buy K12 when a K8 or M30/P1 covers the assessed threat "at half the cost" (uparkbollards, 2026). Crash performance depends on the foundation as much as the barrier.
  2. Site — guardhouse, lighting, perimeter intrusion detection (PIDS), CCTV. Stops/detects approach on foot; forces credential check before the building.
  3. Building envelope — hardened walls, single controlled visitor entry, and the loading dock as the weak point (it must open to receive gear, so it gets its own interlocked, camera-covered, alarmed vestibule).
  4. Room / data hall — hall doors, mantrap/portal, anti-passback.
  5. Cage / suite — tenant boundary in multi-tenant halls.
  6. Rack — locking cabinet, increasingly with electronic handle logging.

Standards mapping. TIA-942 ties these rings to its Rated-1→4 availability tiers; EN 50600-2-5 (2021, superseding 2016) formalises a four-layer physical-protection model with Protection Classes 1–4 and requires "a minimum of three protection classes … to safeguard against unauthorized access" (TÜV Nord). BICSI 002 gives the design practices; ASIS provides the risk-assessment methodology. The compliance frameworks map onto the rings rather than replacing them:

1.2 Access control and surveillance

Credentials. The 2025–26 norm at the hall/mantrap is two-factor: card (increasingly mobile/NFC) + biometric, sometimes card+PIN at outer doors. Biometrics in use are fingerprint, hand-geometry, iris and increasingly face; dual-authentication portals "employing two separate readers (security card plus biometrics)" are "very typical in the data center security entrance control environment" (Wikipedia, mantrap). Anti-passback logic prevents a badge being passed back for a second entry.

Mantraps/portals. Interlocking two-door portals and security revolving doors use weight sensors, time-of-flight or StereoVision to confirm a single occupant and defeat tailgating/piggybacking. Throughput is deliberately low — vendors cite about five people per minute, acceptable because few staff need the white space.

Camera coverage and retention. Rule of thumb: cover every entry/exit and every aisle, no blind spots at doors. The "PCI requires 90 days of video" line is a widespread myth — PCI DSS sets no camera-footage retention period; Req. 9.1.1 says use "video cameras and/or access control mechanisms" and retain that record (video or access logs) at least 90 days. So 90 days is the defensible working figure, but it can be met with access logs if camera storage falls short. SOC 2 has no fixed number — it tests whatever retention you committed to in your own policy, plus evidence the cameras were actually up. Storage sizing scales with camera count, resolution, frame rate and retention; H.265 and motion/event recording are the levers that keep it affordable.

Indicative costs. Access control runs about $1,500–$4,000 per standard door installed and $3,000–$10,000 per door for biometric openings (getsafeandsound, 2026); a high-security mantrap portal is a five-figure item per opening before integration. Label all indicative.

1.3 Guard force vs remote monitoring; multi-tenant control

A single 24×7 guard post needs ≈4.5 FTE (168 guard-hours/week; ~728–730 hours/month once relief and a supervisor are counted). Indicative annual cost of one post lands in a wide vendor-sourced band of ~$130,000–$440,000/yr, with an average-wage midpoint around $166,000 and California/metro single posts around $216,000+; armed posts cost more. A remote video/SOC monitoring service by contrast is cited at roughly $1,000–$3,000/month (~$10–30k/yr) — claimed 60–88% cheaper, but it is detection and notification, not physical intervention. The market reality: hyperscalers (Microsoft documents "security officers … around-the-clock video surveillance by the operations center"), colo anchors (Iron Mountain: "trained security personnel on-site"), and compliance/insurance frameworks all demand documented 24/7 on-site human presence in addition to CCTV/SOC, so the reference campus needs both, not either/or.

Multi-tenant control. In wholesale deals the tenant leases a hall/suite and controls the inner rings (suite door, cage, rack); the operator controls perimeter→building. In retail colo the operator controls everything down to the cage/cabinet and the tenant just badges in under escort rules. Cages (mesh) are cheaper and faster; suites (hard-walled rooms) give the tenant its own four walls and are preferred by regulated tenants; shared halls put anti-passback and camera coverage to work between customers. Tenants negotiate audit rights to inspect logs and walk the space.

1.4 Delivery and risk

Who designs: a dedicated security consultant (not the electrical engineer) should own the security design and the threat/risk assessment; the electrical engineer only carries power/pathways for it. Who supplies/installs: a security integrator, usually a subcontractor to the design-builder, but the access-control platform and CCTV VMS are typically an owner/operator corporate standard (e.g., a single Lenel/Genetec/Verkada estate across the fleet) bought owner-direct so all sites share one head-end. Cost of failing the first tenant audit: an anchor tenant's SOC 2 / ISO 27001 / PCI pre-occupancy audit can gate move-in; a failed audit delays rent commencement and can trigger remediation at the operator's cost — the reason access logging and camera uptime must be complete and evidenced at handover, not "90% done."

2. Fire Detection and Suppression

2.1 Detection

Spot smoke detectors (ceiling/return-air) are the code baseline but respond late in high-airflow rooms because cooling airflow dilutes and carries smoke past them. The data-center standard is therefore aspirating smoke detection (ASD/VESDA-class) — a pipe network continuously samples air and detects combustion products at concentrations ~100× below conventional thresholds. Sensitivity is graded by EN 54-20 Class A/B/C (A highest, "before visible smoke"); VESDA-E is typically Class A. Placement follows the airflow: hot-aisle/return sampling, under raised floor, above ceiling, and in-cabinet sampling for the highest-value racks. Codes: NFPA 72 (fire alarm, transport-time rules for ASD), NFPA 75 (IT equipment; requires detection under floors and above ceilings), NFPA 76 (telecom; two-stage VEWFD sensitivity). FM Global DS 5-32 adds that on VEWFD pre-alarm the cooling air velocity is limited to ≤5 ft/sec (1.5 m/s) so detection and suppression can work. Nuisance-alarm causes are dust at construction turnover, humidity, and diesel/exhaust ingress; ASD's high sensitivity makes commissioning-era false alarms a real management issue, which is why alarm thresholds are set per-room against measured background particulate.

2.2 Suppression — the decision table

The layered baseline in the US is a pre-action sprinkler system in the white space (water held out of the pipe until both a detector and a sprinkler head operate), with gaseous or mist systems added where water contact is unacceptable. Acoustic risk to hard drives is a real design constraint: high-pressure inert-gas and clean-agent discharge noise can vibrate spinning disks and cause I/O errors or damage. FM DS 5-32 was updated (Section 2.4.7.3.7) to require nozzle placement based on the listed sound-pressure-level values to protect HDDs. Room-integrity ("door-fan") testing is mandatory for any gaseous system: NFPA 2001 requires that 85% of the adjusted minimum design concentration is held at the highest combustible level for ≥10 minutes; walls must run slab-to-deck or the agent leaks over the ceiling. Refill logistics differ sharply — halocarbon/FK refill is a bottled-agent recharge (and DOT 49 CFR §180.205 requires 5-year hydrostatic cylinder retest); inert gas is a large high-pressure cylinder bank; mist just needs water and pump service.

2.3 Battery rooms; liquid cooling meets fire

Lithium-ion UPS rooms are governed by NFPA 855 with UL 9540A thermal-runaway fire-test data as the key design input. Design drivers: off-gas detection (H₂/CO/HF sensors before ignition — UL FSRI identifies off-gas sensing as the early-warning method, though NFPA 855 sets no specific setpoints), deflagration venting (NFPA 68) and/or explosion prevention (NFPA 69), mechanical ventilation (NFPA 855 §4.9.3: ≥1 CFM/ft² continuous or intermittent triggered at 25% LFL), 3-ft spacing (reducible with UL 9540A data, though insurers often want more), and typically water-based suppression to cool. The most important 2026 change: NFPA 855's 2026 edition states standalone deflagration venting is no longer sufficient — systems must add NFPA 69-compliant explosion prevention. (Detailed battery kWh/ventilation spacing is a Topic-5 matter; here we only note the room drives its own suppression and detection.)

Liquid cooling meets fire — the unsettled frontier. NFPA 75's 2024 edition newly added requirements for ITE immersion cooling (§8.2.2): the insulating liquid must be noncombustible or have a closed-cup flash point ≥135 °C (275 °F), and tanks need a lid/closed piping and be listed/approved — but NFPA 75 does not mandate a suppression agent for immersion areas (NFPA 13 sprinklers still govern) and does not yet prescribe detailed direct-to-chip CDU/loop fire rules. The chemistry matters: single-phase synthetic-hydrocarbon dielectric fluids are combustible (NFPA 30 Class IIIB), and glycol and refrigerants in direct-to-chip loops "are flammable at high concentrations and temperatures" (FM's Bob Kasiski, Oct 2025); two-phase fluorinated fluids are non-flammable but carry PFAS/HF exposure. FM Global DS 5-32 is far more prescriptive than NFPA 75 on the loops: non-combustible welded stainless piping, braided-steel flex connections, piping arranged to isolate a leak to a single rack, and located low/at the room edge — and FM's blunt advice is "minimize glycol concentration … avoid oils entirely." Leak detection is a separate, parallel system from fire detection, wired to automatic coolant shut-off (target <60 s), and the sensor type must match the fluid (conductive cable for water-glycol; optical/vapor for non-conductive dielectric). The field is explicitly evolving: the NFPA 75 committee "will look into more guidance around liquid-to-chip cooling systems" (NFPA Journal, Feb 2026) and a dedicated panel at NFPA's 2025 conference convened to push those updates. What remains unsettled: suppression strategy for immersion tanks, whether/how sprinklers interact with a full tank, and code-level direct-to-chip loop rules.

2.4 Egress, life safety, and the FM-insured owner

Life safety follows the IBC (building) and IFC (fire): occupant load, travel-distance and two-remote-exits rules, emergency lighting, and exit signage. Data-center wrinkle: the EPO (emergency power-off) interface and NEC 645.10 interplay (covered in Topic 6) must not conflict with orderly egress. The AHJ and fire marshal hold acceptance authority; their sign-off (fire-alarm acceptance, sprinkler flush, door-fan witness) is a hard schedule gate (named here, proven in Topic 11). An FM Global-insured owner designs to FM DS 5-32 (Data Centers) and DS 5-33 (Electrical/related) on top of code — per FM's May 6, 2025 FM Intellium launch release, "Today the company protects approximately 1,100 data centers, representing US$250 billion of insurable value," adding that "FM's engineers now spend about 30,000 hours a year working with some of the world's leading data center providers" (reaffirmed in FM's Jan. 13, 2026 $5B-capacity announcement), and its recommendations "often become deal terms." FM adds things code does not: FM-Approved components only, VEWFD with airflow limits on pre-alarm, and (since Jan 2023) water mist approved for all DC areas including distributed Li-ion.

Delivery and risk. A fire protection engineer (FPE) of record stamps the design; sprinkler/mechanical and fire-alarm subcontractors install under the design-builder; the AHJ acceptance test is the schedule gate. Fire dominates data-center loss severity — per Allianz Commercial's 2026 report, based on "221 claims totaling approximately €677 million ($782 million)," fire "accounts for well over 50%" of loss severity though "water damage led at 21% of claims, followed by willful acts at 19% and fire" by volume — which is why detection is set so sensitive.

3. The Instrumentation Layer

3.1 Sensing and metering points, in order

Following the power and mechanical chains, a hall instruments (electrical) utility revenue meter → MV protective relays → transformer & generator monitoring → UPS input → UPS output → PDU branch circuits → busway tap-offs → rack-PDU outlets; and (mechanical) aisle temperature/humidity, under-floor or containment differential pressure, leak-detection cable, CDU flow & supply/return temperature, BTU meters, and the water meter(s). A 10 MW hall carries a large point count — indicatively several thousand monitored points once every rack-PDU outlet, branch circuit, CRAH/CDU and environmental sensor is counted (see §6.3).

Access control and the fire alarm panel sit as deliberate islands (life-safety and security must not depend on the BMS). Convergence claims vs reality: vendors sell a "single pane of glass," and newer unified platforms do normalise BACnet/Modbus/SNMP/Redfish/OPC UA into one data model — but in practice these are usually separate products from separate vendors installed by separate contractors, so an operator often "cannot correlate a cooling event with the electrical event that caused it" without a gateway or manual cross-reference. Protocol bridging (e.g. chiller Modbus→BACnet for the BMS, PDU SNMP→Modbus for the EPMS) is normal integration work. Time synchronisation (NTP/PTP to a common clock) is essential so the EPMS sequence-of-events and BMS logs order events correctly during a fault.

3.3 Alarms, dashboards, OT security

Alarm management follows ISA-18.2 / IEC 62682 (with EEMUA 191): a documented philosophy, rationalisation (every alarm actionable, with priority/cause/consequence/response), and flood control. Definitions worth knowing: an alarm flood is >10 alarms in any 10-minute window on one operator position; a healthy system averages <6 alarms/operator/hour, with a target priority mix ~80% low / 15% medium / 5% high (EEMUA 191). A single power dip fanning out into hundreds of alarms is the classic failure — the real fault gets buried. Escalation runs from on-site operators to a NOC. Capacity dashboards make power/cooling/space/network headroom — and stranded capacity (power available but unusable because cooling or space is exhausted, or vice-versa) — visible. Asset lifecycle/CMDB and change tickets live in DCIM.

OT security. The monitoring stack is an OT (operational-technology) estate and is governed by IEC 62443 (zones-and-conduits, security levels) and NIST SP 800-82 Rev.3 (ICS security, which in Rev.3 adopted the 62443 zone/conduit model). Core practices: segment OT from corporate IT with a DMZ, kill flat networks, broker remote vendor access through a jump host with MFA and session recording, passive asset inventory, and careful (often deferred) patching because availability dominates. The OOB network is the carrier for management/monitoring traffic and must itself be segmented and access-controlled. Documented OT incidents across critical infrastructure (nation-state, ransomware, and careless vendor remote access) are why vendor remote access is the most scrutinised conduit.

Delivery and risk. A master systems integrator (MSI) ties BMS/EPMS/DCIM together; someone must own the points list (the master register of every monitored point, its address, scaling and alarm setpoint) — this is the single most fought-over integration artifact. Monitoring is the system most often unfinished at handover because it depends on every other trade finishing first (you cannot map a point that isn't installed), the integration test is late in the schedule, and change-orders pile up as points are added or re-scaled. Ask: who owns the points list, when is the integration test, and what's the change-order history?

4. Efficiency Metrics — Where They Are Measured

4.1 PUE and its measurement categories

PUE = total facility energy ÷ IT energy, standardised by ISO/IEC 30134-2 (2016; 2026 revision just published, adding mixed-use-building and unaccounted-energy guidance) and originally by The Green Grid. The categories differ by where and how you measure:

  • Category 1 (PUE1): IT measured at UPS output — easiest, most common, most flattering.
  • Category 2 (PUE2): IT measured at PDU output — more accurate.
  • Category 3 (PUE3): IT measured at the rack/rack-PDU — most rigorous; only Cat-3 credibly supports sub-1.2 claims.
  • (A Category 0 instantaneous/estimate also exists.)

Proper PUE requires 12 coincident months of data (annualised), not a good-day instantaneous reading. Partial PUE (pPUE) isolates a subsystem or a pod (useful for a liquid-cooled AI pod). Crucially, most quoted PUE figures never state the category and are often design PUE (dPUE) — always ask "which category, measured or design, annualised or spot?"

How PUE curves with load — the Phase 1 problem. PUE is worst at low load because fixed overheads (transformer no-load losses, minimum fan/pump speeds, control power, lighting, UPS conversion losses that don't scale linearly) are spread over little IT. So a 40 MW plant running one 10 MW hall reads well above its 1.4 design point (see §6.3 arithmetic). Industry context: per the Uptime Institute Global Data Center Survey 2024, "the industry average PUE of 1.56… reveals a continuing trend of inertia" (flat for the fifth consecutive year); Uptime's Feb 7, 2024 analysis states "In 2023, industry average PUE stood at 1.58… the capacity-weighted PUE figure is 1.47" — down from ~2.5 in 2007, 1.98 in 2011, 1.65 in 2014, 1.58 in 2018 — while hyperscaler fleets report ~1.1–1.2. That capacity-weighted 1.47 sitting below the 1.58 per-site average confirms big new sites are more efficient than the aging long tail.

4.2 WUE, CUE, ERE, and the "PUE is done" debate

  • WUE (ISO/IEC 30134-9:2022) = water ÷ IT energy in L/kWh. Site WUE (Category 1) counts water at the facility meter; source WUE adds the water used off-site to generate the electricity. Most operators (incl. Equinix) report the simple WUE1 from a single incoming water meter. WUE ranges from 0 L/kWh (fully air-cooled) up to ~2.5 L/kWh (evaporative). The key tension for this campus: near-zero-water heat rejection drives site WUE toward ~0, but the trade-off is usually more electricity for mechanical cooling in a hot climate, which pushes PUE up — so the OPR's simultaneous PUE ≤ 1.4 and WUE ≤ 0.2 is a genuine engineering squeeze, not two independent targets.
  • CUE (30134-8) = carbon ÷ IT energy (location- vs market-based factors).
  • ERE / ERF (30134-6) = credit for reused heat; ERF is a headline metric in the EU scheme.
  • "PUE is done" debate: with PUE stuck near 1.56 industry-wide and AI raising IT power itself, critics argue PUE no longer captures the useful-work question and push for water, carbon and compute-productivity metrics; defenders keep it as the one comparable, decade-long baseline. Both are right — PUE stays as the common yardstick, but it's now one of several.

4.3 Measurement pitfalls and efficiency levers

Pitfalls / how PUE is gamed: using Category 1 and calling it 1.1; instantaneous cool-night readings; excluding losses (shared chilled-water plant, OOB, security/office loads); and the server-fan-power problem — server fans are counted inside the IT number, so a design that pushes heat onto server fans can flatter PUE while wasting energy. Meter accuracy classes matter: to prove PUE ≤ 1.4 and WUE ≤ 0.2 to an auditor you need revenue-grade (≈Class 0.5 or better) meters at the fence and at UPS/PDU output, plus a calibrated water meter, logged over 12 months.

4.4 Regulation and who guarantees PUE

  • EU Energy Efficiency Directive 2023/1791, Article 12 + Delegated Regulation (EU) 2024/1364 (in force 6 June 2024): every EU data center with ≥500 kW installed IT must report to the European Database on Data Centres — 24 data points yielding PUE, WUE, ERF and REF, first report due 15 Sept 2024, then annually by 15 May (2025 data due 15 May 2026). A proposed A–G energy-rating label is expected ~2027. (US-sited, our campus isn't bound, but its EU-owned tenants and lenders increasingly demand the same data.)
  • EN 50600-4 series defines the KPI methods (PUE per EN 50600-4-2 etc.); Germany's EnEfG sets PUE limits (≤1.5 for new centers from July 2026, ≤1.3 from 2030) — a preview of where regulation is heading.
  • ASHRAE 90.4 is the design-side check in the US: it does not use PUE for compliance but requires calculating a Mechanical Load Component (MLC) and Electrical Loss Component (ELC) against 19 climate-zone maxima (90.4-2025 also brings water into scope). A design can hit a PUE target yet fail MLC if the cooling plant carries too much overhead for its zone.
  • Who guarantees design PUE, and who pays when Phase 1 misses: under a performance spec the design-builder (or the commissioning/OEM) warrants a PUE figure, but the fight is when and how it's measured. Measured at acceptance on a lightly-loaded Phase 1, PUE will read ~1.55–1.65 and "fail"; measured after a year at design load it can hit 1.4. Best practice is to write the guarantee as a curve or a loaded-condition test (PUE at ≥X% load) or defer to a 12-month annualised measurement, so neither party is punished for the physics of ramp. If the contract naively says "1.4 at acceptance," the builder eats liquidated damages for a plant that is actually fine.

5. Delivery and Risk Across the Four Subjects

5.1 Scope split under design-build with OFE

  • In the design-builder's scope: fire detection/suppression (FPE + sprinkler/alarm subs), BMS and EPMS (MSI + controls subs), building security infrastructure (pathways, door hardware, the integrator's field devices).
  • Owner-direct / corporate standard: DCIM and the access-control/CCTV platform are almost always operator corporate standards bought owner-direct (one head-end across the fleet); often the UPS/EPMS head-end and sometimes the generator-paralleling SCADA are OFE too.
  • Specialty subs & interfaces: FPE-of-record, sprinkler/mist, clean-agent/inert-gas, fire-alarm, security integrator, controls/MSI, meter vendor. The interfaces between them are the risk — e.g. fire alarm must signal the BMS to shut dampers and the EPMS/EPO, and the access system must release doors on alarm.
  • Cost as a share of MEP: security + fire + monitoring are individually small but collectively meaningful; monitoring/controls especially is under-scoped. (Indicative campus share in §6.3.)

5.2 Integration risk and what auditors inspect

The BMS–EPMS–DCIM integration is where schedule and budget leak: a disputed points list, a late integration test, and a change-order pattern as points are discovered missing or mis-scaled. Life-safety commissioning (fire-alarm acceptance, door-fan test, AHJ witness) is named here and proven in Topic 11. What the auditors actually ask to see in the installed systems: - SOC 2 / ISO 27001: access logs (complete, time-synced), camera coverage and camera uptime records, visitor logs, alarm/event records, evidence of review. - PCI DSS Req. 9: the ≥90-day retained record (video or access logs) for sensitive areas, badge issuance/revocation, escort logs. - All three care that the systems were actually operating and evidenced, not merely installed — the single biggest gap at a first audit.

6.3 The metering tree that produces PUE (indicative, with arithmetic)

Electrical branch (for PUE): Utility revenue meter (fence) → MV switchgear + relays → substation transformers → generator & UPS input meters → UPS output meters → PDU meters → busway tap-offs → rack-PDU outlet meters. Mechanical branch: chiller/pump/CRAH/CDU sub-meters + BTU meters feeding the "total facility" side. Water branch: one incoming utility water meter = WUE Category 1 (plus optional make-up sub-meters for a finer figure).

  • PUE category supported: metering at UPS output = Category 1; adding PDU-output meters = Category 2; rack-PDU outlet metering (present here for tenant billing) = Category 3. So this hall can credibly report Cat-2, arguably Cat-3.
  • Indicative point counts (Hall 1, ~10 MW): utility+MV+transformers ≈ 30–60; generators/UPS in/out ≈ 100–200; PDUs + branch circuits ≈ 400–800; rack-PDU outlets across ~250 racks ≈ 1,000–2,000; mechanical (CRAH/CDU/BTU/dP) ≈ 300–600; environmental (temp/humidity/leak) ≈ 500–1,000. Total ≈ 2,500–4,500 points for one hall — which is why the points list is contested.
  • WUE meters: 1 primary incoming water meter (WUE1); near-zero-water design means make-up water is tiny, so site WUE ≈ 0.0–0.05 L/kWh indicative, comfortably under the 0.2 OPR ceiling.

Phase 1 part-load PUE — worked arithmetic (all indicative): Take Phase 1 = 10 MW IT in Hall 1. Overhead splits into a fixed part (transformer no-load, control power, minimum fan/pump/chiller speeds, UPS conversion floor, lighting) and a load-proportional part (chiller lift, fan/pump work rising with heat). - At full 40 MW design: total facility ≈ 56 MW ⇒ overhead ≈ 16 MW ⇒ PUE = 56/40 = 1.40. Split that 16 MW as ~6 MW fixed + 10 MW proportional (proportional = 0.25 × IT). - At Phase 1 10 MW IT: proportional overhead ≈ 0.25 × 10 = 2.5 MW; fixed overhead doesn't shrink much — but only part of the plant is energised, so assume ~40% of fixed is online ≈ 2.4 MW. Total overhead ≈ 2.5 + 2.4 = 4.9 MW. - Phase 1 PUE ≈ (10 + 4.9) / 10 = 1.49 if you can idle most fixed plant; if the full 6 MW fixed plant must run (common when shared chilled-water and switchgear energise campus-wide), overhead ≈ 2.5 + 6 = 8.5 MW ⇒ PUE ≈ (10 + 8.5)/10 = 1.85. Realistic Phase-1 answer sits between, ~1.55–1.65 — i.e. it misses 1.4, entirely from part-load physics, not bad design. This is the number to raise with any builder guaranteeing "1.4 at acceptance."

Indicative capex for the three systems, against the $400–500M ex-IT campus: - Physical security (barriers, fencing, PIDS, access control, CCTV, mantraps, guardhouse): ~$8–20M (≈1.5–4% of ex-IT capex). - Fire detection & suppression (ASD, pre-action, inert-gas/mist, battery-room protection, alarm): ~$10–25M. - Monitoring/controls (BMS, EPMS, DCIM, MSI integration, metering): ~$10–25M. - Combined ≈ $30–70M, i.e. ~3–6% of ex-IT capex — small next to power and cooling, but the part most likely to be unfinished at handover and to gate a tenant audit. All figures indicative.

Open Questions & What's Changing Fast in 2026

  1. Clean-agent phase-outs: 3M's FK-5-1-12/Novec exit (end-2025) and the AIM Act HFC phase-down (to 2036) are pushing new-build toward inert gas and water mist; PFAS regulation may eventually catch generic FK-5-1-12 too.
  2. Lithium-ion room protection: NFPA 855 (2026) now requires explosion prevention, not just venting; UL 9540A data is the currency with AHJs and insurers, and insurer spacing demands often exceed code.
  3. Liquid-cooling fire guidance: the genuinely unsettled frontier — NFPA 75-2024 covers immersion tanks but not direct-to-chip loops; suppression strategy for a flooded tank, sprinkler interaction, and dielectric-fluid HF/PFAS behaviour are open; the NFPA 75 committee is actively working it.
  4. EU reporting & ratings: EED Article 12 reporting is live; an A–G efficiency label is expected ~2027; Germany's EnEfG PUE limits (≤1.5 from 2026, ≤1.3 from 2030) preview binding thresholds.
  5. The future of PUE: stuck at ~1.56 industry-wide and blind to useful work, PUE is being supplemented (not replaced) by WUE, CUE and compute-productivity metrics; ISO/IEC 30134-2:2026 refreshes the definition.
  6. AI-driven operations: vendors push AI/ML for cooling optimisation and predictive alarms, but per Uptime Institute's 2024 Global Survey "42% of operators said they would not trust an adequately trained AI system to make operational decisions… up 18 percentage points since 2022" (24% said no in 2022 vs 42% in 2024) — i.e., positive trust fell from 76% (2021) to 58% (2024): promising, not yet trusted for autonomous control.

Glossary

  • Anti-passback: access rule preventing one credential being used for a second entry without a matching exit.
  • ASD / VESDA: aspirating smoke detection; pipes sample air for very-early smoke, Class A/B/C per EN 54-20.
  • BMS/BAS: building management system; runs HVAC/cooling/lighting, speaks BACnet.
  • CDU: coolant distribution unit; the heat-exchange/pumping skid for liquid-cooled racks.
  • CUE / ERE / ERF / REF / WUE: carbon, energy-reuse, energy-reuse-factor, renewable-energy-factor and water KPIs in the ISO/IEC 30134 series.
  • DCIM: data center infrastructure management; assets, capacity, tenants; SNMP/Redfish.
  • Door-fan (room-integrity) test: pressurisation test proving a gas-suppression room holds agent ≥10 min.
  • EPMS: electrical power monitoring system; power chain, waveform capture, sequence-of-events; Modbus.
  • Eco-mode: high-efficiency UPS mode (98–99%) that bypasses double-conversion, at a lower protection class.
  • K-/M-rating (ASTM F2656): crash rating of a vehicle barrier (truck weight at 30/40/50 mph), with P penetration suffix.
  • Mantrap / portal: interlocked two-door entry that admits one verified person at a time.
  • MLC / ELC (ASHRAE 90.4): mechanical-load and electrical-loss components used for design-code compliance instead of PUE.
  • OT / IEC 62443 / NIST SP 800-82: operational-technology and its control-system cybersecurity standards (zones/conduits).
  • Points list: master register of every monitored point (address, scaling, alarm setpoint) — the key integration artifact.
  • Pre-action sprinkler: dry sprinkler charged with water only when a detector (and, in double-interlock, a head) operates.
  • pPUE: partial PUE for one subsystem or pod.
  • PUE (ISO/IEC 30134-2): total facility energy ÷ IT energy; Categories 0–3 by measurement point.
  • Rationalisation (ISA-18.2): disciplining alarms so each is actionable; flood = >10 alarms/10 min.
  • UL 9540A: thermal-runaway fire-test method whose data drives lithium-ion room design.

What Surprised the Researcher

  1. The "PCI requires 90 days of video" rule is a myth — PCI DSS sets no camera-retention period at all; the 90 days is borrowed from a log-retention line and can be met with access logs alone. A huge amount of the industry cites it as gospel.
  2. FM Global's flat advice on liquid cooling — "minimize glycol, avoid oils entirely" — is bolder than the codes, and FM's deal terms often bind owners more tightly than NFPA does.
  3. PUE gets worse at low load by physics, so a brand-new, well-designed plant will "fail" a naive 1.4-at-acceptance clause purely because Phase 1 runs a quarter-loaded — a contract trap, not an engineering one.
  4. Monitoring is routinely the least-finished system at handover, precisely because it depends on every other trade completing first — the opposite of intuition that "software is quick."
  5. NFPA 75 only added immersion-cooling rules in 2024 and still has nothing prescriptive for direct-to-chip loops, so the fastest-growing cooling method in the AI era is being built ahead of its fire code.
  6. Eco-mode's efficiency gain (to 98–99%) comes with a drop to the lowest IEC 62040-3 protection class — a reminder that nearly every efficiency lever trades against resilience, which is why operators argue about them.
