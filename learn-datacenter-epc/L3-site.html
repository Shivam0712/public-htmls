From a candidate site to an energized campus

Deep detail and sources: the appendix for this topic. Short version: sixty words. The research report this chapter was distilled from. Earlier version: archived 2026-09-26.

Our example is a 1,000-rack colocation campus near a hot United States metro: it rents powered, cooled space to tenants in large wholesale blocks and smaller retail leases. Its rack mix draws 38.9 megawatts (MW), or 38,900 kilowatts (kW), of information technology (IT) power at full fit-out, rounded to 40 MW. A design power usage effectiveness (PUE) of 1.4, total facility energy divided by IT energy, implies about 56 MW at the utility connection. The owner builds four roughly 10 MW IT phases in a Tier III-class design, so planned maintenance of key power and cooling equipment need not stop computing. We follow what must be proved before any hall can use power.

Translate racks into a dated power request

A utility needs a load forecast with dates, not a rack count. At the design PUE, the first roughly 10 MW IT phase needs about 14 MW at the utility connection; four such phases reach about 56 MW of planned demand. The full-site service contract is planned for roughly 60 MW. The first delivery date and the promised dates for later increases are separate questions.

Takeaway: A live first hall does not entitle the owner to the remaining 42 MW of planned demand. Conversely, reserving roughly 60 MW of full-site contract capacity immediately can incur charges before later tenants arrive. Electricity is about $34 million of the campus's $65 million annual operating cost, so the price of the power ramp matters alongside its date.

The rack and budget arithmetic
  • Racks: 300 × 8 kW = 2.4 MW; 300 × 15 kW = 4.5 MW; 200 × 40 kW = 8 MW; 200 × 120 kW = 24 MW. Total: 38.9 MW, rounded to 40 MW.
  • The first roughly 10 MW IT phase costs $180–230 million because it includes shared site and shell work for later halls. Full-campus capital cost is $400–500 million excluding IT equipment.
  • PUE is a design ratio, not a guarantee at every hour. A utility service agreement must specify when each increment can actually be drawn (APX-site §12).

Screen the parcel before buying it

Suppose the owner takes a land option, a paid temporary right to buy, on about 120 contiguous acres near Phoenix beside a 138-kilovolt (kV) transmission line. Being close to the line does not mean it has spare power. The campus also needs two physically separate fiber-optic data paths, a credible reclaimed-water supply (treated water for non-drinking uses) or a way to release heat to air with little water, sewer capacity and buildable land outside the 500-year floodplain, a zone with about a 0.2% annual flood chance. Homes beyond the fence bring noise and setbacks into the decision.

Takeaway: The map shows possible connections, not granted ones. Industrial zoning may still require permission for a data center, and two fiber entries are useless if their routes share one buried conduit. The land option gives the owner time to check grid capacity, water, fiber, hazards and neighbours before deciding whether to close (APX-site §1).

Why power comes first without making fiber optional
  • A utility must study the nearby line before it can promise capacity or dates. Check the entire route of each fiber path, not just its entry point.
  • The parcel must also fit cooling, substation equipment, setbacks and future phases; raw acreage is not enough.
  • Best-case powered land may have electricity in 18–24 months, while constrained markets can take more than four years. These describe different starting points, not conflicting promises.

Follow a request through the utility

A data center is a load: it consumes electricity rather than exporting it like a power plant. The local utility usually processes its request under state rules, with a regional grid operator involved where wider transmission is affected. In a system impact study, engineers test whether the new demand would overload equipment or upset voltage; required reinforcements elsewhere on the grid are network upgrades. Energizing means applying utility power to the tested connection, not opening the hall to tenants.

Takeaway: The owner can apply while land is still under option. A queue position is only a place in the study line, not a reservation of roughly 60 MW of full-site contract capacity. An agreement sets scope and conditional dates, but the utility's work, the owner's work and final tests still have to finish (APX-site §2 and APX-site §3).

What a study date means
  • Load connections have usually followed utility and state procedures; generator connections have different federal rules. Federal treatment of large loads was changing in 2025–26, so verify the current local process.
  • Initial studies and later construction of network upgrades can take years. A target date should state what equipment and upstream utility work it assumes.

Decide who builds and owns the substation

A substation turns incoming high-voltage electricity into power the halls can use. For this candidate, we propose a customer-owned 138 kV substation: transformers lower the voltage and switchgear protects and routes circuits to the halls. The two depicted utility lines are a requested arrangement, not proof of two independent, approved supplies.

Takeaway: The drawn fence helps explain the split, but the contract defines the actual service boundary. The owner buys its transformers and may pay a contribution in aid of construction (CIAC), a charge for utility-owned taps, metering or lines built for its load. If the utility owns the substation instead, it takes the equipment order and may set the build date through its own queue. Neither choice creates spare grid capacity by itself (APX-site §4).

Questions to settle at the boundary
  • A dedicated substation or transmission tap can produce utility construction charges in the millions to tens of millions; this is not a quote for the parcel.
  • Verify where ownership changes, who maintains and tests the gear, and whether two lines share the same upstream vulnerability.
  • The first hall must already meet its concurrently maintainable design when it opens; empty pads for later halls are not redundant equipment.

Price the right to reserve power

A utility queue counts requests, not megawatts it has promised to deliver. A large-load tariff sets the contract and billing rules for big electricity users, including minimum bills and collateral, money or a bank guarantee pledged if a customer walks away. For a Phoenix candidate, the serving utility might be Salt River Project (SRP); another local utility would apply different rules. The examples show why an unphased full-site forecast of roughly 60 MW can become expensive.

Takeaway: The first hall needs about 14 MW of delivered power, not the roughly 60 MW of full-site contract capacity. Under an unphased SRP-style forecast, the minimum demand basis could be 48 MW while most halls remain empty. A staged bill is not a free guarantee of later capacity: both future delivery and payment obligations must be written down. Posted collateral is a financing exposure, not a substation purchase (APX-site §5).

Why queue totals mislead
  • In an SRP cluster, combined requests fell from about 7,200 MW to 1,000 MW as applicants faced deposits for 30% of their assigned network-upgrade costs; a restudy also changed the upgrade estimate. A request is not bankable capacity.
  • Dominion's cited class applies to qualifying customers taking service after January 1, 2027. Texas's Senate Bill 6 has a separate 75 MW size trigger; neither is a Phoenix tariff.
  • Rates and study rules are dated 2025–26 snapshots. Confirm the actual tariff and a written phase-by-phase capacity ramp before relying on either number.

Make water a design and permit decision

In a hot metro, releasing computing heat into outside air can use more electricity; evaporating water can reduce that electric load. Cooling liquid at the chip does not answer which outdoor heat-rejection method the facility uses. A withdrawal is water taken from a source, while evaporation is consumptive use because it is not returned locally. Blowdown is the mineral-rich water drained from a tower and needs somewhere permitted to go.

Takeaway: Water usage effectiveness (WUE) reports on-site water use in liters (L) per kilowatt-hour (kWh) of IT energy. A kilowatt-hour measures energy used over time, not peak power. These reported ranges are not operating results for our Phoenix candidate.

Takeaway: For this teaching site, the owner sets a design target of at most 0.2 L/kWh and investigates reclaimed water and dry cooling. That target is not a water allocation; hotter, drier cooling must also be checked against the design PUE. Water rights, peak-day supply, sewer capacity and discharge approval belong in land diligence (APX-site §7).

Converting the target into a local question
  • At a constant 40 MW IT load for one day, 0.2 L/kWh would mean 192,000 liters, roughly 51,000 United States gallons of on-site water use. A peak-day supply agreement is a separate question.
  • Western water rights can be curtailed by seniority in a shortage. Reclaimed supply must be allocated, and tower blowdown needs available sewer capacity or a surface-water discharge permit with chemistry and temperature limits.
  • Published WUE results use different facilities and reporting boundaries. Do not treat a reported portfolio value or an unverified industry average as a measured Phoenix outcome.

Earn permission to build

Industrial zoning is not a data-center permit. A conditional-use permit is a discretionary local approval, often decided after residents speak at a public hearing. The pictured homes and floodplain make water, noise, drainage and electricity-rate impacts part of the application. Standby engines, and any gas bridge, also need an air permit controlling their emissions.

Takeaway: A hearing can still deny a project on land labeled industrial. The owner should offer specific water and noise commitments, and explain who funds grid work, before filing. Sales-tax or property-tax relief may break a tie between buildable sites, but it cannot grant water, utility capacity or permission to build. Local approval and incentive checks run alongside grid studies (APX-site §8 and APX-site §9).

What cannot be carried from another city
  • A tax exemption can be worth millions yet depend on location, qualifying investment and jobs; a Virginia or Texas benefit is not an Arizona entitlement.
  • Data Center Watch's June 2026 cumulative tally exceeded $130 billion in United States projects blocked or delayed by opposition. That is project value, not the probability this parcel fails.
  • Air reviews for emergency generators or a bridge can proceed in parallel with land-use reviews, but engines cannot operate without the required authorization.

Buy the equipment before the design is finished

The owner orders electrical gear before every permit is final. A purchase order is not necessarily the factory start: release to manufacture (RTM), the factory's actual go-ahead, can lag by months. Wood Mackenzie's second-quarter 2025 survey found around 128 weeks for a large power transformer; a 10–30% deposit may hold a factory slot before design is complete. An uninterruptible power supply (UPS) carries computing loads across brief power interruptions.

Takeaway: The transformer outlasts an 18-month hall build, so this owner buys scarce gear directly. Its design-build contractor designs and builds the halls, then installs that owner-furnished equipment (OFE). If the contractor buys and installs it, the gear is contractor-furnished equipment (CFE).

A factory slot creates an owner-side clock

If the owner-bought transformer is late, the contractor cannot energize a hall by working faster. Set manufacturing milestones, factory tests, storage, insurance, warranties and schedule relief in the contract before promising a tenant date (APX-site §10 and APX-site §11).

What an early order must specify
  • Buyers may reserve after the main electrical one-line drawing, a map of major equipment and connections, is about 60% developed. Agree the equipment ratings and allowed design changes before paying a deposit.
  • Track the actual release-to-manufacture date, not only the purchase order. Qualify alternative suppliers and decide who stores equipment that reaches the site before its pad is ready.
  • Quoted 2025–26 lead times are planning benchmarks; generator size and supplier allocation can change delivery.

Treat a gas bridge as another project

If utility service slips, could Hall 1 run on its own gas engines? This is bridging power, often called behind-the-meter generation because it serves the customer's load rather than buying that energy through the utility meter. The engines would need a firm gas supply and an air permit, neither of which comes with the land. A battery energy storage system (BESS) could cover short power transfers; it cannot supply the hall for months.

Takeaway: The live hall is a hypothetical alternative, not part of the campus's committed build. A gas bridge can take 18–30 months itself, while air review, gas service and engine supply can delay it further. Price and permit it early if needed; it is not an instant rescue when a grid date slips (APX-site §6).

What a bridge must clear
  • Indicative gas-generation installation estimates run about $0.7–1.4 million per MW of generator capacity, before site-specific gas-supply and air-permit complications. Sizing must cover facility demand and required reliability, not just 10 MW of IT load.
  • A battery handles short transfers, not months of running a data center. Engine and gas infrastructure supply chains can also be long-lead.
