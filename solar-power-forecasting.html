The basics

What is solar power forecasting?

It's the process of predicting a photovoltaic (PV) system's future electricity generation. Forecasts also guide how a battery charges, matching expected solar output against household demand so less clean power goes to waste.

Predict generation

Estimate how many kilowatt-hours the panels will produce across a chosen time horizon.

Optimize storage

Adjust battery capacity ahead of time so surplus solar is stored, not curtailed.

The stakes

Why forecasting matters

Pairing PV with battery storage only pays off if you know what's coming. Accurate forecasts are the backbone of intelligent energy management, especially where prices move with the clock.

Smarter energy use

Combining solar with storage enables the most efficient, intelligent use of every ray.

Time-of-Use pricing

Precise PV forecasts are crucial for dynamic pricing and Time-of-Use optimization.

Reliable systems

Good predictions raise the overall reliability and efficiency of the whole energy system.

Methods

Three ways to forecast the sun

Every technique traces back to one of three foundations. Often the best results come from blending them. Tap a method to learn more.

Physical methods rely on numerical weather prediction (NWP) models, sky imaging and satellite imaging. By simulating how the atmosphere, sunlight and cloud cover behave, they estimate PV output from the real physical characteristics of the environment.

Statistical methods use techniques like persistence forecasting, adjusting NWP predictions with historical and real-time generation data. Persistence assumes current output will hold in the very near future, a simple baseline for judging more sophisticated models against.

This is where a forecast-based EMS comes in. It fuses real-time rooftop PV production, battery status and load consumption with external data like electricity prices and weather forecasts, then makes intelligent decisions about charging and grid interaction to continuously optimize energy flows.

The hard part

Why it's difficult

Solar depends on the weather, and the weather doesn't hold still. Four challenges make accurate forecasting genuinely hard.

Intermittent by nature

Cloud cover, shifting sunlight and unexpected weather make output constantly fluctuate.

Uncertain weather data

Errors in predicting cloud cover, rain or wind ripple straight into unreliable solar forecasts.

Solar curtailment

Excess power the grid can't absorb is wasted; good forecasts keep curtailment to a minimum.

Spatial variability

Irradiance differs by region; shading and terrain demand high-resolution, local models.
