Slide 03 · The one idea that runs through everything

A share price has two moving parts

Price = earnings × the multiple people pay for them. A 60% fall can come from either. They mean opposite things.

Why it decides the whole deck

If earnings did the damage, you are buying a business that got worse, and you need it to get better. That is a turnaround, and it can take years or never arrive — the classic value trap.

If the multiple did the damage while earnings held, you are buying the same business at a lower price. You need sentiment to normalise, not the business to be rebuilt.

So the first question for each name is not how far has it fallen. It is which part fell.

Which part fell

Read it this way

Lululemon, Deckers and On fell mostly because investors stopped paying up. On's earnings actually rose through its drawdown — every bit of the fall is de-rating.

Nike is the outlier: roughly half the damage is real earnings loss, from China and direct-to-consumer margin. EPS went from about $3.90 in FY24 to $2.10–2.20 on FY26 estimates.

adidas sits in between — an old earnings shock (Yeezy, China) plus compression from a very rich starting multiple.

The stock is roughly 70–75% below its 2021 peak. That sounds like deep value. It isn't: earnings fell with it, so the multiple never really got low.

  • Forward P/E of 23–25× against a 3–5 year average near 29–31× — a modest discount, not a dislocation.
  • EV/EBITDA around 13–14×, still a premium to the market.
  • North America revenue has slipped from above $21bn to about $20.5bn, with EBIT falling faster.
  • NIKE Direct is shrinking — double-digit declines in digital — while the rebuild of wholesale is slow and dilutes margin.

The trap: you pay a premium multiple for a business whose returns may be structurally lower. Buy only below about 18× forward, with China stabilising.

Slide 06 · The top idea

Lululemon fell just as far — and got genuinely cheap

Down roughly 70–75% from its 2023–25 peaks, but earnings held in the low teens and EBITDA is still $2.5–2.7bn. Almost the entire fall is de-rating.

10×Forward P/E — half the S&P 500 multiple
5–6×EV/EBITDA, against a 10-year median near 19×

At this price the market is implying roughly zero growth and some margin loss — against a business running mid-teens EBIT margins that is still opening stores in China.

Revenue by region, FY22 → FY25

China Mainland has nearly tripled and Rest of World has more than doubled. Revenue outside North America now clears $2.6bn. The fear is a growth scare; the evidence is not yet a structural break.

Slide 08 · Section 1 — Conclusion

Who is actually cheap

Only one name in the group trades at value multiples on quality earnings. Two trade at fair-to-good prices for genuine growth. One is a market-multiple turnaround. One is still a premium stock with premium problems.

Slide 09 · Section 2 — Regional demand

China stopped being a Western duopoly

Chinese sportswear market share

Domestic brands, combined, moved from under 30% in 2015 to roughly half the market by 2025.

This is the one part of the picture that reads as structural rather than cyclical — and it lands hardest on the two names ranked last.

  • Anta alone holds 21–23% share; Li-Ning is near 9–10%.
  • Nike has slipped to the mid-teens; adidas to roughly 8–9%.
  • National-pride purchasing and local product cycles are reinforcing the shift, not reversing it.

Why it matters for the ranking: Nike and adidas need China to recover to justify their multiples. Lululemon and Deckers do not — for Lululemon, China is still a growth engine rather than a hole to fill.

Slide 10 · Section 2 — Regional demand

Where demand is actually growing

The three names with intact growth are growing in different places — which is part of why they de-rated together but should not stay correlated.

On — from niche to global

Roughly half Americas, a quarter EMEA, a quarter Asia-Pacific. Wholesale still leads, but DTC is growing faster and gross margin is strong.

Deckers — Hoka overtakes UGG

US $3.19bn versus international $2.28bn. DTC is now above $2.2bn with over $800m of segment EBIT — the high-margin end of the business.

Lululemon — outside North America

China Mainland comps are still growing double digits while global athleisure growth moderates to mid single digits. Company-operated stores and DTC carry most of the EBIT.

Slide 11 · Section 2 — Quality of revenue

Sold at full price, or sold on markdown?

Two companies can report the same revenue line and mean completely different things. Discount depth is the tell.

Pricing power intact

  • Lululemon — premium pricing held, shallow discounting, price rises taken without obvious volume loss.
  • Hoka (Deckers) — tight inventory, low discount depth, strong full-price sell-through.
  • On — premium running product clearing at full price with limited markdown.

Revenue leaning on promotion

  • adidas — the post-Yeezy clean-up ran through promotional and outlet channels.
  • Nike — digital and outlet discounting has been doing real work to clear elevated inventory.
  • UGG — seasonal and more discount-sensitive, though still a profitable cash cow.

This is also the early-warning system. If Lululemon or Hoka start discounting hard, the thesis is breaking before the revenue line shows it.

Slide 12 · Section 3 — Tariffs

What it costs to land a shoe in the US

Duties now come in layers. Pick a route to see the stack as of mid-August 2026.

The moving part

The 10% Section 122 surcharge, imposed 24 February 2026, is statutorily temporary — up to 150 days, so a sunset around 24 July 2026. USTR has proposed replacing it with 10–12.5% Section 301 duties across a wide set of countries including Vietnam, India, Thailand and China.

Translation: the number in the box is a snapshot of a stack that is still being rebuilt. Most of the current level is already in guidance; the unpriced risk is further escalation.

Base MFN duty on footwear averages 11–12.5%, ranging from near zero to about 48% by category; apparel base is around 16.5%.

Slide 13 · Section 3 — Supply chain

Who is standing in the blast radius

Sourcing by country

Nike: share of footwear production, FY26. adidas and Puma: share of total volume. Puma is shown as a comparator for how concentrated this industry can get.

Nike and adidas both source well over half their volume from Vietnam and Indonesia, and mid-teens percentages from China. Neither owns a factory — essentially all production is outsourced.

Lower exposure

On — concentrated in Vietnam and China too, but far smaller absolute scale and more flexibility to move.

Deckers — Asian manufacturing, but diversified across origins and two very different brands.

Lululemon — mainly apparel, sourced across North America and Asia; exposed to apparel duties rather than China-specific footwear surcharges.

Slide 14 · Section 3 — What it does to earnings

The margin math, and the three ways out

30–50 bpsGross margin hit for Nike or adidas from each 1 point rise in the blended tariff on Asian imports

At their operating leverage that compounds into a low- to mid-single-digit percentage hit to EPS before any mitigation. The effect scales with how much of cost of goods comes from the taxed origin — which is why the sourcing table on the previous slide is the real risk map.

Mitigation levers, ranked by who can actually pull them

1 · Raise prices. Works where the brand has power: Lululemon, Hoka, On, premium adidas lines. Nike's recent price actions have met mixed elasticity, especially in China and lower-tier product.

2 · Move production and share cost with vendors. Shift out of China to Vietnam, Indonesia, India. Nike and adidas have already done a decade of this — the easy moves are behind them.

3 · Shift the mix. More DTC, more premium, more non-US revenue. Deckers and Lululemon are positioned for this. Nike's DTC margin has compressed, which closes the lever just when it is needed.

Slide 20 · Section 7 — Before you buy anything

Write the exit before the entry

Kill criteria are the events that end a position regardless of price. Decide them now, while you have no money on the line.

  • LULU — several quarters of negative comps in core markets, margins sustained below the low teens, or visible loss of premium positioning through heavy discounting.
  • DECK — Hoka growth drops to low single digits and stays there, or UGG declines structurally with nothing offsetting it.
  • ONON — growth falls below high single digits for several quarters, or margins collapse into discounting: brand heat fading.
  • ADDYY — China recovery stalls and margins miss targets, or the terrace cycle ends without a replacement product hit.
  • NKE — further China share loss plus sustained margin decline with no credible turnaround. Exit even at a loss.

Full exit when valuation is rich and the catalysts have played out — or the moment a kill criterion trips, whichever comes first.
